# England: The Door Opens Inward

There is a pot in Manchester. It was made in Nigeria, a museum in that city holds it, and a poet
from that city wrote a whole poem talking to it — not about it, to it. The poem was commissioned
for the museum and written as a conversation with a Nigerian pot in the collection.

Since 2023 that poem has been available to English classrooms as a set text, and in summer 2025
the first cohort sat an exam that could ask them about it — closed book, nothing on the desk but
what the paper supplies. The pot is the only thing in this story that crossed a border.

All seventy-three rows in this table are domestic. Not one arrived in translation. Measured
through this document, the window England opens on world literature is shut — and yet the room is
full of elsewhere.

## The door

The document is the AQA GCSE English Literature specification, and AQA is an awarding body, not a
ministry. The assessment objectives are set by Ofqual and are identical across every English
Literature specification and every exam board; what each board writes for itself is the menu of
books. So nothing below is England's shelf. It is one shelf in England, in one year. England is
my word, not the file's — the corpus is coded for the United Kingdom, and nothing in it covers
Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland.

And it is a menu, not a mandate. A student takes one play from a list of six, one nineteenth-
century novel from a list of seven, one modern text from a choice of twelve, and one poetry
cluster, whose fifteen poems must all be studied because the exam may ask about any of them. Six,
seven, twelve, forty-five: the 2025 menu runs to seventy items. The table carries seventy-three,
because it also keeps the three modern texts whose last exam was 2024 — a list caught mid-change.
Seventy rows are the space of the possible; the set-text list a real teenager carries is eighteen
of them, fifteen of those poems. Eighteen rows, not eighteen works: one of them, *Telling Tales*,
is an anthology of seven stories.

Two more sentences shape the door. All assessments are closed book, with any stimulus material
supplied by the paper itself. And the aim the specification writes for itself is that students
should come to appreciate the depth and power of the English literary heritage. Hold that aim
beside a table with no translated row and the shelf's Englishness looks less like an omission
than like the subject's own definition — my framing, not a reason the board gives. What the
document proves is the measurement.

## What comes through it anyway

Sixteen of the seventy-three rows name a specific place outside Britain and Ireland. More rows
carry foreign material; those sixteen are where the table itself says so. They arrive in three
unlike ways.

**Elsewhere as stage.** Of the six Shakespeare plays a school can set, not one is set in England.
The settings are Verona, Venice, Messina, Rome, eleventh-century Scotland — and, for *The
Tempest*, a colonial Renaissance with a question mark and no country at all. Add Browning's
Ferrara, and a pattern appears: England's most domestic authors do their thinking abroad. The
passport is the author's, never the setting's.

**Wars with a foreign address.** Tennyson takes the Crimea in 1854; Owen the Western Front;
Orwell the Russian revolution; Armitage the 2003 invasion of Iraq; Duffy a photographer's
cold-war conflict zones; Rumens an exile from a homeland the table leaves unspecified. Britain
was a belligerent in the Crimean War, on the Western Front and in Iraq; the allegory, the
photographs and the exile sit elsewhere, or nowhere the table will name. The sharpest is Beatrice
Garland's *Kamikaze*, the only Pacific-war row here and the interior life of a Japanese pilot's
family. Imtiaz Dharker's *A Century Later* does the same office for the 2012 Taliban attack on a
schoolgirl. Both are coded domestic, because both are English poems.

**The world as citizens.** The 2023–2025 exchange of texts grew this pile. The Worlds and Lives
cluster, first taught in 2023 and first examined in 2025, puts fifteen poems in front of a class,
and several of the new ones reach migration, heritage or elsewhere through people living in
Britain and their families. A conversation on an afternoon train from Purley to Victoria in 1955.
Leeds in 1869, a great-great-grandfather with eyes dried out by twelve hours of combing flax,
imagined by a descendant born and raised in the same city. Guyana revisited and found full of car
tyres, plastic bottles and styrofoam cups. Trinidad carried in a pocket.

The modern-text list moved the same way and left a datable seam: three rows whose last exam was
2024 — Bennett, Stephens, Ishiguro — and three first taught in 2023 and first examined in 2025:
Odimba's *Princess & The Hustler*, on the 1963 Bristol bus boycott; de Waal's *My Name is Leon*;
Pinnock's *Leave Taking*. The board's taster booklet for the Pinnock prints the scene it wants
taught, a daughter turning on her mother: "You're too busy bowing and scraping to your beloved
England. And where's it got you?"

England's classroom did not import that line. It grew it.

## The errand: one pot, one collection

The teacher guide published alongside the cluster walks through the pot poem, and says plainly
what it is for: the pot is used to explore cultural repatriation, colonialism and migration. The
speaker knows only half the story of how it reached England. The guide works through the
euphemisms a museum label might use and is blunt about where they land — the white boat sailing
to England, it says, reminds us of the truth of cultural theft. Then the poem turns and speaks to
the pot about itself, tells it that it is not really one of us, and asks it, twice, to answer.
Growl if you hear me. Pot? Pot?

So here is what this window imports. Not the pot: the guide places it in the museum's collection,
and what reaches a classroom is a poem about it — a poem that same guide reads as staged at the
display glass. Not a foreign literature either: a set of questions about a foreign object and
whether it should be here, asked in English by a poet the guide prints in lower case, shamshad
khan, described there as a second-generation British Asian from Manchester, writing for a museum
in her own city.

That is the mechanism in miniature. The world is admitted constantly, and admitted as content; it
clears customs in the hands of a writer who already belongs. And printed at the start of every
AQA mark scheme is a statement of what the subject is for: high-quality English literature
displays recognisable literary qualities and, though shaped by particular contexts, transcends
them and speaks to the universality of the human condition. AQA nowhere offers that as its reason
for setting no translated text. But hold the sentence against the shelf — my reading, not the
board's argument — and the two fit. If literature transcends its context, one board's menu can
stay entirely English-language without its compilers conceding a gap. You can reach Guyana, the
Pacific and a Nigerian pot without setting a translated page.

## Where the door does open

One part of this course has no set texts at all. Unseen poetry is a whole section of the second
paper and cannot appear in a table row, because nothing is prescribed: two poems, unseen, sat
cold. The specification guarantees the two poems and says nothing about where they come from. The
board's teaching guide for that section offers a starter collection — one it calls
non-prescriptive, telling teachers they may substitute poems of their own — and alongside Blake,
Hardy, Rossetti and Owen it prints Langston Hughes's 'Mother to Son' and Kyle Dargan's 'Poem
Resisting Arrest'. The cluster guide does the same at the foot of every entry, under other poems
for comparison: Czeslaw Milosz beside Raman Mundair, Rabindranath Tagore beside Liz Berry, Maya
Angelou beside Caleb Femi.

None of those names is a row in this table, and none can be: a suggestion in a guide is not a set
text. So the finding is narrower than an admission. AQA's guides suggest foreign poets without
prescribing them, and the unseen section — assessed, and heavily — leaves the identity of the
examined poems open until the paper is opened. What matters here is prescription, not assessment.

## Fifteen, sixteen, and no book on the desk

You were fifteen or sixteen once, in a room with a list that looked like the only list there was.
It never is. Somebody chose a board, somebody chose one play from six, and the set texts you
would be tested on narrowed to eighteen rows — one play, one novel, one modern text, fifteen
poems — before you walked in. Two more poems, named to nobody, waited on the paper.

If your own classroom's list ran on a subject named after your country, the test is not how many
foreigners were let in but how they travelled: as scenery, as wars placed abroad, and — in the
rows first examined in 2025 — as family history carried by people living in Britain. Not one came
carrying a translator's name.

And in summer 2025 a teenager could be asked about a pot in a Manchester collection that a poet
had spent a whole poem begging to answer: *growl if you hear me.* No anthology and no book was
allowed on the desk; the named poem was printed on the paper, and the poem you set beside it, you
brought in your head. An exam is not a bad place to be asked that question: it is a place where
you have to answer, on behalf of something brought here and never asked.

What this chapter cannot know: this is one awarding body's menu in one year, not England — other
boards set different books under the same Ofqual conditions, and every row here is a choice, so
the piles above describe what a school may set and never what a classroom read. No 2025 question
paper is in view, which makes the opening and closing scenes what the documents allow rather than
a report of anything a pupil was actually asked. And the grouping of sixteen foreign-placed rows
rests on interpretive codes and on a criterion of mine, while the documents state the subject's
aims without ever explaining why one book was set and another was not.
