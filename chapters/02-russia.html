The Post-Soviet Quartet · Chapter 02

Russia: The Staircase

A Russian child enters fifth grade at eleven, and somewhere in Moscow a document already knows, year by year, which foreigners they are going to meet on the way up.

At eleven, the world arrives as story: the myths of the world's peoples, required; Andersen's Snow Queen or another of his tales; a choice of Carroll or Tolkien; two works from a childhood shelf holding Twain, London and Bradbury; two adventures, with Stevenson as the printed example. At twelve it gets serious: Homer, in fragments, required; then Defoe's island, required; then Swift's giants and academies, required. At thirteen, Cervantes — the knight himself, chapters of him — and The Little Prince, whole. At fourteen, Shakespeare twice: a sonnet or two of the pupil's choosing, and Romeo and Juliet, required, with Molière's bourgeois gentleman for comedy. And at fifteen, the year this book measures every country at, the staircase reaches its landing: Dante at the gates of the afterlife, Hamlet on the battlements, Faust in his study, Byron's Childe Harold on the road — all four required — with two further slots where a pupil picks a Byron lyric and a teacher picks Hoffmann, Hugo or Scott.

That is a real window, and it is not the one Russia is usually judged by. Judge it by the exam and you would conclude the window barely exists: the grade-nine examination codifier compresses all foreign literature into a single choice line, a parenthesis holding Homer, Cervantes, Shakespeare and Molière as examples. But the exam is only the door out of school. The window is on the staircase, and the staircase is written down in the federal working program for literature — directly binding, since September 2023, on every organization teaching a state-accredited basic general education program. The program is not advice. It is the floor plan.

Facing one direction

So Russia turns out to run a large mandatory foreign shelf — twelve required foreign rows across the five grades, before any teacher makes any choice — and a narrow one. Walk the fixed, named core of the ladder and look at who is on it. Homer is ancient Greece; Dante is the Middle Ages; Cervantes, Shakespeare and Molière the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries; Defoe and Swift the eighteenth; Goethe and Byron the early nineteenth. The newest author on that named mandatory core is Saint-Exupéry, and he is there as a fable. The choice shelves widen the picture — Twain, London, Bradbury, Harper Lee, O. Henry and Cooper wait in the optional slots, so the named shelf as a whole is Europe plus North America — but no named author from Asia, Africa or Latin America appears anywhere in the twenty-six foreign rows; those worlds enter only anonymously, in grade five's myths and tales of the world's peoples. The required window faces in one direction: the European classics, its named core ending roughly where the twentieth century begins. Russia's program does not import the world. It imports the canon of Europe, as a stated inheritance.

Fragments and wholes

And it imports it in pieces. Look again at how the required foreigners arrive: Homer in fragments; Defoe and Swift in selected chapters; Cervantes in chapters; Shakespeare's tragedy in fragments of the pupil's or teacher's choosing; Dante in at least two fragments; Faust in at least two; Childe Harold in at least one. Ten of the eleven named required foreign works carry that stamp — fragments, chapters, by choice. And because so many of the stamps read "by choice," two pupils on the same staircase may have met quite different pieces of the same Dante. Now look at the domestic shelf beside them in grade nine: Griboyedov's Woe from Wit, whole. Pushkin's Eugene Onegin, whole. Lermontov's A Hero of Our Time, whole. Gogol's Dead Souls, whole. The program's arithmetic of attention is visible on the page: the world is met in excerpts, the homeland in full texts. A fifteen-year-old finishes the year having stood briefly at four of European literature's summits and having lived inside four Russian books.

The one exception to the fragment stamp is worth a close look, because it is the program's most tender act of admission. The single foreign work a Russian child is required to read whole, cover to cover, is The Little Prince — at thirteen, in the same year the knight arrives in chapters: a fable about a small person responsible for one rose on one planet. Everything else from abroad arrives as a monument to be visited in fragments; the one whole foreign book is the one about caring for something because you have tamed it. That the ladder's builders required tenderness entire and Dante in excerpts is perhaps the most human sentence the document never quite says.

What the body keeps

Then there is the question of what the body keeps. The program orders memorization the way a codifier never could: a pupil finishing grade nine must be able to recite at least twelve poetic works not learned in earlier years, and for certain named works the by-heart instruction is attached directly to the unit — the medieval Lay of Igor's Campaign, shelved as domestic by the program's own placement; Lomonosov's ode on the empress's accession; Woe from Wit; Dead Souls. Every work carrying that instruction is on the domestic shelf. The four required foreigners of grade nine — Dante, Shakespeare, Goethe, Byron — carry no memorization instruction at all.

In Kazakhstan, this book found a program whose every by-heart mandate pointed at foreign, mostly Russian, texts; in Ukraine, at foreign texts inside a subject built for them. Russia is the mirror: the world is for reading; the memory is for what is Russia's own. The staircase teaches a European inheritance to the mind and a national one to the body. That is this essay's reading, not the program's doctrine — the program mandates lists, hours and recitations, and gives reasons for none of them.

The landing

At the top of the staircase stands the exam, and the two documents part company. Five years of differentiated requirement — this Dante, that Hamlet, those chapters of Cervantes — collapse, in the grade-nine codifier, into one undifferentiated choice line where Homer, Cervantes, Shakespeare and Molière appear as examples. The exam does not exclude the world; it stops guaranteeing any particular piece of it. The program proves what is taught, the codifier what is promised at the door out of school, and the gap between them is the finding.

A pupil who climbs the whole staircase — five years, step by step, from the Snow Queen at eleven to Faust's study at fifteen — meets the foreign classics as a sequence of guided excursions, each visit measured out in fragments and chapters chosen by teacher or pupil, and meets the Russian classics as a house to be occupied: four whole books in the final year alone, and at least twelve poems to be carried out by heart. The window on the staircase is real, and Europe's canon climbs it year by year, all the way to the landing; it is climbed, and it is never kept. But the door of the house locks from the inside, and the key is learned by heart.
