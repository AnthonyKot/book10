Interlude: The Closed Windows

A tenth-year pupil in Bavaria may spend part of this school year with a Nobel laureate. The plan makes room for it: the Realschule curriculum — one of the two Bavarian secondary tracks read for this book, alongside the Gymnasium's — reserves a slot for a text by a winner of the Nobel Prize in Literature, paired with one full-length work of the twentieth or twenty-first century, by choice. Which winner, which work — the plan does not say. The prize is named. The writer never is.

Nearly everything literary in the two captured plans is built the same way: a category with the name left open. Works of youth literature, of European writers, of intercultural literature, in excerpts, by choice. Literary and philosophical texts of the Enlightenment, by choice. Ten rows is what the two curricula put into this book's table, and eight of them are such categories. The only two people the plans name are not literary authors at all but communication theorists, Paul Watzlawick and Friedemann Schulz von Thun, cited for their models of how a message works. The plans name the men who diagram how messages travel. They name no literary author anywhere.

So the book on the desk is real — required, complete, in some systems even counted — and the document that requires it cannot say which book it is. That is the situation this interlude collects. Vietnam, chapters ago, required a choice from each of eight named foreign literatures and named no author to fill the slots; most of the documents gathered here do not name even the literatures. Across all eight tables — Germany in its Bavarian slice, Mexico, Belgium's French- and Dutch-speaking frameworks, two Swiss plans, Norway, Italy's first liceo years, Japan's ninth grade, Mongolia's — five foreign writers are named in total: Homer, Virgil, Confucius, Kalidasa, Lu Xun. The newest of the five died in 1936. A ninth table, Kazakhstan's, ends the chapter for the opposite reason.

Reading as law

Belgium, Switzerland, and Norway wrote documents that name nothing at all. Their three tables in this book are empty, and the emptiness is the documents' own design: each mandates that literature be read and declines to say what.

The two Belgian frameworks captured here answer separately and alike. The French Community's framework of 2022 directs that four or five complete long works be offered in common each secondary year — whole books, counted by the year — and adds that its lists of works are examples for the teacher. The Flemish attainment targets ask pupils to interpret and analyze literary texts, their narrative, poetic, and theatrical structures. In both, the pupil's book has a cover and a title; the framework knows only its category.

Switzerland keeps no single national codifier, and the two plans read here answer the same way twice. Zürich's wants knowledge of selected authors and texts, engagement with literary texts from other cultures, and books pupils pick to match their own interests — part of the choice handed to the pupil in so many words. The common plan for French-speaking Switzerland counts what it will not name: at least two complete works a year, adapted to the pupils' age. Complete, counted, adapted — and untitled.

Norway's is a statute. The Norwegian-subject curriculum is a regulation, and its Year 10 aims expect a pupil to read fiction and nonfiction in bokmål and in nynorsk and in translation from Sami and other languages, and to compare and interpret novels, short stories, poetry, and other texts against their historical context and the pupil's own time. Languages, genres, two spellings of its own language — and, from end to end of the saved text, no author and no title.

The program of nouns

In Mexico the open slot has a purpose written on it. The 2024 synthetic program builds the third year of secondary school around ends rather than titles: essays of aesthetic interpretation on texts written in Spanish or translated; the staging of dramatic texts linked to the community; literary works from distinct cultures and from the linguistic variants of Spanish; texts in favor of an inclusive society; collective artistic creations built on literary texts; songs, corridos, and word games in Indigenous languages. Nine rows, every one of them open; six permit a translated work, and none names one. The program's nouns are community, culture, inclusion. The object of its sentences is always a kind of text, never a person.

The ancestors

Italy names foreign individuals — and every foreign name it prints is ancient. The national indications for the first two years of the liceo, Italy's academic upper school, prescribe the Homeric poems, the Attic tragedy of the fifth century, Virgil's Aeneid, and the Bible, with an open choice of primary Greek and Latin authors beside them. Virgil enters coded as a translation from the Latin: the ancestor become, administratively, a foreigner. For the world since, the document keeps one open row — readings from modern authors, including foreign ones, by choice. Between antiquity and this morning, no foreign name.

One neighbor, seen twice

Japan's ninth-grade rows come from three sources that do not merge into one national list: the ministry's course of study and two publishers' textbook tables of contents. Between them they name two foreign texts, both marked mandatory, both Chinese: selections from the Analects of Confucius, and Lu Xun's story "My Old Home," in Takeuchi Yoshimi's translation — a story that has stood in the major ninth-grade textbooks since 1953. The thirteen other retained rows are Japanese. Two named admissions, one neighbor: once in its antiquity, once in its twentieth century, the second view held in place for more than seventy years.

The guest in the anthology

Mongolia's state assessment instrument for literature names capabilities, not works — read, analyze, stage, debate, present an independently chosen book — and at several grades it points reading toward the best works of East and West without saying which. The names live one shelf down, in the anthology. The Grade IX literature textbook — official, fourth printing, Ulaanbaatar, 2023 — prints fourteen selections. Thirteen are Mongolian: the Gesar and Jangar epics, the oath-poem of Queen Mandukhai the Wise, Tsogt Taij's verses cut into rock, the modern novellas and stories. The fourteenth and last literary selection, sixteen pages of it, is the excerpt of a Sanskrit play: Kalidasa's Recognition of Shakuntala. Its headnote tells pupils the play has entered the treasury of world classics and been staged in London's famous theatres — and adds, on the record of the scholar and writer Byambyn Rinchen, whose own novella appears earlier in the same book, that Mongolians have known this play since the fourteenth century, when it reached them through Tibetan. One foreign admission in fourteen — and the one is a guest the headnote dates to the fourteenth century.

The exception

Kazakhstan closes the interlude as its opposite: a table thick with names. Its grade-nine program yields sixty-two rows here, some mandate and some menu, and twenty-seven of them are foreign-coded — twenty-one translated from Russian, four from English, two from Kyrgyz. Pushkin three times over, Gogol twice, Chekhov, Dostoevsky, Tolstoy, Shakespeare's sonnets and Romeo and Juliet. What sets the table apart is its memory column. Twelve rows carry a work-specific by-heart requirement, and all twelve are foreign-coded: eleven Russian, one an excerpt of Shakespeare's sonnets. Under the program's heading for "the theme of the little man," Gogol's Overcoat comes with a learning objective — find the passages that carry the work's theme, and read them expressively by heart. None of the thirty-five domestic rows carries any such requirement. These counts come from four appendices of a much larger ministerial order, not all of them fully transcribed — the appendices for the Abai schools and for Uyghur-, Uzbek-, and Tajik-language literature are absent — so this is one corridor of a multilingual school system, not the whole building. In that corridor, every text the program marks for memory is a foreign-coded one.

What the closed tables say together

Set side by side, the eight tables before Kazakhstan agree on almost nothing except the shape of their silence. Some mandate ancient epics, some count whole books by the year, some write purposes over open slots; the named foreign shelf, across all eight, contracts to five people, while categories, quotas, and anthology choices carry the rest of the reading. And where the state stopped naming, the naming did not stop — it only left the documents. Mongolia's names live in a textbook's table of contents; Zürich's plan hands part of the choice to the pupil's own interests. In Bavaria the prize is still named and the writer still is not. In Ulaanbaatar, the one named guest has been coming since the fourteenth century.
