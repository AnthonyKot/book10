# India: The Lesson That Won't Be Asked

Before an Indian fifteen-year-old reads a line of Anton Chekhov, they are asked to fill in a
table. The columns are printed for them: customs similar to Indian ones, customs different
from Indian ones. Above the table sits a short article about Russian weddings — the groom
climbing an apartment block's stairs, answering a riddle at each landing — and then the
question of whether Indian and Russian weddings have any customs in common, to be worked out
with a partner.

Only then does the play begin: Lomov arrives at Chubukov's house in a dress-suit to propose to
Natalya, and the three of them quarrel about a meadow and a dog until the proposal is nearly
lost. The unit ends with a group discussion of the qualities one should look for in a marriage
partner, under headings that include attitude to money and wealth.

The errand is written on the page before the work starts: a Russian farce about two landowning
families, brought into an Indian classroom to be set beside an Indian wedding, in ruled
columns, by a child with a partner.

## The door is a book, and a subtraction

India's window has an unusual hinge. The Central Board of Secondary Education issues a
curriculum for each subject code, and the last page of that curriculum carries a list of
prescribed books and a note naming the lessons from which no questions will be asked. The
books themselves are published by the National Council of Educational Research and Training.
The board that examines names the anthology, and then it subtracts.

The English syllabus prints its chapter list in full, prose and poems, book by book. The Hindi
syllabi do not. Hindi Course A names *Kshitij Part 2* and *Kritika Part 2* and stops — then
names, from *Kritika*, only the lessons it removes, one of them a satire called "George the
Fifth's Nose". That is the hole in this corpus: no defining document held here lists
*Kritika*'s remaining contents, so the rows once coded from it were quarantined. The only
*Kritika* chapters the syllabus names are the ones it takes away.

No defining document here mandates memorisation of any named row — not a title, not an
excerpt, not a stated quantity of texts by heart. What the English paper asks for is an
extract and a short written answer.

## Streams in one corridor

A school's window here is not one window. In the same Class 10 corridor a pupil may sit Hindi
Course A — mother-tongue Hindi, with *Kshitij* and *Kritika* — or Hindi Course B, with
*Sparsh* and *Sanchayan*; English Language and Literature is a separate code with its own two
readers. Same board, different worlds. How many pupils walk which door, and when each stream
sits its paper, these documents do not say.

This corpus speaks for the English stream, partly for Hindi Course A, not at all for Hindi
Course B: forty-three rows, thirty-one from the English syllabus — twenty-two in *First
Flight*, nine in *Footprints Without Feet* — and twelve from *Kshitij Part 2*, every one
Class 10, prescribed, unmarked for memorisation.

## What comes through the English door

Twenty of the thirty-one English-syllabus rows arrive from outside India, already in English.
Nineteen rows across the whole corpus are domestic — the twelve Hindi ones, plus seven Indian
pieces inside the English readers: a Goan baker, Coorg, Assam tea, a Tamil girl's bus ride,
the sermon at Benares, Ruskin Bond's thief, Bholi.

And four rows — four, out of forty-three — come from a language that is neither English nor
Indian: a Mexican story from Spanish, a Dutch diary, a Russian one-act play, a French short
story. López y Fuentes, Anne Frank, Chekhov, Maupassant.

The syllabus names this shape itself, in its objectives, as an aim. The course sets out to
develop sensitivity to and appreciation of world literature — and then, in the same sentence,
scopes that world to "varieties of English" and the cultures embedded in it. Further down, the
objectives turn homeward, to Indian languages, multilingualism, translations and Indian
literature.

The poetry section reads clearest. *First Flight* carries ten poems, every one from abroad in
English — seven by American poets (Frost twice, Wells, Berryman, Rich, Sandburg, Nash), one
each from a Welsh, an Australian and an Irish poet. No Indian poet holds a poetry slot in the
English reader. The Indian poetry is next door, in Hindi, where Surdas and Tulsidas open
*Kshitij* and all twelve rows are domestic.

By subject the table supports one split, and only one: each row either carries a
place-and-period setting or carries none. Thirteen of the twenty-four rows from abroad have no
historical setting at all — a tiger, a fog, a dragon, a snowfall — while fourteen of the
nineteen domestic rows do carry one, from ancient Benares to Bismillah Khan's shehnai in 1947.
Whatever else that is, it is not foreign history set against Indian timelessness. And no row
here is coded to Partition — a fact about one Class 10 year with a quarantined reader in it,
and nothing larger.

## Chekhov, twice

What this corpus can do that a list cannot is watch one writer walk through two doors of the
same school.

Through the English door, Chekhov is a chapter with a page number: *First Flight*'s last unit,
printed on the contents page, the one author in the book who wrote in Russian, his surname
spelled Chekov there. The reader's own blurb tells the class what the farce is about — the
tendency of wealthy families to seek ties with other wealthy families, increasing their estates
by encouraging marriages that make good economic sense. He is examinable: the paper draws
extracts from drama and prose and short answers from *First Flight*.

Then the unit closes, and the last page is addressed to the teacher rather than the child.
What we have done, it says: given you a play by the famous Russian writer, Anton Chekhov. What
you can do: dictate the biographical information below, and let the students guess the name of
the playwright. What follows is a paragraph with a hole where a name should be — born at
Stratford-on-Avon, married to an older woman, retired, dead, leaving behind a body of work
that still stands as a pinnacle in world literature.

The one Russian in the book is handed over, and the class is invited to guess the Englishman.
The exercise assumes recognition: it withholds the name because it expects the name back. That
is what a shared shelf looks like from inside — not a text on the syllabus, but a riddle whose
answer is assumed to be in the room already.

Through the Hindi B door, Chekhov appears exactly once, and not as a chapter. On the last page
of that curriculum, under the note saying questions will not be asked from the following
lessons, in the block for the prescribed reader *Sparsh Part 2*, between Bihari's couplets and
a Mahadevi Varma poem, stands Anton Chekhov's name in Devanagari beside a story called
"Girgit" — and in brackets after it, the whole lesson.

The corpus holds no row for it, and whether the current *Sparsh* reprint still prints the story
these documents do not settle. What they settle is the naming. In one stream Chekhov is an
examinable text with a warm-up table about weddings; in the stream across the corridor, his
name is printed for the sole purpose of being fenced off the paper. Placement is all a syllabus
shows. It gives no reasons, and none is inferred here.

## Underneath the syllabus

There is a layer below the rows, and it is where some of the world actually lives. In the
exercises after "A Tiger in the Zoo" a second poem is printed whole — Rilke's "The Panther," in
Stephen Mitchell's English — set beside Leslie Norris's tiger. It is not a chapter and not a
listed set-text row; whether an exercise insert can reach the paper is not a question these
documents answer.

The layer runs the other way too. Inside the Chekhov unit, the writing exercise hands the class
a passage from Tagore's *The Home and the World* to turn into a script — Bimala demanding that
foreign goods be cleared out of the market, her husband refusing: "To tyrannise for the country
is to tyrannise over the country." A swadeshi argument, in the middle of the one
foreign-language play in the book. The anthology put them on facing pages; it does not say why,
and neither will I.

The shelf closes on paperwork. *Footprints Without Feet* still tells its teachers, in a
preface, that it represents writers from across the world — Indian, British, French, American
and Japanese. Its nine rows here are Indian, British, French and American. And the English
reader's last printed page, after Chekhov and after Shakespeare's unnamed biography, is the
Fundamental Rights of the Constitution of India, including the right of minorities to conserve
their language, script and culture.

## The lesson that won't be asked

If you ever watched a teacher go down the contents page with a pen — not this one, not this
one — you saw the only editorial act in a school year a child witnesses directly: the shelf
shrinking in front of them, for reasons nobody explains, shortly before the syllabus becomes
real.

India's documents perform that act in writing, at the bottom of the page, in a box, and those
chapters keep a strange afterlife: still printed, still in the bag, weightless. In the syllabus
that governed Class 10 in 2024-25, one such name in the Hindi B box is Anton Chekhov's — and
one corridor away, in English, the same writer arrives with a warm-up table about weddings.

What this chapter can't know: this is one board, one grade and one year — every row is Class 10
under the Central Board of Secondary Education in 2024-25 — and while the syllabus claims come
from those 2024-25 documents, everything described from inside a reader was read in a later
reprint of the same revised edition, so the pages a 2024-25 pupil held are not verified here.
The Hindi side is under-receipted: *Kritika Part 2* contributes no rows at all and Hindi Course
B was consulted only for its prescribed-books line and its exclusion note, which makes the
Chekhov finding one about two documents rather than two bookshelves. Why that story is off the
Hindi B paper, why the English reader's poetry carries no Indian poet, and why a swadeshi
passage sits inside the Russian unit are questions no document here answers, and none is
guessed at.
