The Anglosphere Paradox · Chapter 10

The United States: The Book You Bring Yourself

Picture a sitting of the kind the College Board's own sample materials describe — a sample, not a room anyone observed. Three essays, in a fixed order: a poem, a passage of prose, and then a third question that stops naming books.

The first two hand the student their texts. The third hands over a concept — a character given a gift that is also a burden — and then does what no ministry in this book does: it tells the student to choose a work of fiction, either from their own reading or from the list printed below. That list is a column of titles offered the way a waiter offers bread; the student may take one or ignore them all. Then, with a recommended forty minutes to spend on it, they write in the title of the book they intend to be examined on.

That blank is the American window, and it is cut one reader at a time.

Nobody fills it in from above — but not because nobody chooses. There is no federal ministry with power to name a book, no national exam codifier, no enforceable list of the kind that anchors every other chapter here; what stands in their place is selection pushed down and dispersed. The exam question comes from a course and exam description published by the College Board, a private organization, which tells schools that the works themselves are selected locally. The titles come from somewhere else entirely: an appendix to academic standards that states adopted one at a time, whose own work group made the final choice of what it printed. Between them the two documents hold the two things a codifier holds — force, and titles — and neither holds both. The exam has force over a score and names no required book. The appendix names sixty-three titles for the ninth- and tenth-grade band and denies, in its own front matter, being a list at all.

The door that says it is not a door

The appendix is called Text Exemplars and Sample Performance Tasks, and its opening page explains what an exemplar is for: the samples are there mainly to show the level of complexity and quality that the standards require students in a grade band to engage with. Then the writers close the door they appeared to be opening. "They expressly do not represent a partial or complete reading list."

Take the disclaimer at face value. All sixty-three titles, stories through technical subjects, are printed as exemplars, not assigned, and none carries a line to learn by heart. Not one of them is a book an American fifteen-year-old must read. They are specimens of a difficulty level, chosen to show teachers what that level feels like in the hand. The criterion of admission the document states is complexity, quality and range. The criterion it does not state is nationality.

The other document draws its one boundary the same way. By the end of the course, the AP description says, students should have studied a variety of texts by diverse authors across a range of periods, from the English Renaissance to the present. A real boundary — and a boundary of period. Both American doors specify a shape and leave the passport unchecked.

So the interesting question is not what this country admits. It is what the shelf looks like when the choosing is dispersed to a work group, districts, schools and teachers.

What walks in

The answer, first, is an inheritance. By this book's own count — the appendix itself tags no author with a nationality — forty-one of the sixty-three titles are domestic, and of the twenty-two that are not, eleven never met a translator — Shakespeare twice, Donne, Shelley, Housman; Achebe's Things Fall Apart, Fugard's "Master Harold"…and the boys, Zusak's The Book Thief — most of them British, the family books that came over with the language.

The eleven that did meet a translator are old, European and few, and the appendix names the glass as well as the view: Fagles for Homer, Ronald Wilks for Gogol's barber who finds a nose in his breakfast roll, Constance Garnett for Turgenev, Derek Prouse for Ionesco. That is more than a citation habit — the complexity these rows exemplify is the complexity of an English page a named translator made. Among the translated eleven, nothing comes from Asia, from Latin America, from the Arabic-speaking world; Africa arrives on this shelf twice, already in English.

Several of the foreign rows carry a state consuming its people, none of it American — the Holocaust, the neighbours turning into rhinoceroses under 1930s fascism, apartheid South Africa. A lean, not a law; and the South African row is where this shelf does its strangest choosing.

What a foreign book is for here

Every other document in this book eventually says something to a teacher about a foreign work: study this alongside that, note the ties to our own culture, learn these lines by heart. The teaching language this appendix attaches to a named work is its sample performance tasks — illustrations, its front matter says, of how the standards apply to exemplars already chosen — and each one closes with a standards code in square brackets.

Odysseus, the man of twists and turns, appears in a task that asks students to analyze how he reflects conflicting motivations through his dealings with other characters, and how those conflicting loyalties develop the poem's themes. Then the code, naming the standard being exercised. What the task asks of the Odyssey is that one numbered skill be practised on it. Two tasks later Macbeth appears as raw material — students analyze how the Japanese filmmaker Akira Kurosawa, in Throne of Blood, draws on and transforms the play to develop a similar plot set in feudal Japan. In these tasks an admitted foreigner's errand is to be an instance of an operation. Not a feeling a country cannot source domestically; a skill it can be shown performing.

And yet the appendix does something no standard requires. It prints excerpts, and somebody chose them; the front matter concedes the awkwardness, granting that where excerpts appear they serve only as stand-ins for the full text.

The stand-in chosen for Fugard is not the scene the play is famous for. "Master Harold"…and the boys ends with a white schoolboy spitting in the face of the Black man who raised him. What the appendix prints instead is Sam, earlier, trying to explain ballroom dancing to the boy: none of us knows the steps, there is no music playing, and it does not stop with us. "America has bumped into Russia, England is bumping into India, rich man bumps into poor man." Those are big collisions, he says. They make for a lot of bruises.

A South African play about a South African injury, excerpted at the exact half-page where it turns and names America first in a list of collisions. The choice is an editorial act and a visible one.

The list nobody collects

What an American pupil actually reads is set locally, by districts, schools and teachers — the kind of choosing that lives in a board meeting, a department vote, a publisher's catalogue. That is the American arrangement: selection everywhere, and no single national address for it.

Which leaves the exam room. Every other window in this book can be photographed: a codifier you can print, a program with page numbers, a spine a pupil carries in a bag. The American window has to be reconstructed one answer at a time, and no document in this chapter ever collects those answers into a list. What the country shows instead is the act itself: a student, a concept, a recommended forty minutes — and a hand writing in the title of the book they brought themselves.
