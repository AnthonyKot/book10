# Vietnam: A Bookcase, and Eight Names for the World

Before it says anything at all about foreign literature, Vietnam's national program for Ngữ
văn — the single subject that carries the Vietnamese language and its literature — furnishes
the room. The minimum equipment of the subject, it says, is a reference bookcase: stories,
poetry, drama, reportage, argument, picture books. It also wants portraits of the program's
major writers, and pictures illustrating some of the major works — six titles follow as
examples, between a *such as* and an ellipsis, and the program sets no number of pictures and
no place to hang them. At the desks it wants whole books, telling teachers to limit teaching by
extract.

So furnish the room yourself, because the document won't. Put the bookcase where you like and
the pictures wherever you last saw pictures in a school. The six titles under them are not
arbitrary: the same six appear earlier in the program as its compulsory works, the only
literature it obliges both a textbook's author and a teacher to carry out. An anonymous poem
from the Lý dynasty; Trần Quốc Tuấn's address to his officers; Nguyễn Trãi's great
proclamation; *Truyện Kiều*; Nguyễn Đình Chiểu's funeral oration for the fighters of Cần Giuộc;
Hồ Chí Minh's declaration of independence. Four of the six sit against an invasion or an
occupation — Song, Mongol, Ming, French — and a fifth against the end of a world war.

The rest of the world's literature is in this room too. It is not among the six, and on the
day the program was signed it was not yet a book. It was one line.

## The line

Foreign literature: choose at least one work for each of the following literatures — English,
French, American, Greek, Russian, Japanese, Chinese, Indian.

Eight literatures. A floor of one work apiece. No titles, no authors, no centuries — eight
names, and not one book.

The two lines above it are built the other way round. Vietnamese folk literature gets five
categories, each with a minimum attached — folk-tale genres, folk verses, an epic, an
ethnic-minority verse narrative, a script for one of the traditional theatre forms. Vietnamese
written literature gets thirteen names, Nguyễn Trãi and Nguyễn Du and Hồ Xuân Hương among them.
Then, in the same tier and the same grammar, foreign literature gets eight
countries. At home the program guarantees particular writers. Abroad it guarantees only the
literatures a work must come from. Nothing in the program explains the difference, and a list
cannot be made to give a reason for itself.

Nor is the floor a promise of space. The program asks for a relative balance between Vietnamese
and foreign literature, between East and West, and then defines its own word against the
obvious reading of it: "'Balance' is understood as a suitable proportion, not an equal one." A
ministry writing down that a quota is a floor and not a scale.

## Whose job is a name

Take one of the eight names — Nga, Russia — and follow it toward the bookcase. Somebody has to
turn it into a page, and the program is unusually clear about who: it grades its own force in a
single sentence. There are three tiers, and the addressee changes as you go down.
Compulsory works bind textbook authors and teachers alike, but only *as the program provides* —
and the program provides by grade, so what binds a particular teacher is whatever it has placed
in the grades they teach. Compulsory-selection works, the eight names among
them, bind one person: the textbook author, who must choose a work by an author the program has
named. Suggested works bind nobody.

This is the door, and it opens onto a desk rather than onto a classroom. The circular that
promulgates it, signed by the Minister of Education and Training at the end of 2018 and in
force from February 2019, addresses its foreign-literature instruction not to a country's
teachers but to whoever is writing a book for them. The word *Nga* lands on somebody's desk.
The teacher will meet it later, bound into a volume, as a story with a title, and the program
that required it nowhere in sight.

## The back of the book

Suppose that person looks for help. The program's own suggestions are at the back, grouped by
genre and then, by its own rule, arranged alphabetically by the Vietnamese title, with the
author's name in brackets after it. Nothing there says which entry answers which of the eight
names. The two halves of the document are never joined: the quota
names literatures, the menu names books, and no line runs between them.

The menu also rations its authors. Each one gets a single line across twelve years of
schooling, and the only exemption belongs to the authors of the six compulsory works, all of
them Vietnamese. And it declines to be a schedule: it calls itself not all the material for the
grades but only illustrative examples, and the same page lets textbook authors go outside it
for equivalent texts. The program states the thought elsewhere as a principle: it has an open
orientation as to materials.

So in the grade 10–12 fiction list, sorted by first letter of the Vietnamese title, Hemingway
sits between *Odysseus* and *Số đỏ*, and nowhere does the document say that he is the American
one. Across the two grade bands this corpus covers, twenty-one entries name a foreign author or
a foreign origin: six English, five French, four Chinese, two Greek, two Russian, one Japanese,
one Spanish. An entry is not the same thing as a work — La Fontaine's line prints two fables,
Lu Xun's prints three, and *Sherlock Holmes* stands in for a shelf. Not one is matched to a
slot. Eight names in one half of the document, twenty-one entries scattered through the other,
and no line drawn between them by anyone but the reader.

Two edges make the point sharply. *One Hundred Years of Solitude* is printed like everything
else, a Vietnamese title and a bracketed G. Marquez, and it is the one named foreign entry here
written in a language — Spanish — that answers to none of the eight names. India is the one
name of the eight for which the grades covered here offer no named example at all. A suggestion
matching no requirement; a requirement with no suggestion.

## Two teachers

The two Russian-language stories in the menu are printed as flatly as the rest — a dash, a
Vietnamese title, a bracket: *The First Teacher*, C. Aitmatov; *The Man in a Case*, A. Chekhov.
That both were written in Russian is a fact about language and nothing more. The document does
not say that either answers to *Nga*, or that anything else does; and the label will not even
carry a nationality, since Chinghiz Aitmatov was Kyrgyz and wrote in both Kyrgyz and Russian.
In this list a foreign author is an initial, a surname and a bracket — unhoused.

The titles rhyme before anyone opens the books: one names a teacher, the other a man who has
put himself away. Open them and the rhyme holds. Chekhov's Belikov teaches Greek and keeps
every part of himself in a case, and his fear settles over a whole town. Aitmatov's Duishen is
the opposite man and the opposite failure — a demobilised soldier with barely more schooling
than his pupils, who turns a stable into a classroom and teaches from almost nothing, carrying
children across an icy stream on his back.

Now look at what surrounds them. The grade 10–12 Vietnamese fiction here is heavy with the
wars: Anh Đức, Nguyễn Thi, Phan Tứ, Nguyễn Minh Châu, Bảo Ninh, Nguyễn Khải — six entries whose
subject is the anti-US war or its aftermath. That describes what those books are about, not
what the ministry wants them for, and the two Russian stories carry no such tag. So what
follows is this essay's reading and not the document's: beside a
domestic shelf that keeps returning to the war, these two imports are both about a room with a
teacher in it, one of them a warning and one of them a debt. Whether that ever crossed anyone's
mind in Hà Nội, the circular does not say, and a list cannot be made to say it.

## The last instruction is the loosest

Come back to the room. There is one more thing the program asks a pupil to do with a book, and
from grade 6 to grade 12 it asks it in the same sentence, word for word: "Learn by heart some
favourite verse passages or poems from the program."

Seven grades, seven identical lines. No work is named, no number is set, and the sentence does
not say who decides that a poem is a favourite — the pupil, the textbook, or the teacher. The
primary grades are written the other way: grade 1 asks for four or five short passages already
studied, and the count climbs year by year to at least ten or twelve longer ones by grade 5.
The counting stops when primary school does. What replaces it is the favourite.

So no work here carries a memorization mandate of its own — which means only that no text is
named and no quantity tied to one, not that nothing is learned by heart in Vietnam. Learning by
heart is itself one of the outcomes the program requires.

That is the shape of this window, top to bottom: a named domestic canon; eight unnamed
literatures, addressed to a person with a manuscript; a menu the document says is only
examples; and an instruction to learn a poem by heart with the poem left blank.

The blank is the last thing this document does, and it is the same move it made in the quota
line: require the thing, decline to name it. If you can still recite something from when you
were fifteen, there was a document behind it too, somewhere, with a blank in it — and somebody
filled that blank in. A ministry, an editor, a teacher, or you. The Vietnamese program says
the blank must be filled. It does not say by whom.

What this chapter can't know: this is one multi-grade national program as promulgated in 2018,
not a school year's syllabus and not an examination list, and of the grade bands its menu is
published in, only the upper two are counted here. Because the quota binds a textbook's author
rather than a teacher, what any pupil actually read depends on a book this corpus does not
contain — an available window, then, not a taught one. And where the essay says what a work is
about, that is a reading of the work, never the ministry's statement of what it is for.
