# Ukraine: The Other Literature Door

At fifteen, a school timetable can make a theory of the world look like an ordinary Tuesday. There is Ukrainian language. There is Ukrainian literature. And then there is **Зарубіжна література**: Foreign Literature, not a visiting unit inside another course but a subject with its own name, program and hour.

That is Ukraine’s door. The remarkable thing is not that Swift, Goethe or Harper Lee can enter a Ukrainian classroom. Translated books enter classrooms everywhere. Here, the crossing itself has an administrative home. The Nikolenko model program says that Foreign Literature “вивчається окремо як самостійний і самодостатній предмет”—is studied separately as an independent and self-sufficient subject (p. 5). A window on other people’s writing has become part of the building.

## The door has two leaves

The Ministry of Education and Science recommends model curricula from which schools and teachers construct teaching programs. The documents therefore do more than suggest that children read widely, but less than decree that every child will encounter every title in the table. Nikolenko and her co-authors divide the course into an obligatory component and a variable one: prescribed works on one side; works from which a teacher may choose on the other (p. 11). The Voloshchuk program describes its Grade 9 design as a “мікромодель історії світової літератури від давнини до сьогодення”—a micro-model of world literary history from antiquity to the present (p. 2).

Separate does not mean sealed off from Ukrainian culture. The Nikolenko program says the subject is taught in Ukrainian and presents the works in Ukrainian translation; it places Ukrainian language and Ukrainian literature beside Foreign Literature within the same language-and-literature field (p. 5). The Voloshchuk program makes the hinge even clearer: translation is how writing in another language is integrated into Ukrainian culture, while authors connected to Ukrainian territory belong to the country’s multicultural inheritance (pp. 2, 4). So the door does not open onto an untouched “abroad.” Every entrant arrives through a Ukrainian translator, can be compared with Ukrainian writing and is discussed in the state language. The subject makes crossing a border into a reading practice.

That distinction keeps the shelf honest. The repaired table contains thirty-nine foreign-literature rows: thirteen classified as mandatory and twenty-six as choice-list admissions. Those are not thirty-nine books piled on one pupil’s desk. A row can bundle poems, offer one title against another, or sit in a menu from which the teacher selects. The table records what may pass through the door and under what label; it does not reconstruct a single school’s completed year.

The obligatory side begins as a compressed history of literary forms. Swift brings the Enlightenment into Lilliput. Goethe’s nature lyric, Prometheus poem and supernatural ballad let one writer occupy several imaginative climates. Schiller gives the ode; Heine, the song cycle; Byron, lyric and pilgrimage and a European poem about Mazeppa. Dante, Petrarch and Shakespeare turn the sonnet into a form that can survive the journey into Ukrainian. Balzac brings the novella of money. The sequence is not a neutral survey of the planet. It is a route through selected works, and the program itself calls the selection representative rather than exhaustive.

Then the shelf stops behaving like a museum. *To Kill a Mockingbird* enters the Jim Crow South; Barbara Kosmowska’s *Goldfish* enters a young person’s family life. Ibsen, Shaw and Maeterlinck turn the classroom into a stage on which marriage, language, self-creation and desire can be argued aloud. On the choice side, *Jane Eyre*, *The Catcher in the Rye*, *The Curious Incident of the Dog in the Night-Time* and *Harry Potter and the Order of the Phoenix* sit within reach of readers still close to their protagonists’ ages. The door admits old forms, but it does not require foreignness to smell of dust.

Its geography is broad and uneven. The table marks source languages including German, Italian, Romanian, French, Norwegian, Polish, Yiddish, Russian and Japanese. English accounts for nineteen of the thirty-nine rows—a shade under half the window. That concentration is a fact about this shelf, not a measure of how “open” Ukraine is. It means that a course designed as world literary history often reaches the contemporary world through Anglophone books: Bradbury’s burning library, Zusak’s wartime Germany, Foer’s bereaved New York, Asimov’s robots, Whitehead’s underground railway, Morpurgo’s war horse.

The other languages matter partly because they complicate the word *foreign*. Sholem Aleichem is represented by the Yiddish-language *Tevye the Dairyman*. He was born in Pereiaslav, yet the row stands in Foreign Literature and its note names the Jewish-Ukrainian historical experience. The program’s teaching language does not flatten that experience into a nationality label. It calls the work a story of “історичного зламу, який пройшов крізь долю людини і народу”—a historical rupture that passed through the fate of a person and a people (Nikolenko, p. 81). Tevye’s errand is therefore unusually precise: large history must arrive through a milkman’s household, folk humour and displacement. The window looks outward and finds part of Ukraine’s own multilingual past looking back.

## The shelf that will not sort cleanly

Two Russian-language works sit in the repaired foreign shelf, and both resist the shortcut of calling author, language and territory the same thing. The Ukrainian-born, Russian-language Nikolai Gogol appears through either *The Government Inspector* or *The Overcoat*. One turns officialdom into farce; the other makes urban hierarchy press down on a “little man.” The table’s note ties the row’s retention to Gogol’s Ukrainian origin.

The other is *Heart of a Dog* by the Kyiv-born, Russian-language Mikhail Bulgakov. Its table note records both Kyiv birth and anti-totalitarian satire. The Voloshchuk program is more exact about the classroom errand, calling the novella “гостра політична сатира на більшовицьку революцію 1917 р.”—a sharp political satire on the Bolshevik revolution—and directing attention to the attempt to create a “new person” and a “new society” (p. 97). The shared question is not which passport owns satire. It is what satire can see when political systems propose to manufacture human beings.

None of this proves why a ministry retained one writer or removed another. A list proves admission; its notes and teaching prompts prove the frame supplied for that admission. Here the supplied frames are Ukrainian connection, imperial bureaucracy and anti-totalitarian critique. Beyond that, motive would be guesswork.

The same discipline helps with the shelf’s historical wounds. *Tevye the Dairyman* carries pogroms and Jewish displacement around the collapse of an empire. *Heart of a Dog* carries the revolution. *Three Comrades* follows First World War veterans into the Weimar crisis and Depression. *The Book Thief* approaches Nazi Germany and the Holocaust; *Extremely Loud & Incredibly Close*, the grief of 9/11; *The Underground Railroad*, American slavery; *Heart of Darkness*, colonial violence in the Congo. Their presence does not establish a single ministry intention called “teaching trauma.” It establishes a set of admitted encounters in which public catastrophe is made intimate: a friendship, a child, a family, a journey, a voice.

## What enters the body

Five foreign rows do something stronger than appear on a reading list: the Voloshchuk program explicitly places them under “Твори для вивчення напам’ять,” works to be learned by heart (pp. 103–104). They are Heine, Dante, Petrarch, Shakespeare and Mihai Eminescu. Choice still operates inside the command: one of two Petrarch sonnets, one of two Shakespeare sonnets, one of three Heine poems. But after the choice, the work must be remembered.

Eminescu’s *To Bukovina* is the revealing edge of this little memory shelf. A Romanian-language poem addresses a region whose cultural geography exceeds a single national literature. The program asks the class to attend to Bukovina’s landscape, the lyric speaker’s inner world and the writer’s connection to the region. Then it asks for the poem by heart. Foreign literature is not only information about elsewhere; translated language is placed in a Ukrainian-speaking pupil’s mouth.

This is where the institutional door lands in an actual classroom. Think back to the exercise book that belonged to one subject, the teacher who could turn a poem into next Thursday’s recitation, the few lines still lodged in memory long after the examination vanished. A Ukrainian fifteen-year-old can have a separate notebook whose title means *the literature from abroad*. The window is not a metaphor to that pupil. It is on the timetable, and sometimes it has to be spoken without looking down.

## Limits

This is a one-year, Grade 9 slice assembled from model programs, not an observation of every Ukrainian classroom and not a record of what any named pupil was examined on. It combines admissions from documents with different internal structures. “Mandatory” and “choice-list” are corpus classifications; alternatives, bundled works and teacher autonomy mean the row total is not a pupil workload.

The table also cannot establish a post-2022 removal story because it contains no matched prior-year shelf. The two Russian-language admissions make the before-and-after question sharper, but they do not answer it. Any claim about what was removed, how much changed or why must wait for a dated earlier list reconciled by the same method.

Finally, memorization is counted only where a defining document explicitly names a text for learning by heart. Expressive reading, closed-book assessment, optional recitation and general recommendations do not qualify. The five surviving memorization flags are therefore narrower than the larger culture of poetry learning that may exist in individual schools.
