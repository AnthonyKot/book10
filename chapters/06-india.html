Three Ways to Hand a Child the World · Chapter 06

India: The Lesson That Won't Be Asked

Before an Indian fifteen-year-old reads a line of Anton Chekhov, they are asked to fill in a table. The columns are printed for them: customs similar to Indian ones, customs different from Indian ones. Above the table sits a short article about Russian weddings — the groom climbing an apartment block's stairs, answering a riddle at each landing — and then the question of whether Indian and Russian weddings have any customs in common, to be worked out with a partner.

Only then does the play begin: Lomov arrives at Chubukov's house in a dress-suit to propose to Natalya, and the three of them quarrel about a meadow and a dog until the proposal is nearly lost. The unit ends with a group discussion of the qualities one should look for in a marriage partner, under headings that include attitude to money and wealth.

The errand is written on the page before the work starts: a Russian farce about two landowning families, brought into an Indian classroom to be set beside an Indian wedding, in ruled columns, by a child with a partner.

The door is a book, and a subtraction

India's window has an unusual hinge. The Central Board of Secondary Education issues a curriculum for each subject code, and the last page of that curriculum carries a list of prescribed books and a note naming the lessons from which no questions will be asked. The books themselves are published by the National Council of Educational Research and Training. The board that examines names the anthology, and then it subtracts.

The English syllabus prints its chapter list in full, prose and poems, book by book. The Hindi syllabi do not. Hindi Course A names Kshitij Part 2 and Kritika Part 2 and stops — then names, from Kritika, only the lessons it removes, one of them a satire called "George the Fifth's Nose". The only Kritika chapters the syllabus names are the ones it takes away.

And nothing the lists name is marked to be learned by heart — not a title, not an excerpt, not a stated quantity of texts. What the English paper asks for is an extract and a short written answer.

Streams in one corridor

A school's window here is not one window. In the same Class 10 corridor a pupil may sit Hindi Course A — mother-tongue Hindi, with Kshitij and Kritika — or Hindi Course B, with Sparsh and Sanchayan; English Language and Literature is a separate code with its own two readers. Same board, different worlds.

The shelf in view here is the English stream's thirty-one chapters and the twelve of Kshitij — forty-three works, every one Class 10 and prescribed. Hindi Course B stays shut, all but the last page of its syllabus. That page comes later.

What comes through the English door

Mostly, the English-speaking world itself. The syllabus says so in its own objectives: the course sets out to develop appreciation of world literature — and, in the same sentence, scopes that world to varieties of English. Of the forty-three works in view, only four come from a language that is neither English nor Indian: a Mexican story from Spanish, a Dutch diary, a Russian one-act play, a French short story — López y Fuentes, Anne Frank, Chekhov, Maupassant. The split is cleanest in poetry. Every poem in the English reader comes from abroad, in English — seven of the ten American, the others Welsh, Australian, Irish — and no Indian poet holds a poetry slot there. The Indian poetry is next door, in Hindi, where Surdas and Tulsidas open Kshitij and everything is domestic.

Chekhov, twice

One writer walks through two doors of the same school.

Through the English door, Chekhov is a chapter with a page number: First Flight's last unit, printed on the contents page, the one author in the book who wrote in Russian, his surname spelled Chekov there. The reader's own blurb explains the farce to the class as a study in wealthy families courting other wealthy families, marrying their estates together for sound economic reasons. He is examinable: the paper draws extracts from drama and prose and short answers from First Flight.

Then the unit closes, and the last page is addressed to the teacher rather than the child. It reports what has been done — the class has been given a play by the famous Russian writer Anton Chekhov — and then proposes a game: dictate the life of another writer, and let the students guess his name. The paragraph that follows has a hole where the name should be. Its subject was born at Stratford-on-Avon, married a woman older than himself, retired, died, and left behind a body of work that "still stands as a pinnacle in world literature".

The one Russian in the book is handed over, and the class is invited to guess the Englishman. The exercise assumes recognition: it withholds the name because it expects the name back. That is what a shared shelf looks like from inside — not a text on the syllabus, but a riddle whose answer is assumed to be in the room already.

Through the Hindi B door, Chekhov appears exactly once, and not as a chapter. On the last page of that curriculum, under the note saying questions will not be asked from the following lessons, in the block for the prescribed reader Sparsh Part 2, between Bihari's couplets and a Mahadevi Varma poem, stands Anton Chekhov's name in Devanagari beside a story called "Girgit" — and in brackets after it, the whole lesson.

Whether today's reprint of Sparsh still prints the story is an open question; the naming is not. In one stream Chekhov is an examinable text with a warm-up table about weddings; in the stream across the corridor, his name is printed for the sole purpose of being fenced off the paper.

Underneath the syllabus

There is a layer below the syllabus, and it is where some of the world actually lives. In the exercises after "A Tiger in the Zoo" a second poem is printed whole — Rilke's "The Panther," in Stephen Mitchell's English — set beside Leslie Norris's tiger. It is not a chapter and not a listed text; it sits there anyway, entire, in the exercises.

The layer runs the other way too. Inside the Chekhov unit, the writing exercise hands the class a passage from Tagore's The Home and the World to turn into a script — Bimala demanding that foreign goods be cleared out of the market, her husband refusing: "To tyrannise for the country is to tyrannise over the country." A swadeshi argument — the home-made against the foreign-made — in the middle of the one foreign-language play in the book. The anthology put them on facing pages.

The shelf closes on paperwork. Footprints Without Feet still tells its teachers, in a preface, that it represents writers from across the world — Indian, British, French, American and Japanese. Its nine chapters here are Indian, British, French and American; the Japanese writer the preface promises is not among them. And the English reader's last printed page, after Chekhov and after Shakespeare's unnamed biography, is the Fundamental Rights of the Constitution of India, including the right of minorities to conserve their language, script and culture.

The lesson that won't be asked

A teacher going down the contents page with a pen — not this one, not this one — performs the only editorial act in a school year that a child witnesses directly: the shelf shrinking in front of the class, for reasons nobody explains, shortly before the syllabus becomes real.

India's documents perform that act in writing, at the bottom of the page, in a box, and the box promises a chapter a strange afterlife: still printed, still in the bag, weightless. In the syllabus that governed Class 10 in 2024-25, one such name in the Hindi B box is Anton Chekhov's. One corridor away, in English — in a reader still printed today — the same writer arrives with a warm-up table about weddings.
