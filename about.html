Method

How this book was built

Whole literatures cannot be a corpus. Lists can. The unit of analysis in this book is the official list — the document through which a school system tells its classrooms what a fifteen-year-old can be examined on.

The corpus

For each of twenty school systems, one defining document of one named recent year was located, saved, and transcribed into a table: an exam codifier, a ministry program, a state-anthology table of contents, an exam board's set-text list, or — where a system names nothing — the curricular regulation that declines to name. One row per work: author, title, origin (domestic or translated, from which language), the force of the row (mandate, menu, anthology entry, open formula), and whether the document requires the text to be learned by heart. Every row cites its source document, and every table went through an adversarial confirmation pass against the saved document; rows that could not be receipted were removed. Three tables — Belgium, Switzerland, Norway — are empty by their documents' own design, which is itself a finding.

What a row can and cannot prove

A row proves that the named document gives a work or a category the stated place. It does not prove that a class read the book, how it was taught, or why a ministry chose it — a list proves admission, never motive. Rows of unlike force are never compared as "openness": a named mandate, a teacher menu, an anthology entry, and an open formula are different objects, and each table is a one-year slice of one document, not a portrait of a national classroom. Where a chapter says a number, that number exists in the tables; where it reads a purpose, it reads it from the document's own teaching language.

Who wrote this

The corpus was gathered, transcribed, confirmed, and drafted by AI agents; every chapter was audited by a separate adversarial model against the tables and the saved documents, and the whole was read and ruled on by one human reader — the first reader, a product of one of the school systems in the book. The narrator memorized nobody's poem and sat in no country's classroom, and the book is written from that outside seat.
