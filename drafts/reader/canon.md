# There Is No World Canon

This book went looking for the world's reading list — the common floor under the
differences, the few books every school system hands its fifteen-year-olds because no
system can do without them. Twenty tables later, here is the closest thing that floor
has to a resident. No name crosses more borders than William Shakespeare: seven of
the seventeen populated tables admit him as a foreigner. And no two of the seven
admit the same Shakespeare. Bolivia's official textbook prints an adapted Hamlet
fragment credited, by its own heading, to an author named Hamlet William Shakespeare.
Kazakhstan mandates the sonnets, an excerpt of them marked for memorization, and
lists Romeo and Juliet on a choice menu. Ukraine sets one sonnet for memory, a choice
between 116 and 130. The American exemplar appendix offers Macbeth and one sonnet,
number 73. Vietnam's menu holds Romeo and Juliet. New South Wales lists six different
plays for its senior electives. Russia meets him three times on the staircase of its
five-year program: sonnets and Romeo and Juliet at fourteen, Hamlet at fifteen. Seven
admissions, and no shared assignment among them: the most-admitted author in these
tables produces no common book.

One sentence of method, before the conclusion that depends on it: a row in these
tables proves that a document admitted a name — not that a classroom read it — and
the rows are too unlike in force to rank countries as open or closed. What rows allow
is comparison, and the comparison is this chapter.

## The summit of the shared shelf

Drop from authors to works and the ceiling drops further. Exactly one work in the
current tables reaches four of the seventeen: Gulliver's Travels. Australia offers it
whole on a choice list. Bolivia prints it in the anthology. Ukraine requires Part One
— Lilliput only — at fifteen. Russia requires chapters by choice, at twelve. Four
admissions in four administrative shapes — a menu option, an anthology entry, one
island of the four, an open handful of chapters. If there is a world canon, this is
it: a satire about a man repeatedly washed up in countries built to the wrong scale,
admitted by four of seventeen systems and held differently by each.

Nothing reaches five. One step down, in three tables each, sit A Doll's House, The
Overcoat, Romeo and Juliet, Hamlet, Robinson Crusoe, and the Odyssey — and even there
the placements split: Ukraine's Doll's House is mandatory where Australia's and
America's are options; Russia's Odyssey is fragments; Ukraine's Overcoat is one side
of an either/or. The summit of the shared shelf, measured across the seventeen
populated tables, is one eighteenth-century satire and a handful of books that recur
in three. Everything else — hundreds of admissions, from Aitmatov in Vietnam to Daoud
in New South Wales — belongs to one or two tables and no more.

## The office that mostly stands empty

The premise had a second half: that even if the imports differed, every country would
at least staff the same office — one national poet, non-exportable, a Shevchenko or a
Pushkin or a Goethe in every system, the same chair with a different portrait. The
documents mostly leave the chair unfilled. Twelve of the seventeen single no one out:
India's reader gives every named domestic author exactly one row; Japan's rows give
Bashō one, like everyone else; the American appendix's domestic peak is shared
between Abraham Lincoln and Martin Luther King. Where the office does show, it shows
five ways. Belarus gives Yanka Kupala six domestic rows, one of them marked to be
learned by heart. Russia's program keeps Pushkin barely ahead of every other domestic
name. Kazakhstan's table centers Abai less by his own rows than by rows about him —
On Abai's Land, Path of Abai, Great Poet of the Kazakhs — a poet present mostly as a
subject. Ukraine bundles five Shevchenko works into a single row, the largest
one-author bundle on its domestic shelf. And on the English exam board this book
reads, the chair belongs to Shakespeare himself: six rows on the AQA lists, twice his
nearest rival — in the one populated table with no translated-coded row at all. The
most-admitted foreigner in the corpus holds his one domestic chair on the board where
nothing is coded foreign.

## The memory column

Admission is the shallow register; the deep one is memory — the texts a document does
not merely assign but orders learned by heart. Sixteen of the twenty tables order no
named text into memory at all. Four do, and no two of their memory columns face the
same direction. Russia's four by-heart works are all its own: the Igor Tale,
Lomonosov's ode of 1747, Woe from Wit, and Dead Souls, the by-heart instruction
written into the named work. Kazakhstan's twelve are all foreign-coded — eleven
Russian, plus that excerpt of Shakespeare's sonnets. Ukraine's five are all foreign
and none of them Russian: Heine, Dante, Petrarch, Shakespeare, Eminescu. Belarus
alone signs both — four of its own, two Russian-coded. And across all twenty-seven
by-heart rows, one title repeats: Griboyedov's Woe from Wit, the by-heart instruction
attached to the named play in Russia, a single monologue of it in Belarus.
Shakespeare's sonnets brush past each other in Kazakhstan and Ukraine without the
tables naming the same sonnet. That is the entire overlap. Even here, at the deepest
register these documents have, there is no common floor — only directions: inward,
toward the neighbor, toward everywhere but the neighbor, and both ways at once.

## Five Gogols

One writer runs through all of this like a dye through water. Nikolai Gogol stands in
five of the tables, and no two of them hold the same man. Russia requires Dead Souls
as a domestic classic and writes reading by heart into the row. Belarus mandates The
Overcoat under its program's heading of Russian Literature. Kazakhstan's program
lists The Overcoat as foreign literature and marks its passages for memorization.
Ukraine keeps him behind the door of its Foreign Literature subject, as a choice —
The Government Inspector or The Overcoat — with a note explaining that the
Russian-language work is retained because of the author's Ukrainian origin. The
American appendix suggests The Nose. The same man, five placements: a domestic
classic on the memory list, a neighbor's mandate, a foreign-coded import memorized by
excerpt, a Russian-language work retained with a note on the author's Ukrainian
origin, a suggestion.

No work in these tables reaches five systems. The national-poet chair mostly stands
empty, and where it is filled, it is filled five different ways. The four memory
columns face four different directions, and the one author admitted almost everywhere
arrives nowhere the same. Given the same world literature to draw on, the seventeen
populated documents drew seventeen different worlds. There is no world canon in them.
There are only windows — and held up to the same man, five of them give back five
different Gogols, and not one common book.
