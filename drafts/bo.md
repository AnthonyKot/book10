# Bolivia: A Cast, Not a Reading List

The last thing Bolivia's literature unit asks a fourth-year secondary pupil to do is not
read anything. It is to put on a play about a period:

> Inspired by the study of literary currents, let us stage a short dramatization about one
> of them.
>
> • In the dramatization, present an author and their literary production.
> — *Texto de aprendizaje*, 4th year of secondary, p. 29

So somebody has to be the author. Four pages earlier the book has printed, for the
eighteenth century, five names one to a line, each with a country stamped after it like a
passport:

> Jean de la Fontaine. France. "Fables"
> Félix María Samaniego. Spain. "Fables"
> Daniel Defoe. Spain. "Robinson Crusoe"
> Jonathan Swift. Ireland. "Gulliver's Travels" Kant.
> Germany. "Critique of Pure Reason"
> — p. 25, translated; the line breaks are the book's own

Two of those lines are worth stopping on. Daniel Defoe is Spanish here. And Kant is
standing in a line of literary works, holding the *Critique of Pure Reason*.

Read those five lines next to that staging task — and the pairing is this essay's, not
something the document states — and Bolivia's window stops looking like a shelf of books
to be read and starts looking like a company of foreigners available to be played.

## The door is an object

Most doors in this corpus are lists: a codifier of what an examiner may ask, a
specification of set texts, a ministry program naming units. Bolivia's door is a book. One
physical volume, the learning text for the fourth year of secondary education, marked
"official text 2024" above the Minister of Education's name, and at the foot of the same
page, in capitals: *the sale of this document is prohibited*. What the imprint establishes
is that rule about sale — not how copies reach anyone, not that any classroom opened them.

It is also everything at once. The same covers carry the endocrine glands at page 216 and
the War of the Pacific at page 408. The world-literature material this corpus codes runs
from page 25 to page 36 — five pages of tables and a few pages of extracts.

How hard does this door close? The honest answer is that the corpus cannot say. Every one
of the twenty-nine rows coded from the volume is `anthology-only`; not one is coded to an
exam, not one carries a memorization mandate. The book disclaims itself in its own
foreword, calling its contents an element that energizes learning, one that "can always be
widened, deepened and contextualized from the experience and reality of each cultural,
social and educational setting" (p. 5). And it points upward to an instrument this corpus
does not hold: its contents follow the Plans and Programs established for each educational
level. There is a door above this door, and it was not retrieved — so the force of what
follows is not light, it is unknown. A name printed here is present. It is not thereby
required.

## The names, and how they are labelled

The unit sits under a heading that would be hard to invent: *Promotion of gender equity in
harmony with Mother Earth in the study of literary currents* (p. 24). Beneath it, six
numbered sections covering the currents, printing seven ruled tables between them —
section 4 gives Realism and Naturalism a table each. Each table's final row is headed
**"Emblematic works and representative authors,"** and twenty of the twenty-five foreign
rows in this corpus are names harvested from those cells. That heading is the instruction
for how to read them: *emblematic*. A name there is not a book assigned; it is offered as
evidence that a period existed.

Read the tables in order and they walk in a direction. Neoclassicism is filed as France,
eighteenth century; Romanticism as Germany, nineteenth; Costumbrismo as Spain; Realism as
France, Spain, England, Mexico; Naturalism as France (pp. 25–28). All arriving from across
the Atlantic. Then the compass swings west — Indigenismo, "Latin America, twentieth
century" — and the last section says out loud what the swing means:

> It stands out from the rest because it was born for the first time in America and is not
> an imitation of any other current. On the contrary, its influence reached Europe from the
> Americas. — p. 29

Modernismo is the one current the book describes as born in the Americas and exported. And
it is in those last two tables, and in none of the five before them, that a Bolivian name
stands as an equal instance of the same thing as a foreign one: Ricardo Jaimes Freyre
beside Rubén Darío and José Martí; Alcides Arguedas and Jesús Lara beside Ciro Alegría of
Peru. For five currents the country is the audience. For two it is a member of the company.

Two entries are labelled in ways the document never notices. Defoe, tagged Spain. And
Andrés Bello, sole representative author of Costumbrismo — the current about local custom —
introduced not by a novel but as "Simón Bolívar's teacher," holding a treatise on the law
of nations (p. 26). The corpus codes that row as an essay. Whatever a pupil presenting
Costumbrismo would have to present, it is closer to a founding than to a fiction.

One more thing about this company, set against the heading over its head. Of the
twenty-five foreign rows, twenty-four name a man and one names no author at all. The only
woman named anywhere among these twenty-nine rows is Bolivian, and she stands five pages
past the last table, outside the currents entirely — Mónica Paulina Gutiérrez Jiménez,
credited on p. 34 for a free adaptation of an Ayoreo legend printed under the heading
*spelling workshop*. The table records placement. It does not record why.

## Shakespeare comes on loan

If you want to see the mechanism at once, look at what happens when the book stops naming
authors and prints one. On p. 30 a *Hamlet* fragment appears under an instruction that the
class reads it "with expressiveness" — one of the best-known texts in the history of world
theatre, the book says. The heading above it reads, exactly as printed, *"(Adapted
fragment) Author: Hamlet William Shakespeare."* The source line credits not an edition of
the play but a 2019 web article about what dramatic texts are, on a popular psychology
site. Two of the questions underneath are: have you heard of William Shakespeare, and do
you find the situation sad or funny?

Adapted, mis-headed, and sourced from an article about dramatic form rather than from the
play. The world's literature arrives on this page to be recognized and read out, and the
corpus records no edition behind it.

## The errand: a scruple, six pages later

The clearest imported feeling in this table is not on the literature pages at all. It is on
p. 36, under a heading about the importance of radio media in the struggle against social
inequality, where an extract from José Ignacio López Vigil's *Passion for Radio* argues
against treating a microphone as a weapon:

> For these chiefs, the microphones were cannons that fired ideas […] A sad confusion of
> levels. Because on military ground one wins. But on the ground of ideas one persuades.
> — p. 36

He keeps going: radio in the service of big capital, he writes, bombards us daily with news
that misinforms — "they practise media terrorism. But we cannot and do not want to play
that same game, even in reverse. For ethical reasons."

Where the tables offer periods, this page offers a scruple: the difference between winning
and persuading, set in front of a fourth-year pupil as a working rule. And the reflection
question printed under the last table asks the same kind of thing of the literature — pick
a current and a present-day problem, and say how we would contribute to solving it
"through the literature of that current" (p. 29). Not what the literature is like. What it
can be put to.

## The object

Some readers were handed a textbook and some were sold one, and those are two different
childhoods. If you were handed yours — stamped, unsellable, one volume for the whole
subject — you know that such a book is not a shelf but a fact, the way a room is a fact.
Page 25 of Bolivia's official text for the fourth year sets out the literature of the world
as a line of names with countries after them: a Frenchman with fables, an Irishman with a
giant's country, a German philosopher holding the *Critique of Pure Reason* as though it
were a novel, an Englishman wearing Spain's flag by a printer's slip. Four pages on, the
unit asks for a dramatization presenting an author and their literary production.

The document never says those names are the cast for that play. It prints them, and it
prints the task, and the pages invite the comparison.

## Limits

- **An anthology is not a mandate, and its force here is unknown.** All 29 rows are
  `anthology-only`; nothing shows that any Bolivian pupil must read, or can be examined on,
  any of these works. The volume defers to the Plans and Programs for each level, and those
  are not in `resources/` — so the door's force is undetermined rather than demonstrably
  light. No row count above is offered as a measure of openness, and none is comparable
  with a codifier or exam-specification country's.
- **Distribution and use are outside the evidence.** The imprint prohibits sale. It does
  not establish that copies are free, how they reach pupils, that any pupil possesses one,
  or that a teacher ever taught these pages. Nothing above should be read as describing a
  Bolivian classroom.
- **The coded slice is not proof of the volume's whole literary content.** Pages 25–36 hold
  the world-literature material this corpus codes; no exhaustive search of the 476-page
  volume was made, and other material is identified as literary elsewhere (p. 11 introduces
  a piece labelled a literary essay).
- **One volume, one year, one grade.** Fourth year of secondary, 2024 edition. No reform
  history is receipted — the corpus holds no earlier Bolivian volume.
- **One row's origin is uncertain.** The playlet about litter (p. 32) is coded
  `translated:spanish`, but its own note records that the document supplies neither author
  nor original language; it could be domestic. The 25/4 split and the 24-men count include
  it.
- **`subject_era` is the compiler's reading**, recoded six times in reconciliation; the
  groupings above rest on it, and it is not a ministry category.
- **The cast-list reading is this essay's.** The document prints the emblematic-works cells
  and, four pages later, sets a dramatization task; it never says the listed authors are
  the cast for it, that a pupil must choose among them, or that performance rather than
  reading is why any name was included.
- **Motive is absent.** The gender-equity heading over a company of twenty-four men, the
  country tags that diverge from the authors, the compass swing at Indigenismo and
  Modernismo — all are facts of what is printed. The document offers no rationale, and none
  is inferred here.
- **Length.** About 2,520 words by `wc -w` against the 2,200 cap; the story through the
  lander runs about 1,600. The overrun is the Spanish-originals block, which the
  English-first rule moves out of the body into Receipts — evidence relocated, not added.
