# Vietnam: A Bookcase, and Eight Names for the World

Before it says anything about foreign literature, Vietnam's national program for Ngữ văn —
the single subject that carries the Vietnamese language and its literature — furnishes the
room. The minimum equipment of the subject, it says, is «tủ sách tham khảo», a reference
bookcase, holding stories, poetry, drama, reportage, argument, picture books. It also wants
«Một số tranh ảnh như chân dung các nhà văn lớn có trong chương trình» — some pictures, such
as portraits of the major writers in the program — and pictures illustrating «một số tác
phẩm lớn như Nam quốc sơn hà, Hịch tướng sĩ, Bình Ngô đại cáo, Truyện Kiều, Văn tế nghĩa sĩ
Cần Giuộc, Tuyên ngôn Độc lập,...». Six titles, offered as examples, after a *such as* and
before an ellipsis: the document sets no number of pictures and no place to hang them. At the
desks it wants whole books — teachers are to limit teaching by extract, and to give pupils
the chance «đọc trực tiếp và trọn vẹn những tác phẩm được chọn học», to read the works chosen
for study directly and entire.

So furnish the room yourself, because the document won't. Put the bookcase where you like and
the pictures wherever you last saw pictures in a school. The six titles under them are not
arbitrary: the same six appear earlier in the program as its «tác phẩm bắt buộc», its
compulsory works, the only literature it obliges both a textbook's author and a teacher to
carry out. An anonymous poem from the Lý dynasty; Trần Quốc Tuấn's address to his officers;
Nguyễn Trãi's great proclamation; *Truyện Kiều*; Nguyễn Đình Chiểu's funeral oration for the
fighters of Cần Giuộc; and Hồ Chí Minh's declaration of independence. This table codes four
of the six to an invasion or an occupation — Song, Mongol, Ming, French — and a fifth to the
end of a world war.

The rest of the world's literature is in this room too. It is not among the six, and on the
day the program was signed it was not yet a book. It was one line on page 18.

## The line

> «– Văn học nước ngoài, chọn ít nhất 1 tác phẩm cho mỗi nền văn học sau: Anh, Pháp, Mĩ,
> Hy Lạp, Nga, Nhật Bản, Trung Quốc, Ấn Độ.» — "Foreign literature: choose at least one work
> for each of the following literatures: English, French, American, Greek, Russian, Japanese,
> Chinese, Indian." (my translation)

Eight literatures. A floor of one work apiece. No titles, no authors, no centuries — eight
names, and not one book.

The two lines above it are built the other way round. Vietnamese folk literature gets five
categories with minimums: at least four folk-tale genres, at least three ca dao, at least one
epic, one ethnic-minority verse narrative, one chèo or tuồng script. Vietnamese written
literature gets thirteen names — Nguyễn Trãi, Nguyễn Du, Hồ Xuân Hương, Nguyễn Đình Chiểu,
Nguyễn Khuyến, Nguyễn Ái Quốc–Hồ Chí Minh, Nam Cao, Vũ Trọng Phụng, Xuân Diệu, Tố Hữu,
Nguyễn Tuân, Nguyễn Huy Tưởng, Lưu Quang Vũ. Then, in the same tier, in the same grammar,
foreign literature gets eight countries. At home the program guarantees particular writers.
Abroad it guarantees only the literatures a work must come from. Nothing in the program
explains the difference, and a list cannot be made to give a reason for itself.

Nor is the floor a promise of space. The program asks for a suitable ratio «giữa văn học Việt
Nam và văn học nước ngoài, giữa Đông và Tây» — between Vietnamese and foreign literature,
between East and West — and then defines its own word against the obvious reading of it:
«“Sự cân đối” được hiểu là một tỉ lệ thích hợp, chứ không phải có tỉ lệ bằng nhau» —
"'Balance' is understood as a suitable proportion, not an equal one." A ministry writing down
that a quota is a floor and not a scale.

## Whose job is a name

Take one of the eight names — «Nga», Russia — and follow it toward the bookcase. Somebody has
to turn it into a page. The program is unusually clear about who, because it grades its own
force in a single sentence: «tác phẩm bắt buộc (tác giả sách giáo khoa và giáo viên bắt buộc
thực hiện theo quy định của chương trình); tác phẩm bắt buộc lựa chọn (tác giả sách giáo khoa
bắt buộc lựa chọn tác phẩm của tác giả có tên trong danh mục quy định của chương trình); tác
phẩm gợi ý lựa chọn (tác giả sách giáo khoa tự lựa chọn tác phẩm theo danh mục gợi ý của
chương trình)».

Three tiers, and the addressee changes as you go down. Compulsory works bind the textbook
author *and* the teacher — but «theo quy định của chương trình», as the program provides, and
the program provides by grade, so what binds any particular teacher is whatever the program
has placed in the grades that teacher teaches. Compulsory-selection entries, the eight names
among them, bind the *tác giả sách giáo khoa*, the textbook author, and only that person.
Suggested works bind nobody at all.

This is the door, and it is a door onto a desk rather than onto a classroom. The circular that
promulgates the program — 32/2018/TT-BGDĐT, signed by the Minister of Education and Training
on 26 December 2018 and in force from 15 February 2019 — issues its foreign-literature
instruction not to a country's teachers but to whoever is compiling a book for them. That is
the document this essay reads: a multi-grade national program as promulgated, not a syllabus
for a named school year. The word «Nga» lands on somebody's desk. The teacher will meet it
later, bound into a volume, as a story with a title, and the program that required it will be
nowhere in sight.

## The back of the book

Suppose the person holding the name looks for help. The program's own suggestions are at the
back, in section IX, grouped by genre and then «xếp theo thứ tự A, B, C» — alphabetically, by
the Vietnamese title. The author's name sits in brackets after the title. Nothing in the menu
says which entry answers which of the eight names; a few entries carry an origin instead of an
author, so that *Herakles đi tìm táo vàng* is bracketed «Thần thoại Hy Lạp», Greek mythology,
where the next line carries a surname. But the two halves of the document are never joined:
the quota names literatures and the menu names books, and no line runs between them.

The menu also rations its authors: «Các tác giả có tên ở danh mục này chỉ xuất hiện một lần
trong cả ba cấp học, trừ một số tác giả tác phẩm bắt buộc đã nêu trong chương trình» — the
authors named in this list appear only once across all three levels of schooling, except for
some authors of the compulsory works already named in the program. One line each in twelve
years, and the exemption is reserved for the authors of the six compulsory works, all six of
which are Vietnamese.

The list also declines to be a schedule: «Danh mục văn bản này không phải là tất cả ngữ liệu
của các lớp mà chỉ là những ví dụ minh hoạ […]» — "This list of texts is not all the material
for the grades, only illustrative examples." The same page lets textbook authors go outside it
altogether for equivalent texts, and lets teachers and pupils add extended reading of their
own. Section V puts the same thought as a principle: «Chương trình có định hướng mở về ngữ
liệu» — the program has an open orientation as to materials.

So in the grade 10–12 fiction list, sorted by first letter of the Vietnamese title, Hemingway
sits between *Odysseus* and *Số đỏ*, and nowhere does the document say that he is the American
one. Twenty-one entries naming a foreign author or a foreign origin appear in the two bands
this corpus covers — by source language, six English, five French, four Chinese, two Greek,
two Russian, one Japanese, one Spanish. An entry is not the same thing as a work: La
Fontaine's line prints two fables, Lu Xun's prints three, and *Sherlock Holmes* stands in for
a shelf. Not one of the entries is matched to a slot. Eight names on page 18, twenty-one
entries scattered from page 101 to page 106, and no line drawn between the two halves of the
document by anyone but the reader.

Two edges of the list make the point sharply. *Trăm năm cô đơn* is printed like everything
else, a title and a bracketed «G. Marquez» — and it is the one named foreign entry in this
table whose source language, Spanish, answers to none of the eight names. And Ấn Độ is the one
name of the eight for which the grades this table covers offer no named example at all. A
suggestion matching no requirement; a requirement with no suggestion.

## Two teachers

The two Russian-language stories in the menu are printed as flatly as the rest — «– Người
thầy đầu tiên (C. Aitmatov)» and «– Người trong bao (A. Chekhov)», *The First Teacher* and
*The Man in a Case*. That both are Russian-language is this table's coding of source language
and nothing more; the document says neither that either answers to «Nga» nor that anything
else does. The coding will not even carry a nationality: Chinghiz Aitmatov was Kyrgyz and
wrote in both Kyrgyz and Russian. In this list a foreign author is an initial, a surname and
a bracket — unhoused.

The titles already rhyme before anyone opens the books. One names a teacher; the other names
a man who has put himself away. Open them and the rhyme holds. Chekhov's Belikov teaches
Greek and keeps every part of himself in a case, and his fear settles over a whole town.
Aitmatov's Duishen is the opposite man and the opposite failure — a demobilised soldier with
barely more schooling than his pupils, who turns a stable into a classroom, carries children
across an icy stream on his back, and teaches from almost nothing.

Now look at what surrounds them on the same pages. The grade 10–12 Vietnamese fiction in this
table is heavy with the wars: Anh Đức, Nguyễn Thi, Phan Tứ, Nguyễn Minh Châu, Bảo Ninh,
Nguyễn Khải — six entries whose recorded subject matter is the anti-US war or its aftermath.
Those subject codings are this corpus's description of what each work represents, not the
ministry's statement of what it is for, and the two Russian-language stories carry no such
tag. What follows is therefore this essay's reading and not the document's: beside a domestic
shelf that keeps returning to the war, these two imports are both about a room with a teacher
in it, one of them a warning and one of them a debt. Whether that ever crossed anyone's mind
in Hà Nội, the circular does not say, and a list cannot be made to say it.

## The last instruction is the loosest

Come back to the room. There is one more thing the program asks a pupil to do with a book, and
from grade 6 to grade 12 it asks it in the same sentence, word for word: «Học thuộc lòng một
số đoạn thơ, bài thơ yêu thích trong chương trình» — "Learn by heart some favourite verse
passages or poems from the program."

Seven grades, seven identical lines. No work is named, no number is set, and the sentence does
not say who decides that a poem is «yêu thích», favourite — the pupil, the textbook, or the
teacher. The primary grades are written the other way. Grade 1 asks for «4 – 5 đoạn thơ hoặc
bài thơ đã học», four or five studied passages or poems, each about 30–40 chữ long; the figure
climbs grade by grade to «ít nhất 10 – 12 đoạn thơ, bài thơ hoặc đoạn văn đã học», at least
ten to twelve, of about 100 chữ, in grade 5. The counting stops when primary school does. What
replaces it is the favourite.

That is why every row in this table reads `no` under memorization, and the coding is narrower
than it looks: the reconciliation rule required the document to name a text, or a quantity
tied to one, and a grade-wide instruction to learn some favourite poems supplies neither. The
column records that no work in these 107 rows carries a memorization mandate of its own. It
does not record that nothing is learned by heart in Vietnam; learning by heart is itself one
of the outcomes the program requires.

That is the shape of this window, top to bottom. Six compulsory works, named twice over —
once as a duty and once as a picture — binding a textbook's author and, grade by grade, a
teacher. Eight names for the rest
of the world, addressed to a person with a manuscript. Twenty-one entries the document says
are only examples. And, at the end of the chain, an instruction to learn a poem by heart with
the poem left blank. What actually stood in that bookcase — which volume a teacher was handed,
whether the word «Nga» came out as Chekhov or as a novella about a man teaching in a stable —
is past the edge of this corpus.

The blank is the last thing this document does, and it is the same move it made on page 18:
require the thing, decline to name it. If you can still recite something from when you were
fifteen, there was a document behind it too, somewhere, with a blank in it — and somebody
filled that blank in. A ministry, an editor, a teacher, or you. The Vietnamese program says
the blank must be filled. It does not say by whom.

## Limits

- **The file is called `vn-2024`; the document is 2018, and the essay claims no more than
  that.** The annex is a single multi-grade national program, not a year's syllabus, and its
  promulgation and effective dates are receipted above. This corpus contains no amendment
  search, no rollout evidence and nothing establishing which parts of the program governed
  which grades in 2024. The essay therefore describes the annex as promulgated, and the
  one-year-slice caveat should be read against 2018.
- **Not a one-grade slice.** The book's operating definition — what a 15–16-year-old can be
  examined on — is only approximated here. The table's rows carry grade codings of `6-9`,
  `8-9`, `8-12`, `10-12` and `6-12`, and the section IX menu is published in bands (1–3, 4–5,
  6–7, 8–9, 10–12) of which this table captures the last two. Nothing above describes a
  single classroom's year.
- **The room is imagined; the equipment clause is not.** The bookcase, the portraits and the
  pictures are the program's minimum-equipment requirement at p. 91, which asks for «Một số
  tranh ảnh» without fixing a number or a place. The wall, the arrangement and the school are
  this essay's invitation to the reader. This corpus contains no evidence about what
  Vietnamese classrooms actually hold, and no claim is made about it.
- **The quota is not an exam list.** Vietnam's national school-leaving examination is not in
  this corpus, and `exam_status` here is the compiler's coding of the circular's tiers, not a
  statement of examinability. No count in this essay is offered for comparison with another
  country's table; the door types are not commensurable and row totals are not a measure of
  openness.
- **The obligation lands on a book, not a lesson.** The tiers are not one rule, and the top
  tier is not uniform either: compulsory works bind textbook authors *and* teachers «theo quy
  định của chương trình», which distributes them across grades. Compulsory-selection
  categories, the foreign quota among them, bind textbook authors; the section IX menu binds
  no one. What any pupil actually read therefore depends on a textbook this corpus does not
  contain, so the essay describes an available window and not a taught one. How many textbook
  series answer this program, and how a given book reached a given classroom, is outside this
  corpus; no such claim is made above.
- **`translated:english` covers two of the quota's eight.** The program lists Anh and Mĩ as
  separate literatures; the table's `origin` field records language, so British and American
  rows share a value. Which of the six English-language named entries would count against
  which slot is not stated in the document and is not inferred here. The same caution governs
  «Nga»: no work in the menu is identified by the document as answering that name, and the
  essay identifies none.
- **The era codings are repairs, not the document's words.** Circular 32/2018 is a
  competency curriculum: it gives no per-work teaching notes of the kind that let Ukraine's
  model programs be quoted against individual books. Every `subject_era` value is the
  compiler's reading of the work, which is why 33 of them were rewritten in reconciliation.
  Nine of those repairs fall on foreign rows: Saint-Exupéry and La Fontaine had carried their
  composition period as if it were their subject, O. Henry and Frost had both been coded
  `none/romance`, and the two quota rows for Greek and Indian literature had been coded
  `antiquity?` when the requirement names a national literature and no period at all. Treat
  that column as description of content, never as the ministry's stated purpose.
- **Memorization is coded conservatively, and that is a judgement.** The rule required a named
  text or a quantity tied to one; the grade 6–12 favourite-poems outcome supplies neither, so
  all 107 rows read `no`. The primary grades do set quantities, but they attach them to «đoạn
  thơ, bài thơ hoặc đoạn văn đã học» — passages already studied — and name no work either. Vietnamese
  classroom practice around recitation is real and is not measured by this column.
- **No motive anywhere.** The circular states requirements and criteria — including the pair
  at p. 16 that asks material to show «tinh thần yêu nước, độc lập dân tộc, ý thức về chủ
  quyền quốc gia» and, in the same sentence, «tinh thần hội nhập quốc tế, hướng đến những giá
  trị phổ quát của nhân loại» ("the spirit of patriotism, national independence, awareness of
  national sovereignty… the spirit of international integration, oriented toward the universal
  values of humanity"). It nowhere explains why these eight literatures and no others. The
  asymmetry between a named domestic canon and an unnamed foreign quota, and the juxtaposition
  of the compulsory war texts with the quota's Chinese, French and American slots, are facts
  about one document's contents and are offered as nothing more. Nothing above is a claim about
  what anyone intended, and no other country's document is invoked for comparison.
