# Australia: The Word Between Two Books

On a beach outside Algiers, a Frenchman shoots a man the novel never names. The book
calls him the Arab. He has a sister, a knife, and no line of dialogue; the rest of the
novel belongs to the shooter, tried less for the killing than for failing to weep at his
mother's funeral.

Year 12 students in New South Wales meet that beach — but only the ones whose class
picked the third pair on a state list, where two titles are stacked one above the other
with a single word printed between them: Camus's *The Stranger*, **AND** Kamel Daoud's
*The Meursault Investigation*. The second book gives
the dead man a name — Musa — and a brother who will not stop talking about him. Camus
died in 1960; the man who answered him was born ten years later. In a table of 140
prescribed texts, that is the one pairing I read as a book and its rebuttal both holding
a row — and a class that picks it studies the two together, in one module, because a
state exam authority set the word AND between them.

## The door

The document is the HSC English prescriptions for 2019 to 2026, issued by the NSW
Education Standards Authority and updated in May 2024. It is a state document, and that
is the first thing to say plainly: in Australia the enforceable named-text list belongs
to the state, not the federation. The HSC is a New South Wales credential; Victoria's
authority publishes its own annual text list for its own exam. What follows is New South
Wales standing in for a federation — the same finding-shape as Germany's Länder, where
the absence of one national list is not a gap in the evidence but the evidence.

Inside the state, the choosing is pushed down again. The foreword asks that teachers
selecting specific texts consider the ethos of the school and its local community. A row
in this table is not something a pupil will meet; it is something a pupil's teacher may
offer.

And there is an age line above this door worth drawing before anything is counted. At
fifteen — the age this book measures every other country at — Australia names no book.
The national curriculum for those years asks for categories: wide-ranging Australian and
world authors, First Nations texts, different cultures and times. The state syllabus adds
quantities — so many novels, so many films — and exactly one proper noun: at least one
Shakespeare play, title unnamed. The entire named canon of an Australian fifteen-year-old
is one surname. The 140-title menu opens two years later, in elective senior courses, for
the minority who have chosen literature as a path. What follows is not a generation's
canon but the specialists' shelf — and it happens to be the widest window in this book,
which is its own finding: the country that names nothing for everyone offers nearly
everywhere to those who ask.

The mechanics matter, because everything else depends on them. An
Advanced student closely studies four prescribed texts, one drawn from each of four
categories — Shakespearean drama, prose fiction, poetry or drama, and a fourth that may
be nonfiction, film or media. At least two more short texts come in for the writing
module, and those do not contribute to the required pattern. So the floor of the year is
four plus two: at least six rows out of 140. A Standard floor is five. The 140 is the
length of the menu, never of anyone's reading year, and a menu count and a mandate count
are not on the same axis.

The menu-door works like this. Every Standard, Advanced and English Studies pupil does
one common module, whose list holds fourteen titles, of which they study one: Orwell's
*Nineteen Eighty-Four*, Miller's *The Crucible*, Doerr's *All the Light We Cannot See*,
Shakespeare's *The Merchant of Venice*, Slessor's poems, Winton's essays, a
reality-television series about asylum seekers. Fourteen doors into one room; each pupil
walks through one; the other thirteen are somebody else's year. The Advanced comparison
module offers seven pairs and takes one. Extension 1 spreads thirty rows across five
electives with titles like "Literary homelands" and "Worlds of upheaval."

## What is on the shelf, and in what language

The subject is called English, and that name does most of the border work before any
title is chosen. Of the 140 rows, 55 are Australian; the other 85 arrive from elsewhere,
and 77 of those 85 were written in English to begin with. Adiga, Ishiguro, Lahiri, Levy,
Tóibín, Mistry, Zadie Smith, Colum McCann, Langston Hughes: not one needed a translator
to reach a Sydney desk.

Eight rows in the whole table come from another language. Kafka and Fritz Lang from
German, Camus and Daoud from French, Guevara and Guillermo del Toro from Spanish,
Calvino from Italian, Ibsen from Norwegian. Two of the eight are films. Six are print,
and two of those six are the Algiers pair — joined by that AND, in a module where
students choose a pair, so that within this menu a class selecting either French novel
studies both. The second, on my reading, is a prosecution of the first.

Against that, the door carries two written selection requirements, identical across the
Standard, Advanced and EAL/D lists. Across the senior years, the
selection of texts must give students experience of texts widely regarded as quality
literature, including a range of literary texts written about intercultural experiences
and the peoples and cultures of Asia; and of a range of Australian texts, including texts
by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander authors and texts giving insight into diverse
Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander experience. Neither clause carries a number.

Both leave marks on the table, and the marks are different shapes. Thirteen rows are
coded to a subject naming an Asian country, city or people: Adiga's Bangalore, Forster's
Raj, Ishiguro's postwar Japan, Mistry's Bombay, Thien's Cultural Revolution, Baillie's
Tiananmen, Gavron's British-Bangladeshi London, Lahiri's Indian-American crossing,
Yousafzai's Pakistan, Noonuccal's China poems, and the arrivals written by Nam Le, Alice
Pung and Lily Chan — five of the thirteen Australian books about Asian pasts and
Asian-Australian arrivals. Twelve
rows are coded to Indigenous Australia, and every one is Australian: Noonuccal, Ali Cobby
Eckermann, Tara June Winch, Melissa Lucashenko,
Rachel Perkins, Jane Harrison, Linda Burney, Noel Pearson among them. Asia sits on this
shelf as subject matter, written from both sides of the border; Indigenous Australia sits
on it as home. Nothing explains why the shapes differ; the table records placement, not
reasons.

One row bends the pattern hard enough to be worth stopping on. Oodgeroo Noonuccal appears
twice — the only author with two rows in that Indigenous count — her second entry a set of
seven poems: "The Past," then "China…Woman," "Reed Flute Cave," "Entombed Warriors,"
"Visit to Sun Yat-Sen Memorial Hall," "Sunrise on Huampu River," "A Lake Within a Lake."
Six of the seven come out of her 1984 visit to China. What those titles afford a pupil is
an Aboriginal poet set for study as a traveller — looking outward from the country, not
being looked at. The window opens the other way.

## The errand: what the pair affords

The comparison module has its own teaching language, and it is unusually candid about
what the comparing is meant to produce. Students explore how comparing texts can reveal
resonances and dissonances between them and within them, and consider the ways a
reimagining of one text might "mirror, align or collide" with the details of another.

*Mirror, align or collide.* Six of the seven pairs I read as chiefly mirroring: Al Pacino
documenting a staging of *Richard III*, Jane Campion filming Keats's last love, Stephen
Daldry filming Woolf writing *Mrs Dalloway*, Margaret Atwood rewriting *The Tempest*
inside a prison theatre — the canon in conversation with its own admirers. That grouping
is my reading; the document sorts no pair into a bucket.

The Algiers pair I read as a collision. Daoud's narrator is Musa's brother, older now,
drinking in Oran, addressing a stranger about a famous book in which his brother is a
corpse without a name. The novel he is angry at is the other novel on the same page of
the same list, in the same module, examined together.

What the pairing affords is not French existentialism, which Camus alone would supply. It
is the experience of reading a masterpiece and then reading the person it stepped over —
a canon being audited by the place it was set in. And the pupil who does that sits in a
state whose own prescribed shelf includes *The Secret River*, a stage adaptation about
dispossession on the Hawkesbury. Both books can go on one desk. That is what the list
allows; it is not what the list is *for*. The list proves the pairing and the module's
language proves the framing; no statement of intention for either appears anywhere in the
document.

## The book nobody prescribed

There is a second English course in this state, for students learning English as an
additional language or dialect, with its own shelf of 22 rows. Its module on language,
identity and culture centres explicit English study on the Australian vernacular, its
idioms and its colloquialisms. On that same page sit two rows worth reading side by side:
an anthology called *Contemporary
Asian Australian Poets*, and Langston Hughes — seven poems, including "Theme for English
B," in which a young Black student, the only one of his kind in the class, is told to go
home and write a page that will be true. A module built on the Australian vernacular
offers a pupil a poem about being asked, in an English class, to produce something honest
on demand.

Which is close to what the state asks of all of them. Every Standard, Advanced, English
Studies and EAL/D pupil must also study one related text of their own — in the common
module for the first three, in the first module for the last. The document names no list
for it, no category, no title.

So the true end of this 140-row table is not a row. In a document that specifies
publishers, editions and ISBNs, and that pauses to record how many pages one Wordsworth
edition runs to, the one textual slot required in each of these four courses is the one
the document declines to name.

You had a last year of school once, with a list someone had chosen before you walked in.
If your classroom ever let you bring a text of your own in beside the prescribed one —
what did you bring, and did anyone ask you why? A man on a beach outside Algiers got a
name because a novelist in Oran wrote it down; a class in Sydney may read both books at
one desk, because a state authority printed the word AND between them.

What this chapter can't know: this is one state's list for one year of schooling — New
South Wales, not Australia, with Victoria and Queensland setting their own texts
elsewhere — so read every sentence above about Australia as the New South Wales door.
Nothing on this shelf is a book a pupil must read; what is mandatory is a pattern of
categories filled from menus, at least six rows of the 140 for an Advanced candidate, and
every sentence here about a class reading the Algiers pair describes something a class
*may* do. The groupings — Asia, Indigenous Australia, mirroring against collision — are
readings offered for inspection rather than categories the authority assigns.
