Latin Doors · Chapter 08

Brazil: Nine Books and a Door You Have to Want

The guide that names Brazil's nine obligatory books greets its reader twice, once in each gender: the same Portuguese noun printed a second time with its ending changed, feminine and then masculine: the guide refuses to greet only half its readers. And the noun is not pupil. A vestibulando is somebody standing outside a university, trying to get in. You have in your hands, the greeting says, your Journey Guide.

Deep in the same book, nine titles are printed under Leituras Obrigatórias, Obligatory Readings — what the journey costs in reading. Gonzaga's Marília de Dirceu. Machado's Quincas Borba. Graciliano Ramos's Angústia. Drummond's Alguma Poesia. Pessoa's Mensagem. Honwana's Nós matamos o cão tinhoso! Guimarães Rosa's Campo Geral. Cecília Meireles's Romanceiro da Inconfidência. Milton Hatoum's Dois irmãos.

No national mandate is in evidence; nothing here obliges a Brazilian to read them. It obliges a route: whoever sits the FUVEST vestibular for a place at the University of São Paulo reads the nine.

Whose door this is

Most doors in this book are the state's — a codifier, a national program, a state anthology, reaching into a compulsory classroom to tell a fifteen-year-old what will be on the paper. This one belongs to a university. FUVEST runs the entrance exam, the vestibular, for USP, and the guide states its legal footing plainly: exam and guides alike rest on a 2023 resolution of the university's own council. One state, one institution, one year — and not even all of USP's doors, since the same guide points to two other ways in, and the nine belong to neither.

That does not shrink the list's force; it relocates it. The candidate is not a captive audience but a volunteer; the reading is not homework but a toll. The exam runs in two rounds: a day of multiple-choice questions, then, for whoever clears the bar, a second phase opening with written-answer questions in Portuguese — on text interpretation, grammar and literature — and an essay.

Two lists sit in the same chapter, and they do not weigh the same. The syllabus offers a repertoire and hedges it as it does so: the forty-eight readings it names are examples among others it never names — not a menu and not a fence. The other list is the nine, and its sentence carries no hedge at all: the works of obligatory reading for the 2024 competition will be. Will be, for this exam, this year.

What the window holds

The window opens onto the language rather than the world. Every foreign work the guide names sits under a Portuguese-language heading: of its fifty-seven titles, thirty-seven are Brazilian, seventeen Portuguese, and three come from a shelf headed African literatures in the Portuguese language — defined by what it is written in, not by where it is from. No French shelf, no English, no Greek, no Russian; no translator, no edition, no language named for any single work. The heading overhead is the whole customs declaration.

The shelves are not equal even so. What the guide expects, where literary texts are concerned, is knowledge of the representative works of the different periods of Brazilian and Portuguese literature — the African heading is not in that sentence, and the lists underneath repeat the omission. Brazil gets a lettered run of periods, Baroque through contemporary; Portugal a longer one, from the medieval troubadours to Modernism. Africa gets no periods at all — two lines, Pepetela's Mayombe and José Luandino Vieira's Luuanda, two Angolan names and their titles with no century around them. Two of these shelves are given a history. The third is given authors.

Two lines of the nine

The obligatory list is where an African title becomes compulsory, and it is worth reading lines five and six in the order the guide prints them: Fernando Pessoa's Mensagem, and then Luís Bernardo Honwana's Nós matamos o cão tinhoso!

Mensagem — "Message" — is a short cycle in which the sea, the kings and the waiting for a lost king add up to a national myth in miniature: empire read as destiny. Nós matamos o cão tinhoso! — "We killed the mangy dog!" — is a book of stories out of Mozambique, by an author born in 1942 in Lourenço Marques, in a Mozambique that was then Portuguese.

One line apart, to a reader's eye: the empire's poem about its own greatness, and a book of stories from underneath it. A former colony's entrance exam requires both, in the same language, in the same year, from the same candidate. The guide prints the pair without comment — no gloss, no aim under any of the nine, nothing like the paragraph of purpose a ministry program prints above every unit.

Placement does other quiet work here. Padre Antônio Vieira preached most of his life in Brazil and died in Salvador; the guide files him under Portuguese literature, and so he counts as foreign. Tomás Antônio Gonzaga was born in Porto; the guide files him as Brazilian, and so Marília de Dirceu is domestic. Two men crossed the same ocean in the same direction and the guide sorts them opposite ways. Foreign, in this window, is a heading printed over a title, never a judgement on a life.

Reading to get in

Not one of the fifty-seven titles asks for memory: nowhere is a candidate told to hold a named text by heart. The guide says the knowledge it tests is not reducible to the memorization of facts, dates and formulas, or to the automated use of techniques. The books are to be read, and then perhaps argued about in longhand, on a paper of written answers — and marked blind.

Anyone who ever read a book because it stood between them and a place they wanted knows this shelf better than any curriculum can teach it. Not the assigned book — the other kind. The one bought second-hand, because a list said it would be on the paper; the one read at the wrong speed, in the wrong months, with a pen, hunting for what the examiners would ask. That book is never quite the reader's and never quite the exam's.

Nine books in São Paulo that year; two of them sat one line apart.
