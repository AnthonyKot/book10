# The Poem in Your Body

There is a poem in you that starts itself. You have not thought about it in years, and
you could not stop it if you tried: someone says the first three words and the next line
arrives on its own, in the voice of whoever made you learn it, in the classroom where
you were fifteen. You did not choose that poem. Somebody chose it for you, and this book
began with the discovery that the choosing was signed.

I read the documents that do the choosing — the exam codifiers, the ministry programs,
the state anthologies of twenty school systems — looking for the world in them: which
foreign books they let into their classrooms, and why. The second question the
documents almost never answer. But somewhere in the middle of the reading, a smaller
column in my tables started to glow. Row after row in these documents asks that a book
be read — discussed, examined, moved past. A few rows carry a different wording: учить
наизусть, learn by heart — the text to be carried in the body. And when I filtered
twenty files for that one instruction, almost everything fell away. Sixteen of the
twenty sign no named text into memory. Four do. Three of them —
Russia, Kazakhstan, and Ukraine — are school systems raised out of one Soviet
inheritance, and they point the same instruction at three different shelves.

The by-heart column of Kazakhstan's program at fifteen holds twelve entries — for
each, a passage to be found and held from memory. I expected Abai — the poet a whole
specialized school network is named for, the closest thing these tables have to a
Kazakh national poet. He is not among them. Not one Kazakh entry is. All twelve are
foreign: eleven Russian — Pushkin, Gogol, Dostoevsky, Tolstoy, Chekhov, an overcoat, a
set of poor folk, a garnet bracelet — and Shakespeare's sonnets, the one non-Russian
aboard, a tourist on the wrong train. The program assigns Kazakh literature for
reading, discussion, essays; its by-heart instruction is written, eleven times out of
twelve, against the literature of the empire the country used to belong to.

Ukraine sets five texts by heart at the same age — the memorization list of a model
program its ministry recommends for the subject — and not one of them is Ukrainian
either. The resemblance to Kazakhstan ends there, because none of them is Russian. A
sonnet by Dante. A sonnet by Petrarch. A sonnet by Shakespeare. A lyric by Heine. And a
poem called "To Bukovina" by Mihai Eminescu — a Romanian, writing about a region now
divided between Ukraine and Romania. The Ukrainian-literature programs next door make
memorization a recommendation and let the pupil choose the poem; the named by-heart
texts, every one, are the world's. This is the country that runs an entire school
subject called Foreign Literature, and its memory list matches: what the list sets
into a Ukrainian body is Europe.

Russia is the mirror of both, and it nearly stayed off this list: judge it by its exam
codifier and no text is named for memory at all; the mandate is written in the federal
teaching program, binding on every state-accredited school since September 2023 — the
document this book found second. That program orders a pupil finishing grade nine to recite at
least twelve poetic works not learned in earlier years, and attaches its by-heart
instruction to named units: the medieval Lay of Igor's Campaign, a courtier's ode from
1747, Griboyedov's Woe from Wit, Gogol's Dead Souls. Every one on the domestic shelf.
The same program marches its children up a staircase of required foreign classics —
Homer at twelve, Cervantes at thirteen, Hamlet at fifteen — so it is not that Russia
hides the world. The world is for reading, mostly in fragments, on the way up. The
memory is for what is Russia's own. Three states raised in one Soviet schoolhouse,
holding the same pedagogic habit in three hands — and it points at Europe in Ukraine,
at the old metropole in Kazakhstan, and at home in Russia.

The fourth is Belarus, and Belarus is the reason I trust this column more than any
other in my tables. Its programs name six texts for memory, and of the twenty files it
is the only one where the instruction splits between two literatures: four Belarusian
texts on one side — Yanka Kupala and Yakub Kolas among the authors — and set passages
of Pushkin and Griboyedov on the other. Two canons set into memory in the same
schooling — a pupil's own, and the neighbour's. Belarus runs Russian literature as a separate
subject with its own hours, and it is the one place in this corpus where the by-heart
instruction lands on both a country's own literature and its neighbour's.

And the sixteen that sign nothing? Their silence is the documents' silence — a table
of mandates cannot see past the mandate. In England, the exam board whose papers this book read
makes its poetry exam closed-book, so quotation can only come from memory, while never
once printing the instruction memorize; whatever memorizing happens hides in the
exam's design. China mandates the practice and declines to sign the poem: recitation
is required, foreign poetry is explicitly welcome in it, and the choice of texts is
handed down to textbook editors and teachers. America's nearest thing to a national
list is an appendix of exemplar titles, every one a suggestion, none ordered by heart.
The four files that write names into the by-heart wording are the exception, and that
is exactly why they matter: there, a school document does its rarest thing — it names
the text a pupil is to carry.

So this book starts where you are already an expert. Before it walks you through the
twenty windows — Ukraine's room-sized one, Russia's staircase, China's mirror,
Bolivia's cast list, the ones where a state mandates reading and declines to name a
single book — it asks you to run the experiment that no ministry can audit. Find the
poem that starts itself in you. Say the first line. Notice the language it arrives in
— because for some of you it will not be the language of your childhood street.
Decisions like that sometimes have signatures — an office, a document, a dated list —
and sometimes only an editor's desk, a teacher's taste, a blank the pupil filled
themselves. By the end of this book you will know where the looking starts — and you
can put to whatever chose your poem the question this book could never make a
document answer: what did you want me to be?

# Ukraine: The Door Is a Subject

A ninth-grader stands up to say a poem by heart. The poem is «Буковині» — "To Bukovina" —
and it was written in Romanian, by Mihai Eminescu. Bukovina is a historical region now
divided between Ukraine and Romania; the pupil is saying the poem in Ukrainian
translation. And the lesson is not Ukrainian Literature. On the timetable this hour reads
Foreign Literature.

A foreign book can enter a classroom by several routes: a unit inside the national
literature course, a line in an exam specification. Ukraine built a door. Foreign Literature sits beside Ukrainian Language and Ukrainian
Literature with its own hours, its own programs, its own list of poems to be learned by
heart. The program says so in its own voice: the subject is studied separately, as an
independent and self-sufficient one, with a uniquely unbroken teaching tradition since 1991,
under conditions of Independence.

The window is not a pane in a wall. It is a room.

## The door, and its hinges

Who decides what stands in the room? Not the top of the stack, as far as the saved
documents show: both foreign-literature
programs open by naming the instrument they were built under — the State Standard for basic
secondary education, a Cabinet of Ministers resolution — and neither reproduces its
contents.

Books are named a level down. Each of these documents is a model program, carrying on its
title page a line recommending it, from the Ministry of Education and Science of Ukraine,
above an order number. Recommended, in its own word — and there is more than one for the
same subject and the same grades.

Inside a program, the shelf then splits. The Nikolenko program divides its own contents in
two: a mandatory component of works set for compulsory study, and a variable component from
which the teacher may pick one or several. And it grants a further
step outward — teachers may use this model program as the basis for programs of their own.

So the state mandates that world literature happen and hands part of the *which* down the
stack: to a program, and inside the program, to a teacher. Only part. The obligatory works
stay put; unit headings that read one work at the teacher's choice mark where the
handing-down happens, and they do not stand over everything.

The thirty-nine translated rows here are a hybrid: the Nikolenko program's Grade 9 shelf
entire, joined by the Voloshchuk program's five-poem memorization list — taken in because
the first sets no memorization at all. Beside them, eighteen domestic rows from the
Ukrainian Literature programs next door. A selection, not an inventory: no pupil meets all
thirty-nine, and no program contains this particular thirty-nine.

## What comes through

Everything is read in Ukrainian, and the program makes the
translation part of the lesson: the works are to come in the best Ukrainian translations,
taking account of the achievements of the Ukrainian school of translation. A foreign book
enters this room already holding a Ukrainian hand — and ten source languages come through
the door behind it: English supplies nineteen of the thirty-nine rows, French six, German
four; the rest arrive in ones and twos — Italian, Polish, Russian, Yiddish, Romanian,
Norwegian, Japanese.

English holds four of the five latest-born authors — Foer, Zusak, Whitehead, Rowling — and
most of those rows sit in an additional-reading appendix rather than a taught unit.

The shelf sorts into three uneven piles.

**Europe as a destination.** The Grade 9 Enlightenment unit — reason, nature, education —
carries Swift's first voyage, Goethe's lyrics, and Schiller's ode to joy. The teaching line
under Schiller does not stop at the Enlightenment. It names the Enlightenment idea of the
unification of humanity, and then the pathos of a work that became the anthem of the
European Union. The program's sentence gives the
ode two lives in one breath: an idea out of its own century, and an anthem.

**Other people's wounds.** Line up the named injuries on each side and they do not mirror.
On the translated rows: Jim Crow in Lee, American slavery in Whitehead, the Congo Free State
in Conrad, the First World War in Morpurgo, wartime Germany in Zusak, 9/11 in Foer. On the
domestic rows next door: totalitarianism in Bahrianyi and Stus, the 1944 deportation of the
Crimean Tatars in Umerov, and a unit of poetry from the war that began in 2014. Among those
named categories, the current war appears only on the domestic side.

Subjects do cross the seam. The translated shelf also carries totalitarianism, in
Bradbury; the late Russian Empire appears on both sides; and Byron's «Мазепа» puts
Cossack-era Ukraine on a translated row. Rows record admission, never intention.

**The Ukrainian pages.** Every unit in the Nikolenko program carries a standing rubric,
Ukraine and the World; the Voloshchuk program runs the equivalent as the Ukrainian motif,
covering the ties of foreign writers to Ukraine, the mastery of Ukrainian translators, and
authors biographically connected with Ukrainian territories. Its reason, in the
program's own words: such authors "join Ukraine to the world," carrying their conceptions of
it into other cultures.

Read the shelf through that rubric and a second shelf appears inside the first. Byron
arrives with «Мазепа», tagged beside the battle of Poltava and the figure of Mazepa in
Ukrainian culture. Conrad's row notes that he was born in Berdychiv. Sholem Aleichem's Tevye
the Dairyman, the corpus's single Yiddish row, sits under a Ukraine-and-the-World line that
opens on Sholem Aleichem and Ukraine.

Two rows are Russian-language. Gogol's offers The Government Inspector or The Overcoat, and
the program frames the author by the Petersburg stage of his life and work, with the rubric
over the unit reading Gogol and Ukrainian culture. Bulgakov's row is
Heart of a Dog, and his entry opens on the Ukrainian pages of his life and work. The corpus
notes mark both as retained — Ukrainian origin in Gogol's case, Kyivan birth in Bulgakov's.
What the documents themselves do is placement: two Russian-language authors taught inside a
subject about the world, under a rubric about Ukraine.

One thing sits outside this table. There is no row for it, but both Ukrainian-literature
model programs set Gogol's Sorochyntsi Fair in Grade 9 — so one author walks through both
doors of the same school, a national writer down one corridor, a foreign one down the other.

## The poem in the body

Back to the ninth-grader on their feet.

Five rows carry a memorization mandate, and every one of them is translated: Heine, Dante,
Petrarch, Shakespeare, Eminescu. The domestic rows carry none — the defining
Ukrainian-literature program makes memorization recommendatory and lets the learner choose
the poem. Whatever a Ukrainian teenager is *required* to hold in their body, in this corpus,
came from somewhere else.

Four of the five are lyric, and four of the five are about a person: a Dante sonnet to
Beatrice, a Petrarch sonnet (61 or 132, learner's choice), a Shakespeare sonnet (116 or
130), one Heine lyric of three. The fifth is «Буковині», and it is the only one whose
subject is a place.

The program does not print the poem and walk away. Under the title it sets brief information
about Eminescu and Bukovina, and then what the hour is for: the image of Bukovina and its
nature, the romantic moods and motifs, the inner world of the lyric speaker. Beside it the
Ukrainian-motif rubric sends the class out of the poem and into the geography — Eminescu in
Chernivtsi, his travels through Ukraine, the monument to him there.

So the text a ninth-grader must hold by heart, in Ukrainian translation, inside a subject
about the world, is a Romanian poet's address to a region that lies partly inside their own
country's border — and what the teaching line asks them to find in it is an image of
Bukovina and its nature, while the rubric beside it asks them to find a statue in
Chernivtsi.

What this placement imports is a Ukrainian place, described in a language that is not
Ukrainian. The program itself puts it more narrowly: such authors join Ukraine to the
world.

The other program names something adjacent. Over the Grade 9 history-and-modernity unit,
where the teacher picks two works from five — Sholem Aleichem, Bulgakov, Bradbury, Zusak,
Foer — stands a culture rubric: culture as spiritual resistance to violence. That heading
belongs to those five books, not to the whole shelf.

## Fifteen

You were fifteen in some room with a list — a photocopy with a column of due dates, or the
back page of a textbook you never finished. Someone chose it, and the choosing was invisible
to you. If your classroom made you learn a poem by heart, run the Ukrainian test on it: was
the poem yours?

In this table the five texts a ninth-grader must hold by memory are an Italian's sonnet to
Beatrice, another Italian's sonnet to Laura, an Englishman's sonnet, a German's lyric — and
a Romanian's poem to Bukovina, learned in Ukrainian, inside a subject the Nikolenko program
describes as having a uniquely unbroken teaching tradition since 1991. The clock in the
Voloshchuk header is an hour and a half a week, recommended. Small, weekly, and a room of
its own.

# Russia: The Staircase

A Russian child enters fifth grade at eleven, and somewhere in Moscow a document already
knows, year by year, which foreigners they are going to meet on the way up.

At eleven, the world arrives as story: the myths of the world's peoples, required;
Andersen's Snow Queen or another of his tales; a choice of Carroll or Tolkien; two works
from a childhood shelf holding Twain, London and Bradbury; two adventures, with Stevenson
as the printed example. At twelve it gets serious: Homer, in fragments, required; then
Defoe's island, required; then Swift's giants and academies, required. At thirteen,
Cervantes — the knight himself, chapters of him — and The Little Prince, whole. At
fourteen, Shakespeare twice: a sonnet or two of the pupil's choosing, and Romeo and
Juliet, required, with Molière's bourgeois gentleman for comedy. And at fifteen, the year
this book measures every country at, the staircase reaches its landing: Dante at the
gates of the afterlife, Hamlet on the battlements, Faust in his study, Byron's Childe
Harold on the road — all four required — with two further slots where a pupil picks a
Byron lyric and a teacher picks Hoffmann, Hugo or Scott.

That is a real window, and it is not the one Russia is usually judged by. Judge it by the
exam and you would conclude the window barely exists: the grade-nine examination codifier
compresses all foreign literature into a single choice line, a parenthesis holding Homer,
Cervantes, Shakespeare and Molière as examples. But the exam is only the door out of
school. The window is on the staircase, and the staircase is written down in the federal
working program for literature — directly binding, since September 2023, on every
organization teaching a state-accredited basic general education program. The program is
not advice. It is the floor plan.

## Facing one direction

So Russia turns out to run a large mandatory foreign shelf — twelve required foreign rows
across the five grades, before any teacher makes any choice — and a narrow one. Walk the
fixed, named core of the ladder and look at who is on it. Homer is ancient Greece; Dante
is the Middle Ages; Cervantes, Shakespeare and Molière the sixteenth and seventeenth
centuries; Defoe and Swift the eighteenth; Goethe and Byron the early nineteenth. The
newest author on that named mandatory core is Saint-Exupéry, and he is there as a fable.
The choice shelves widen the picture — Twain, London, Bradbury, Harper Lee, O. Henry and
Cooper wait in the optional slots, so the named shelf as a whole is Europe plus North
America — but no named author from Asia, Africa or Latin America appears anywhere in the
twenty-six foreign rows; those worlds enter only anonymously, in grade five's myths and
tales of the world's peoples. The required window faces in one direction: the European
classics, its named core ending roughly where the twentieth century begins. Russia's
program does not import the world. It imports the canon of Europe, as a stated
inheritance.

## Fragments and wholes

And it imports it in pieces. Look again at how the required foreigners arrive: Homer in
fragments; Defoe and Swift in selected chapters; Cervantes in chapters; Shakespeare's
tragedy in fragments of the pupil's or teacher's choosing; Dante in at least two
fragments; Faust in at least two; Childe Harold in at least one. Ten of the eleven named
required foreign works carry that stamp — fragments, chapters, by choice. And because so
many of the stamps read "by choice," two pupils on the same staircase may have met quite
different pieces of the same Dante. Now look at the
domestic shelf beside them in grade nine: Griboyedov's Woe from Wit, whole. Pushkin's
Eugene Onegin, whole. Lermontov's A Hero of Our Time, whole. Gogol's Dead Souls, whole.
The program's arithmetic of attention is visible on the page: the world is met in
excerpts, the homeland in full texts. A fifteen-year-old finishes the year having stood
briefly at four of European literature's summits and having lived inside four Russian
books.

The one exception to the fragment stamp is worth a close look, because it is the
program's most tender act of admission. The single foreign work a Russian child is
required to read whole, cover to cover, is The Little Prince — at thirteen, in the same
year the knight arrives in chapters: a fable about a small person responsible for one
rose on one planet. Everything else from abroad
arrives as a monument to be visited in fragments; the one whole foreign book is the one
about caring for something because you have tamed it. That the ladder's builders required
tenderness entire and Dante in excerpts is perhaps the most human sentence the document
never quite says.

## What the body keeps

Then there is the question of what the body keeps. The program orders memorization the
way a codifier never could: a pupil finishing grade nine must be able to recite at
least twelve poetic works not learned in earlier years, and for certain named works the
by-heart instruction is attached directly to the unit — the medieval Lay of Igor's
Campaign, shelved as domestic by the program's own placement; Lomonosov's ode on the
empress's accession; Woe from Wit; Dead Souls. Every work carrying that instruction is on
the domestic shelf. The four required foreigners of grade nine — Dante, Shakespeare,
Goethe, Byron — carry no memorization instruction at all.

In Kazakhstan, this book found a program whose every by-heart mandate pointed at foreign,
mostly Russian, texts; in Ukraine, at foreign texts inside a subject built for them.
Russia is the mirror: the world is for reading; the memory is for what is Russia's own.
The staircase teaches a European inheritance to the mind and a national one to the body.
That is this essay's reading, not the program's doctrine — the program mandates lists,
hours and recitations, and gives reasons for none of them.

## The landing

At the top of the staircase stands the exam, and the two documents part company. Five
years of differentiated requirement — this Dante, that Hamlet, those chapters of
Cervantes — collapse, in the grade-nine codifier, into one undifferentiated choice line
where Homer, Cervantes, Shakespeare and Molière appear as examples. The exam does not exclude the world; it
stops guaranteeing any particular piece of it. The program proves what is taught, the
codifier what is promised at the door out of school, and the gap between them is the
finding.

A pupil who climbs the whole staircase — five years, step by step, from the Snow Queen at
eleven to Faust's study at fifteen — meets the foreign classics as a sequence of guided
excursions, each visit measured out in fragments and chapters chosen by teacher or pupil,
and meets the Russian classics as a house to be occupied: four whole books in the final
year alone, and at least twelve poems to be carried out by heart. The window on the
staircase is real, and Europe's canon climbs it year by year, all the way to the landing;
it is climbed, and it is never kept. But the door of the house locks from the inside,
and the key is learned by heart.

# China: The Mirror on the Shelf

Somewhere in the compulsory years of a Chinese schooling, a pupil is set a habit rather
than a book. The curriculum standard requires that students "read more, read good books,
read whole books," and for the long ones it offers a single example sentence. It is worth
walking like a shelf, left to right.

Nine titles. The first four are Chinese: Wu Cheng'en's *Journey to the West*, Shi Nai'an's
*Water Margin*, Lao She's *Camel Xiangzi*, then Luo Guangbin and Yang Yiyan's *Red Crag*.
The last four are foreign: Swift's *Gulliver's Travels*, Charlotte Brontë's *Jane Eyre*,
Gorky's *Childhood*, Ostrovsky's *How the Steel Was Tempered*. Between the halves, fifth
of nine, stands Edgar Snow's *Red Star Over China* — a book translated from English whose
subject is China: an account of China, written from outside it, in another language.
A window is supposed to face outward. This one has been hung facing the room.

Of the printed line's five foreign-authored titles, three — Snow, Brontë, Ostrovsky — can
be followed closely here; Jules Verne, printed on a different line of the same appendix,
makes a fourth. A foreign shelf small enough to read closely.

## A rule with two leaves

The defining document is the Compulsory Education Chinese Language Curriculum Standard,
issued by China's Ministry of Education in April 2022. It governs one subject — Chinese
language and literature — and it names books in two appendices, with unlike force.

The first leaf is recitation, and the window arrives there before any foreign book does,
inside a subordinate clause of the sentence that establishes the mandate: students must
recite from memory excellent poetry and prose ancient and modern — Chinese classical,
Chinese modern and contemporary, and foreign — with the specific pieces left to textbook
editors and teachers, and only 135 pieces of classical poetry and prose recommended
underneath.

The clause opens a Chinese teenager's memory to the poetry of the world — and then the
list beneath it names classical Chinese poetry and prose, sixty pieces in its
lower-secondary stretch, not one of them foreign. The foreigners are all on the other
leaf.

The second leaf is reading — suggestions for reading in and out of class. It opens by
requiring the habit, then disclaims its own catalogue in the next paragraph: its
recommendations are examples only. Neither leaf makes a named work examinable. Force in
China is a named example inside a mandated practice: the state requires that reciting
happen and that whole books be read, and leaves to editors and teachers which texts do
the work.

## The guests

All four foreign books sit on the reading leaf, under two of its headings.

Under popular-science and science-fiction works stands a single line naming two examples:
*A Hundred Thousand Whys*, and Jules Verne's *Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Sea*.
Verne enters not among the masterworks but on the line shared with popular science, and
the filing speaks through what stands next to him: the submarine arrives as apparatus, a
book admitted in the company of explanations.

Two of the other three stand on the masterworks line already walked. Charlotte Brontë supplies
*Jane Eyre*, a nineteenth-century story of class and gender. Nikolai Ostrovsky supplies
*How the Steel Was Tempered*, set in the Russian civil war — the one book on this shelf
about a war fought outside China.

Ostrovsky's is the book worth opening, because the feeling it imports announces itself on
the page, in the novel's most famous passage, the one that made it a school text across
the Soviet world — life "is given to him once, and it must be lived so that there is no
agonizing pain over years lived aimlessly." Not grief but resolve — a steelworks of the
will, offered at the age when years first look spendable.

## Edgar Snow, the mirror

And the fifth position. Snow is the one foreigner on the shelf not writing fiction: his
book is reportage, its subject the Red Army and the Long March. Three things about it can
be read closely.

First, the heading. The word the appendix uses for that line, 名著, is canon vocabulary —
the established, famous work. The appendix keeps a separate line for works of poetry and
prose, where Lu Xun's *Dawn Blossoms Plucked at Dusk* stands; Snow's book is on the
masterworks line instead, wedged between the Chinese novels and the foreign ones: a book
of reporting filed among the classics.

Second, the title. The Chinese does something the English gloss does not. *Red Star Over
China* is a preposition — a star hanging in place. The Chinese title, 《红星照耀中国》,
puts a verb in the middle: 照耀, to shine upon, to illuminate. The star acts, and the
country is the grammatical object.

Third, an adjacency. The appendix's one stated direction about content — that teachers
recommending supplementary reading should favor works reflecting revolutionary culture
and advanced socialist culture — attaches to supplementary recommending in general, not
to the masterworks line. But Snow's is the one foreign book on the shelf whose subject —
the Red Army, the Long March — belongs to the history behind the culture that direction
favors.

The inference here comes from a placement and a grammar; the ministry states no purpose.
And still: a pupil who walks the printed line meets, in the middle of it, a foreign
witness filed among the masters, whose Chinese title has the star illuminating the
country itself. A window hung facing the room.

## What is held, and what is only read

Not one of the sixty-nine named works is a mandated memory. The recitation leaf requires
reciting in general, calls its pieces recommendations, and leaves the specific choices to
editors and teachers.

But look where the two leaves point. The recitation shelf — Guan Ju, Mulan, Du Fu's
spring, Wen Tianxiang passing Lingding Ocean — is Chinese without exception; the
whole-book shelf is where the foreigners stand. On this national list, no foreign work is
named for memory; the four this chapter follows are all named for reading. And the clause
that opened recitation
to foreign poetry and prose stands open, its filling handed to editors and teachers; a
foreign poem may already be in Chinese children's mouths by the route the document
describes and declines to name.

Any country's shelf of whole books can be put to the test this one teaches: which of the
books came from outside, and which of those looked back at the country itself. Of the
four foreign books this chapter follows, exactly one looks back — and the pupil who walks
the state's example sentence meets it midway, fifth of nine, a foreign witness among the
masters, holding the mirror up to the room.

# Vietnam: A Bookcase, and Eight Names for the World

Before it says anything at all about foreign literature, Vietnam's national program for Ngữ
văn — the single subject that carries the Vietnamese language and its literature — furnishes
the room. The minimum equipment of the subject, it says, is a reference bookcase: stories,
poetry, drama, reportage, argument, picture books. It also wants portraits of the program's
major writers, and pictures illustrating some of the major works — six titles follow as
examples, between a *such as* and an ellipsis, and the program sets no number of pictures and
no place to hang them. At the desks it wants whole books, telling teachers to limit teaching by
extract.

So furnish the room yourself, because the document won't. Put the bookcase where you like and
the pictures wherever you last saw pictures in a school. The six titles under them are not
arbitrary: the same six appear earlier in the program as its compulsory works, the only
literature it obliges both a textbook's author and a teacher to carry out. An anonymous poem
from the Lý dynasty; Trần Quốc Tuấn's address to his officers; Nguyễn Trãi's great
proclamation; *Truyện Kiều*; Nguyễn Đình Chiểu's funeral oration for the fighters of Cần Giuộc;
Hồ Chí Minh's declaration of independence. Four of the six sit against an invasion or an
occupation — Song, Mongol, Ming, French — and a fifth against the end of a world war.

The rest of the world's literature is in this room too. It is not among the six, and on the
day the program was signed it was not yet a book. It was one line.

## The line

It instructs: choose at least one work for each of the following literatures — English,
French, American, Greek, Russian, Japanese, Chinese, Indian.

Eight literatures. A floor of one work apiece. No titles, no authors, no centuries — eight
names, and not one book.

The two lines above it are built the other way round. Vietnamese folk literature gets five
categories, each with a minimum attached — folk-tale genres, folk verses, an epic, an
ethnic-minority verse narrative, a script for one of the traditional theatre forms. Vietnamese
written literature gets thirteen names, Nguyễn Trãi and Nguyễn Du and Hồ Xuân Hương among them.
Then, in the same tier and the same grammar, foreign literature gets eight
countries. At home the program guarantees particular writers. Abroad it guarantees only the
literatures a work must come from. Nothing in the program explains the difference, and a list
cannot be made to give a reason for itself.

Nor is the floor a promise of space. The program asks for a relative balance between Vietnamese
and foreign literature, between East and West, and then defines its own word against the
obvious reading of it: balance means a suitable proportion, not an equal one. A ministry
writing down that a quota is a floor and not a scale.

## Whose job is a name

Take one of the eight names — Nga, Russia — and follow it toward the bookcase. The program
grades its own force in a single sentence, and the addressee changes as you go down:
compulsory works bind textbook authors and teachers alike; compulsory-selection works, the
eight names among them, bind one person — the textbook author, who must choose a work from
each named literature; suggested works bind nobody. The circular that promulgates all this,
signed by the Minister of Education and Training at the end of 2018, addresses its
foreign-literature instruction not to a country's teachers but to whoever is writing a book
for them. The word *Nga* lands on somebody's desk.

And the program's own suggestions, at the back of the document, do not answer it. They are a
menu grouped by genre, alphabetical by Vietnamese title, calling itself only illustrative
examples: in the grade 10–12 fiction list Hemingway sits between *Odysseus* and *Số đỏ*, and
nothing says he is the American one. The quota names literatures, the menu names books, and
no line runs between them — *One Hundred Years of Solitude* is there, a Vietnamese title and
a bracketed G. Marquez, written in Spanish, which answers to none of the eight names, while
India, one of the eight, gets no named example in the grades covered here. A suggestion
matching no requirement; a requirement with no suggestion.

## Two teachers

The two Russian-language stories in the menu are printed as flatly as the rest — a dash, a
Vietnamese title, a bracket: *The First Teacher*, C. Aitmatov; *The Man in a Case*, A. Chekhov.
That both were written in Russian is a fact about language and nothing more. The document does
not say that either answers to *Nga*, or that anything else does. Chinghiz Aitmatov, for his
part, was Kyrgyz, and wrote in both Kyrgyz and Russian. In this list a foreign author is an
initial, a surname and a bracket — unhoused.

The titles rhyme before anyone opens the books: one names a teacher, the other a man who has
put himself away. Open them and the rhyme holds. Chekhov's Belikov teaches Greek and keeps
every part of himself in a case, and his fear settles over a whole town. Aitmatov's Duishen is
the opposite man and the opposite failure — a demobilised soldier with barely more schooling
than his pupils, who turns a stable into a classroom and teaches from almost nothing, carrying
children across an icy stream on his back.

Now look at what surrounds them. The grade 10–12 Vietnamese fiction here is heavy with the
wars: Anh Đức, Nguyễn Thi, Phan Tứ, Nguyễn Minh Châu, Bảo Ninh, Nguyễn Khải — six entries whose
subject is the anti-US war or its aftermath. Beside a domestic shelf that keeps returning to
the war, these two imports are both about a room with a teacher in it, one of them a warning
and one of them a debt.

## The last instruction is the loosest

Come back to the room. There is one more thing the program asks a pupil to do with a book, and
from grade 6 to grade 12 it asks it in the same sentence, word for word: "Learn by heart some
favourite verse passages or poems from the program."

Seven grades, seven identical lines. No work is named, no number is set, and the sentence does
not say who decides that a poem is a favourite — the pupil, the textbook, or the teacher. The
primary grades are written the other way: grade 1 asks for four or five short passages already
studied, and the count climbs year by year to at least ten or twelve longer ones by grade 5.
The counting stops when primary school does. What replaces it is the favourite.

So no work here carries a memorization mandate of its own — which means only that no text is
named and no quantity tied to one, not that nothing is learned by heart in Vietnam. Learning by
heart is itself one of the outcomes the program requires.

That is the shape of this window, top to bottom: a named domestic canon; eight unnamed
literatures, addressed to a person with a manuscript; a menu the document says is only
examples; and an instruction to learn a poem by heart with the poem left blank.

The blank is the last thing this document does, and it is the same move it made in the quota
line: require the thing, decline to name it. The Vietnamese program says only that the blank
must be filled. It does not say by whom.

# India: The Lesson That Won't Be Asked

Before an Indian fifteen-year-old reads a line of Anton Chekhov, they are asked to fill in a
table. The columns are printed for them: customs similar to Indian ones, customs different
from Indian ones. Above the table sits a short article about Russian weddings — the groom
climbing an apartment block's stairs, answering a riddle at each landing — and then the
question of whether Indian and Russian weddings have any customs in common, to be worked out
with a partner.

Only then does the play begin: Lomov arrives at Chubukov's house in a dress-suit to propose to
Natalya, and the three of them quarrel about a meadow and a dog until the proposal is nearly
lost. The unit ends with a group discussion of the qualities one should look for in a marriage
partner, under headings that include attitude to money and wealth.

The errand is written on the page before the work starts: a Russian farce about two landowning
families, brought into an Indian classroom to be set beside an Indian wedding, in ruled
columns, by a child with a partner.

## The door is a book, and a subtraction

India's window has an unusual hinge. The Central Board of Secondary Education issues a
curriculum for each subject code, and the last page of that curriculum carries a list of
prescribed books and a note naming the lessons from which no questions will be asked. The
books themselves are published by the National Council of Educational Research and Training.
The board that examines names the anthology, and then it subtracts.

The English syllabus prints its chapter list in full, prose and poems, book by book. The Hindi
syllabi do not. Hindi Course A names *Kshitij Part 2* and *Kritika Part 2* and stops — then
names, from *Kritika*, only the lessons it removes, one of them a satire called "George the
Fifth's Nose". The only *Kritika* chapters the syllabus names are the ones it takes away.

And nothing the lists name is marked to be learned by heart — not a title, not an excerpt,
not a stated quantity of texts. What the English paper asks for is an extract and a short
written answer.

## Streams in one corridor

A school's window here is not one window. In the same Class 10 corridor a pupil may sit Hindi
Course A — mother-tongue Hindi, with *Kshitij* and *Kritika* — or Hindi Course B, with
*Sparsh* and *Sanchayan*; English Language and Literature is a separate code with its own two
readers. Same board, different worlds.

The shelf in view here is the English stream's thirty-one chapters and the twelve of
*Kshitij* — forty-three works, every one Class 10 and prescribed. Hindi Course B stays shut,
all but the last page of its syllabus. That page comes later.

## What comes through the English door

Mostly, the English-speaking world itself. The syllabus says so in its own objectives: the
course sets out to develop appreciation of world literature — and, in the same sentence,
scopes that world to varieties of English. Of the forty-three works in view, only four come
from a language that is neither English nor Indian: a Mexican story from Spanish, a Dutch
diary, a Russian one-act play, a French short story — López y Fuentes, Anne Frank, Chekhov,
Maupassant. The split is cleanest in poetry. Every poem in the English reader comes from
abroad, in English — seven of the ten American, the others Welsh, Australian, Irish — and no
Indian poet holds a poetry slot there. The Indian poetry is next door, in Hindi, where Surdas
and Tulsidas open *Kshitij* and everything is domestic.

## Chekhov, twice

One writer walks through two doors of the same school.

Through the English door, Chekhov is a chapter with a page number: *First Flight*'s last unit,
printed on the contents page, the one author in the book who wrote in Russian, his surname
spelled Chekov there. The reader's own blurb explains the farce to the class as a study in
wealthy families courting other wealthy families, marrying their estates together for sound
economic reasons. He is examinable: the paper draws extracts from drama and prose and short
answers from *First Flight*.

Then the unit closes, and the last page is addressed to the teacher rather than the child. It
reports what has been done — the class has been given a play by the famous Russian writer
Anton Chekhov — and then proposes a game: dictate the life of another writer, and let the
students guess his name. The paragraph that follows has a hole where the name should be. Its
subject was born at Stratford-on-Avon, married a woman older than himself, retired, died, and
left behind a body of work that "still stands as a pinnacle in world literature".

The one Russian in the book is handed over, and the class is invited to guess the Englishman.
The exercise assumes recognition: it withholds the name because it expects the name back. That
is what a shared shelf looks like from inside — not a text on the syllabus, but a riddle whose
answer is assumed to be in the room already.

Through the Hindi B door, Chekhov appears exactly once, and not as a chapter. On the last page
of that curriculum, under the note saying questions will not be asked from the following
lessons, in the block for the prescribed reader *Sparsh Part 2*, between Bihari's couplets and
a Mahadevi Varma poem, stands Anton Chekhov's name in Devanagari beside a story called
"Girgit" — and in brackets after it, the whole lesson.

Whether today's reprint of *Sparsh* still prints the story is an open question; the naming is
not. In one stream Chekhov is an examinable text with a warm-up table about weddings; in the
stream across the corridor, his name is printed for the sole purpose of being fenced off the
paper.

## Underneath the syllabus

There is a layer below the syllabus, and it is where some of the world actually lives. In the
exercises after "A Tiger in the Zoo" a second poem is printed whole — Rilke's "The Panther,"
in Stephen Mitchell's English — set beside Leslie Norris's tiger. It is not a chapter and not
a listed text; it sits there anyway, entire, in the exercises.

The layer runs the other way too. Inside the Chekhov unit, the writing exercise hands the class
a passage from Tagore's *The Home and the World* to turn into a script — Bimala demanding that
foreign goods be cleared out of the market, her husband refusing: "To tyrannise for the country
is to tyrannise over the country." A swadeshi argument — the home-made against the
foreign-made — in the middle of the one foreign-language play in the book. The anthology put
them on facing pages.

The shelf closes on paperwork. *Footprints Without Feet* still tells its teachers, in a
preface, that it represents writers from across the world — Indian, British, French, American
and Japanese. Its nine chapters here are Indian, British, French and American; the Japanese
writer the preface promises is not among them. And the English reader's last printed page,
after Chekhov and after Shakespeare's unnamed biography, is the Fundamental Rights of the
Constitution of India, including the right of minorities to conserve their language, script
and culture.

## The lesson that won't be asked

A teacher going down the contents page with a pen — not this one, not this one — performs the
only editorial act in a school year that a child witnesses directly: the shelf shrinking in
front of the class, for reasons nobody explains, shortly before the syllabus becomes real.

India's documents perform that act in writing, at the bottom of the page, in a box, and the
box promises a chapter a strange afterlife: still printed, still in the bag, weightless. In
the syllabus that governed Class 10 in 2024-25, one such name in the Hindi B box is Anton
Chekhov's. One corridor away, in English — in a reader still printed today — the same writer
arrives with a warm-up table about weddings.

# Bolivia: A Cast, Not a Reading List

The last thing Bolivia's literature unit asks a fourth-year secondary pupil to do is not read
anything. It is to put on a play about a period. Inspired by the study of literary currents,
the book says, let us stage a short dramatization about one of them — and in the
dramatization, present an author and their literary production.

So somebody has to be the author. Four pages earlier the book has printed, for the eighteenth
century, five names one to a line, each with a country stamped after it like a passport. Jean
de la Fontaine, France, fables. Félix María Samaniego, Spain, fables. Daniel Defoe, Spain,
*Robinson Crusoe*. Jonathan Swift, Ireland, *Gulliver's Travels*. And Kant, Germany, the
*Critique of Pure Reason* — his name left stranded at the end of Swift's line by the book's own
typesetting.

Two of those lines are worth stopping on. Daniel Defoe is Spanish here. And Kant is standing
in a line of literary works, holding the *Critique of Pure Reason*.

Read those five lines next to that staging task, and Bolivia's window stops looking like a
shelf of books to be read and starts looking like a company of foreigners available to be
played.

## The door is an object

Most doors in this corpus are lists: a codifier of what an examiner may ask, a specification of
set texts, a ministry program naming units. Bolivia's door is a book. One physical volume, the
learning text for the fourth year of secondary education, marked *official text 2024* above the
Minister of Education's name, and at the foot of the same page, in capitals, a line prohibiting
the sale of this document.

It is also everything at once. The same covers carry the endocrine glands and the War of the
Pacific. Of it all, world literature is a short stretch: five pages of tables and a few pages
of extracts.

Every one of the twenty-nine rows coded from the volume is anthology-only: not one is tied
to an exam, not one carries a memorization mandate. The book disclaims itself in its own
foreword — its contents an element that energizes learning, one that can always be widened,
deepened and contextualized — and points upward to an instrument it does not print, the
Plans and Programs established for each educational level. There is a door above this door.
A name printed here is present; it is not thereby required.

## The names, and how they are labelled

The unit sits under a heading that would be hard to invent: the promotion of gender equity in
harmony with Mother Earth in the study of literary currents. Beneath it, six numbered sections
cover the currents and print seven ruled tables between them — the fourth section gives Realism
and Naturalism a table each. The final row of every table is headed *emblematic works and
representative authors*, and twenty of the twenty-five foreign rows in this corpus are names
harvested from those cells. That heading is the instruction for how to read them: emblematic. A
name there is not a book assigned; it is offered as evidence that a period existed.

Read the tables in order and they walk in a direction. Neoclassicism is filed as France,
eighteenth century; Romanticism as Germany, nineteenth; Costumbrismo as Spain; Realism as
France, Spain, England, Mexico; Naturalism as France. All of it arriving from across the
Atlantic. Then the compass swings west — Indigenismo, Latin America, twentieth century — and
the last section says out loud what the swing means. Modernismo, the book explains, stands out
from the rest because it was born for the first time in America and is not an imitation of any
other current; on the contrary, its influence reached Europe from the Americas.

It is the one current the book describes as born here and exported. And it is in those last two
tables, and in none of the five before them, that a Bolivian name stands as an equal instance
of the same thing as a foreign one: Ricardo Jaimes Freyre beside Rubén Darío and José Martí;
Alcides Arguedas and Jesús Lara beside Ciro Alegría of Peru. For five currents the country is
the audience. For two it is a member of the company.

Two entries are labelled in ways the document never notices. Defoe, tagged Spain. And Andrés
Bello, the sole representative author of Costumbrismo — the current about local custom —
introduced not by a novel but as Simón Bolívar's teacher, holding a treatise on the law of
nations. Whatever a pupil presenting Costumbrismo would have to present, it is closer to a
founding than to a fiction.

One more thing about this company, set against the heading over its head. Of the twenty-five
foreign rows, twenty-four name a man and one names no author at all. The only woman named
anywhere among the twenty-nine rows is Bolivian, and she stands five pages past the last table,
outside the currents entirely — Mónica Paulina Gutiérrez Jiménez, credited for a free
adaptation of an Ayoreo legend printed under the heading *spelling workshop*.

## Shakespeare comes on loan

If you want to see the mechanism at once, look at what happens when the book stops naming
authors and prints one. A *Hamlet* fragment appears under an instruction that the class read it
with expressiveness — one of the best-known texts in the history of world theatre, the book
says. The heading above it reads, exactly as printed, "(Adapted fragment) Author: Hamlet
William Shakespeare." The source line credits not an edition of the play but a 2019 web article
about what dramatic texts are, on a popular psychology site. Two of the questions underneath
are: have you heard of William Shakespeare, and do you find the situation sad or funny?

Adapted, mis-headed, and sourced from an article about dramatic form rather than from the play.
The world's literature arrives on this page to be recognized and read out, and no edition of
the play stands behind it.

## The object

A book stamped official and forbidden to be sold — one volume for the whole subject,
however it reaches the desk — is not a shelf but a fact, the way a room is a fact. Bolivia's
official text for the fourth year sets out the literature of the world as a line of names with
countries after them: a Frenchman with fables, an Irishman with a giant's country, a
German philosopher holding the *Critique of Pure Reason* as though it were a novel, an
Englishman wearing Spain's flag by a printer's slip. Four pages on, the unit asks for a
dramatization presenting an author and their literary production.

The document never says those names are the cast for that play. It prints them, and it prints
the task, and the pages invite the comparison.

# Brazil: Nine Books and a Door You Have to Want

The guide that names Brazil's nine obligatory books greets its reader twice, once in each
gender: the same Portuguese noun printed a second time with its ending changed, feminine
and then masculine: the guide refuses to greet only half its readers. And the noun is
not *pupil*. A *vestibulando* is somebody standing outside a university, trying to get in.
You have in your hands, the greeting says, your Journey Guide.

Deep in the same book, nine titles are printed under Leituras Obrigatórias, Obligatory
Readings — what the journey costs in reading. Gonzaga's *Marília de Dirceu*. Machado's
*Quincas Borba*. Graciliano Ramos's *Angústia*. Drummond's *Alguma Poesia*. Pessoa's
*Mensagem*. Honwana's *Nós matamos o cão tinhoso!* Guimarães Rosa's *Campo Geral*. Cecília
Meireles's *Romanceiro da Inconfidência*. Milton Hatoum's *Dois irmãos*.

No national mandate is in evidence; nothing here obliges a Brazilian to read them. It
obliges a route: whoever sits the FUVEST vestibular for a place at the University of São
Paulo reads the nine.

## Whose door this is

Most doors in this book are the state's — a codifier, a national program, a state
anthology, reaching into a compulsory classroom to tell a fifteen-year-old what will be on
the paper. This one belongs to a university. FUVEST runs the entrance exam, the
*vestibular*, for USP, and the guide states its legal footing plainly: exam and guides
alike rest on a 2023 resolution of the university's own council. One state, one
institution, one year — and not even all of USP's doors, since the same guide points to two
other ways in, and the nine belong to neither.

That does not shrink the list's force; it relocates it. The candidate is not a captive
audience but a volunteer; the reading is not homework but a toll. The exam runs in two
rounds: a day of multiple-choice questions, then, for whoever clears the bar, a second
phase opening with written-answer questions in Portuguese — on text interpretation, grammar
and literature — and an essay.

Two lists sit in the same chapter, and they do not weigh the same. The syllabus offers a
repertoire and hedges it as it does so: the forty-eight readings it names are examples
among others it never names — not a menu and not a fence. The other list is the nine, and
its sentence carries no hedge at all: the works of obligatory reading for the 2024
competition *will be*. Will be, for this exam, this year.

## What the window holds

The window opens onto the language rather than the world. Every foreign work the guide
names sits under a Portuguese-language heading: of its fifty-seven titles, thirty-seven are
Brazilian, seventeen Portuguese, and three come from a shelf headed African literatures in
the Portuguese language — defined by what it is written in, not by where it is from. No
French shelf, no English, no Greek, no Russian; no translator, no edition, no language
named for any single work. The heading overhead is the whole customs declaration.

The shelves are not equal even so. What the guide expects, where literary texts are
concerned, is knowledge of the representative works of the different *periods* of Brazilian
and Portuguese literature — the African heading is not in that sentence, and the lists
underneath repeat the omission. Brazil gets a lettered run of periods, Baroque through
contemporary; Portugal a longer one, from the medieval troubadours to Modernism. Africa
gets no periods at all — two lines, Pepetela's *Mayombe* and José Luandino Vieira's
*Luuanda*, two Angolan names and their titles with no century around them. Two of these
shelves are given a history. The third is given authors.

## Two lines of the nine

The obligatory list is where an African title becomes compulsory, and it is worth reading
lines five and six in the order the guide prints them: Fernando Pessoa's *Mensagem*, and
then Luís Bernardo Honwana's *Nós matamos o cão tinhoso!*

*Mensagem* — "Message" — is a short cycle in which the sea, the kings and the waiting for a
lost king add up to a national myth in miniature: empire read as destiny. *Nós matamos o
cão tinhoso!* — "We killed the mangy dog!" — is a book of stories out of Mozambique, by an
author born in 1942 in Lourenço Marques, in a Mozambique that was then Portuguese.

One line apart, to a reader's eye: the empire's poem about its own greatness, and a book
of stories from underneath it. A former colony's entrance exam requires both, in the same language, in the
same year, from the same candidate. The guide prints the pair without comment — no gloss,
no aim under any of the nine, nothing like the paragraph of purpose a ministry program
prints above every unit.

Placement does other quiet work here. Padre Antônio Vieira preached most of his life in
Brazil and died in Salvador; the guide files him under Portuguese literature, and so he
counts as foreign. Tomás Antônio Gonzaga was born in Porto; the guide files him as
Brazilian, and so *Marília de Dirceu* is domestic. Two men crossed the same ocean in the
same direction and the guide sorts them opposite ways. Foreign, in this window, is a
heading printed over a title, never a judgement on a life.

## Reading to get in

Not one of the fifty-seven titles asks for memory: nowhere is a candidate told to hold a
named text by heart. The guide says the knowledge it tests is not reducible to the
memorization of facts, dates and formulas, or to the automated use of techniques. The books
are to be read, and then perhaps argued about in longhand, on a paper of written
answers — and marked blind.

Anyone who ever read a book because it stood between them and a place they wanted knows this
shelf better than any curriculum can teach it. Not the assigned book — the *other* kind. The
one bought second-hand, because a list said it would be on the paper; the one read at the
wrong speed, in the wrong months, with a pen, hunting for what the examiners would ask. That
book is never quite the reader's and never quite the exam's.

Nine books in São Paulo that year; two of them sat one line apart.

# Australia: The Word Between Two Books

On a beach outside Algiers, a Frenchman shoots a man the novel never names. The book
calls him the Arab. He has a sister, a knife, and no line of dialogue; the rest of the
novel belongs to the shooter, tried less for the killing than for failing to weep at his
mother's funeral.

Year 12 students in New South Wales meet that beach — but only the ones whose class
picked the third pair on a state list, where two titles are stacked one above the other
with a single word printed between them: Camus's *The Stranger*, **AND** Kamel Daoud's
*The Meursault Investigation*. The second book gives
the dead man a name — Musa — and a brother who will not stop talking about him. Camus
died in 1960; the man who answered him was born ten years later. In a table of 140
prescribed texts, that is the one pairing I read as a book and its rebuttal — and a
class that picks it studies the two together, in one module, because a
state exam authority set the word AND between them.

## The door

The document is the HSC English prescriptions for 2019 to 2026, issued by the NSW
Education Standards Authority. The first thing to say about it is that it is a state
document: in Australia the enforceable named-text list belongs to the state, not the
federation. Victoria's authority publishes its own annual text list for its own exam;
what follows is New South Wales standing in for a federation — the same pattern as
federal Germany, where the absence of one national list is not a gap in the evidence but
the evidence.

Inside the state, the choosing is pushed down again. The foreword asks that teachers
selecting specific texts consider the ethos of the school and its local community. A row
in this table is not something a pupil will meet; it is something a pupil's teacher may
offer.

And there is an age line above this door worth drawing before anything else. At
fifteen — the age this book measures every other country at — Australia names no book.
The national curriculum for those years asks for categories: wide-ranging Australian and
world authors, First Nations texts, different cultures and times. The state syllabus adds
quantities — so many novels, so many films — and exactly one proper noun: at least one
Shakespeare play, title unnamed. The entire named canon of an Australian fifteen-year-old
is one surname. The 140-title menu opens two years later, in elective senior courses, for
the minority who have chosen literature as a path. What follows is not a generation's
canon but the specialists' shelf.

The rest of the mechanics can be short, because only one of them matters here. What the
state requires is a pattern, not a set of titles — a few texts, one from each of a few
set categories, filled from menus — so 140 is the length of the menu, never of anyone's
reading year. The module that holds the Algiers pair offers seven pairs; a class studies
one. And a pair does not come apart: the word AND printed between Camus and Daoud means
a class choosing either novel studies both; the choice is of the pair, never of one book.

## What is on the shelf, and in what language

The subject is called English, and that name does most of the border work before any
title is chosen. Adiga, Ishiguro, Lahiri, Tóibín, Zadie Smith, Langston Hughes: not one
needed a translator to reach a Sydney desk. Of the 140 rows, 55 are Australian, 85
arrive from elsewhere, and 77 of those 85 were written in English to begin with — which
leaves eight rows, in the whole table, from another language. Kafka and Fritz Lang from
German, Guevara and Guillermo del Toro from Spanish, Calvino from Italian, Ibsen from
Norwegian — and, from French, the Algiers pair: the answered book and its answer.

Against that, the door carries two written selection requirements, repeated across its
course lists. Across the senior years, the selection of texts must give students
experience of texts widely regarded as quality literature, including a range of literary
texts written about intercultural experiences and the peoples and cultures of Asia; and
of a range of Australian texts, including texts by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander
authors and texts giving insight into diverse Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander
experience. Neither clause carries a number.

Both leave marks on the table, and the marks are different shapes. Thirteen rows are
coded to a subject naming an Asian country, city or people: Adiga's Bangalore,
Ishiguro's postwar Japan, Mistry's Bombay, Thien's Cultural Revolution, Yousafzai's
Pakistan, and — written from the Australian side of the border — the arrival stories of
Nam Le and Alice Pung. Twelve rows are coded to Indigenous Australia, and every one is
Australian: Noonuccal, Tara June Winch, Melissa Lucashenko, Linda Burney, Noel Pearson
among them. Asia sits on this shelf as subject matter, written from both sides of the
border; Indigenous Australia sits on it as home.

One row bends the pattern hard enough to be worth stopping on. Oodgeroo Noonuccal appears
twice — the only author with two rows in that Indigenous count — her second entry a set of
seven poems: "The Past," then "China…Woman," "Reed Flute Cave," "Entombed Warriors,"
"Visit to Sun Yat-Sen Memorial Hall," "Sunrise on Huampu River," "A Lake Within a Lake."
Six of the seven come out of her 1984 visit to China. What those titles afford a pupil is
an Aboriginal poet set for study as a traveller — looking outward from the country, not
being looked at. The window opens the other way.

## The errand: what the pair affords

The comparison module has its own teaching language, and it is unusually candid about
what the comparing is meant to produce. Students explore how comparing texts can reveal
resonances and dissonances between them and within them, and consider the ways a
reimagining of one text might "mirror, align or collide" with the details of another.

*Mirror, align or collide.* Six of the seven pairs I read as chiefly mirroring — Al
Pacino documenting a staging of *Richard III*, Jane Campion filming Keats's last love,
Stephen Daldry filming Woolf writing *Mrs Dalloway*, Margaret Atwood rewriting *The
Tempest* inside a prison theatre, the canon in conversation with its own admirers — and
the seventh as the collision.

Daoud's narrator is Musa's brother, older now,
drinking in Oran, addressing a stranger about a famous book in which his brother is a
corpse without a name. The novel he is angry at is the other novel on the same page of
the same list, in the same module, examined together.

What the pairing affords is not French existentialism, which Camus alone would supply. It
is the experience of reading a masterpiece and then reading the person it stepped over —
a canon being audited by the place it was set in. And the pupil who does that sits in a
state whose own prescribed shelf includes *The Secret River*, a stage adaptation about
dispossession on the Hawkesbury. Both books can go on one desk. That is what the list
allows; it is not what the list is *for*.

## The book nobody prescribed

There is a second English course in this state, for students learning English as an
additional language or dialect, with its own shelf of 22 rows. Its module on language,
identity and culture centres explicit English study on the Australian vernacular, its
idioms and its colloquialisms. On that same page sit two rows worth reading side by side:
an anthology called *Contemporary
Asian Australian Poets*, and Langston Hughes — seven poems, including "Theme for English
B," in which a young Black student, the only one of his kind in the class, is told to go
home and write a page that will be true. A module built on the Australian vernacular
offers a pupil a poem about being asked, in an English class, to produce something honest
on demand.

Which is close to what the state asks of all of them. Every pupil in the state's
Standard, Advanced, English Studies and EAL/D courses must also study one related text.
The document names no list for it, no category, no title.

So the true end of this 140-row table is not a row. In a document that specifies
publishers, editions and ISBNs, and that pauses to record how many pages one Wordsworth
edition runs to, the one textual slot required in each of these four courses is the one
the document declines to name.

A man on a beach outside Algiers got a name because a novelist in Oran wrote it down. A
class in Sydney may read both books at one desk, because a state authority printed the
word AND between them.

# The United States: The Book You Bring Yourself

Picture a sitting of the kind the College Board's own sample materials describe — a
sample, not a room anyone observed. Three essays, in a fixed order: a poem, a passage of
prose, and then a third question that stops naming books.

The first two hand the student their texts. The third hands over a concept — a character
given a gift that is also a burden — and then does what no ministry in this book does: it
tells the student to choose a work of fiction, either from their own reading or from the
list printed below. That list is a column of titles offered the way a waiter offers bread;
the student may take one or ignore them all. Then, with a recommended forty minutes to
spend on it, they write in the title of the book they intend to be examined on.

That blank is the American window, and it is cut one reader at a time.

Nobody fills it in from above — but not because nobody chooses. There is no federal
ministry with power to name a book, no national exam codifier, no enforceable list of the
kind that anchors every other chapter here; what stands in their place is selection pushed
down and dispersed. The exam question comes from a course and exam description published by
the College Board, a private organization, which tells schools that the works themselves are
selected locally. The titles come from somewhere else entirely: an appendix to
academic standards that states adopted one at a time, whose own work group made the final
choice of what it printed. Between them the two documents hold the two things a codifier
holds — force, and titles — and neither holds both. The exam has force over a score and
names no required book. The appendix names sixty-three titles for the ninth- and
tenth-grade band and denies, in its own front matter, being a list at all.

## The door that says it is not a door

The appendix is called *Text Exemplars and Sample Performance Tasks*, and its opening page
explains what an exemplar is for: the samples are there mainly to show the level of
complexity and quality that the standards require students in a grade band to engage with.
Then the writers close the door they appeared to be opening. "They expressly do not
represent a partial or complete reading list."

Take the disclaimer at face value. All sixty-three titles, stories through technical
subjects, are printed as exemplars, not assigned, and none carries a line to learn by heart.
Not one of them is a book an American fifteen-year-old must read. They are specimens of a
difficulty level, chosen to show teachers what that level feels like in the hand. The
criterion of admission the document states is complexity, quality and range. The criterion
it does not state is nationality.

The other document draws its one boundary the same way. By the end of the course, the AP
description says, students should have studied a variety of texts by diverse authors across
a range of periods, from the English Renaissance to the present. A real boundary — and a
boundary of period. Both American doors specify a shape and leave the passport unchecked.

So the interesting question is not what this country admits. It is what the shelf looks
like when the choosing is dispersed to a work group, districts, schools and teachers.

## What walks in

The answer, first, is an inheritance. By this book's own count — the appendix itself tags
no author with a nationality — forty-one of the sixty-three titles are domestic,
and of the twenty-two that are not, eleven never met a translator — Shakespeare twice,
Donne, Shelley, Housman; Achebe's *Things Fall Apart*, Fugard's *"Master Harold"…and the
boys*, Zusak's *The Book Thief* — most of them British, the family books that came over
with the language.

The eleven that did meet a translator are old, European and few, and the appendix names
the glass as well as the view: Fagles for Homer, Ronald Wilks for Gogol's barber who finds
a nose in his breakfast roll, Constance Garnett for Turgenev, Derek Prouse for Ionesco.
That is more than a citation habit — the complexity these rows exemplify is the complexity
of an English page a named translator made. Among the translated eleven, nothing comes
from Asia, from Latin America, from the Arabic-speaking world; Africa arrives on this
shelf twice, already in English.

Several of the foreign rows carry a state consuming its people, none of it American — the
Holocaust, the neighbours turning into rhinoceroses under 1930s fascism, apartheid South
Africa. A lean, not a law; and the South African row is where this shelf does its
strangest choosing.

## What a foreign book is for here

Every other document in this book eventually says something to a teacher about a foreign
work: study this alongside that, note the ties to our own culture, learn these lines by
heart. The teaching language this appendix attaches to a named work is its sample
performance tasks — illustrations, its front matter says, of how the standards apply to
exemplars already chosen — and each one closes with a standards code in square brackets.

Odysseus, the man of twists and turns, appears in a task that asks students to analyze how
he reflects conflicting motivations through his dealings with other characters, and how
those conflicting loyalties develop the poem's themes. Then the code, naming the standard
being exercised. What the task asks of the *Odyssey* is that one numbered skill be
practised on it. Two tasks later *Macbeth*
appears as raw material — students analyze how the Japanese filmmaker Akira Kurosawa, in
*Throne of Blood*, draws on and transforms the play to develop a similar plot set in feudal
Japan. In these tasks an admitted foreigner's errand is to be an instance of an
operation. Not a feeling a country cannot source domestically; a skill it can be shown
performing.

And yet the appendix does something no standard requires. It prints excerpts, and somebody
chose them; the front matter concedes the awkwardness, granting that where excerpts appear
they serve only as stand-ins for the full text.

The stand-in chosen for Fugard is not the scene the play is famous for. *"Master
Harold"…and the boys* ends with a white schoolboy spitting in the face of the Black man who
raised him. What the appendix prints instead is Sam, earlier, trying to explain ballroom
dancing to the boy: none of us knows the steps, there is no music playing, and it does not
stop with us. "America has bumped into Russia, England is bumping into India, rich man
bumps into poor man." Those are big collisions, he says. They make for a lot of bruises.

A South African play about a South African injury, excerpted at the exact half-page where
it turns and names America first in a list of collisions. The choice is an editorial act
and a visible one.

## The list nobody collects

What an American pupil actually reads is set locally, by districts, schools and teachers
— the kind of choosing that lives in a board meeting, a department vote, a publisher's
catalogue. That is the American arrangement: selection everywhere, and no single national
address for it.

Which leaves the exam room. Every other window in this book can be photographed: a
codifier you can print, a program with page numbers, a spine a pupil carries in a bag. The
American window has to be reconstructed one answer at a time, and no document in this
chapter ever collects those answers into a list. What the country shows instead is the act
itself: a student, a concept, a recommended forty minutes — and a hand writing in the
title of the book they brought themselves.

# England: The Door Opens Inward

There is a pot in Manchester. It was made in Nigeria, a museum in that city holds it, and a poet
from that city wrote a whole poem talking to it — not about it, to it. The poem was commissioned
for the museum and written as a conversation with a Nigerian pot in the collection.

Since 2023 that poem has been available to English classrooms as a set text, and in summer 2025
the first cohort sat an exam that could ask them about it — closed book, nothing on the desk but
what the paper supplies. The pot is the only thing in this story that crossed a border.

Not one text on the list behind that exam arrived in translation. Measured through the list, the
window England opens on world literature is shut — and yet the room is full of elsewhere.

## The door

There is no ministry behind the list. AQA is an exam board, one of several in England, each
writing its own menu of books to the same assessment objectives — so this is not England's shelf
but one shelf in England, in one year, with nothing to say for Scotland, Wales or Northern
Ireland.

And it is a menu, not a mandate. A school picks one play from six, one nineteenth-century novel
from seven, one modern text from twelve, and one cluster of fifteen poems — all fifteen studied,
because the exam may ask about any of them. By the time a teenager walks into the room, all that
choosing has narrowed to eighteen set texts.

Two more sentences shape the door. Every assessment is closed book, with any stimulus material
supplied by the paper itself. And the aim the specification writes for itself is that students
should come to appreciate the depth and power of the English literary heritage. Hold that aim
beside a list with no translated book on it, and the shelf's Englishness starts to look less like
an omission than like the subject's own definition.

## What comes through it anyway

Sixteen of the works on this shelf are tagged in this book's records with a specific place
outside Britain and Ireland — a tally that rests on interpretive labels, and an undercount, since
more works carry foreign material without naming a place. The elsewhere arrives in three unlike
ways.

**Elsewhere as stage.** Of the six Shakespeare plays a school can set, not one is set in England.
They go to Verona, Venice, Messina, Rome, eleventh-century Scotland — and *The Tempest* to a
colonial elsewhere with no country to its name; the record keeps a question mark there. Add Browning's
Ferrara, and a pattern appears: England's most domestic authors do their thinking abroad. The
passport is the author's, never the setting's.

**Wars with a foreign address.** Tennyson takes the Crimea in 1854; Owen the Western Front;
Orwell the Russian revolution; Armitage the 2003 invasion of Iraq; Duffy a photographer's
cold-war conflict zones; Rumens an exile from a homeland left unnamed. Britain was a belligerent
in the Crimean War, on the Western Front and in Iraq; the allegory, the photographs and the exile
sit elsewhere, or somewhere never named. The sharpest is Beatrice Garland's *Kamikaze*, an
English poem living inside the family of a Japanese pilot; Imtiaz Dharker's *A Century Later*
does the same for the 2012 Taliban attack on a schoolgirl — a foreign war, carried in an English
poem. Both count as domestic, because both are English poems.

**The world as citizens.** The newest texts grew this pile. A poetry cluster called Worlds and
Lives — taught from 2023, examined for the first time in 2025 — puts fifteen poems in front of a
class, and several of the new ones reach migration, heritage or elsewhere through people living
in Britain and their families. A conversation on an afternoon train from Purley to Victoria in
1955. Leeds in 1869, a great-great-grandfather with eyes dry from dust after twelve hours of
combing flax, imagined by a descendant born and raised in the same city. Guyana revisited and found full
of car tyres, plastic bottles and styrofoam cups. Trinidad carried in a pocket.

The modern-text list moved the same way: out went Bennett, Stephens and Ishiguro; in came
Odimba's *Princess & The Hustler*, on the 1963 Bristol bus boycott, de Waal's *My Name is Leon*,
and Pinnock's *Leave Taking*. The board's taster booklet for the Pinnock prints the scene it
wants taught, a daughter turning on her mother: "You're too busy bowing and scraping to your
beloved England. And where's it got you?"

England's classroom did not import that line. It grew it.

## One pot, one collection

The teacher guide published alongside the poetry cluster walks through the pot poem and says
plainly what it is for: the pot is used to explore cultural repatriation, colonialism and
migration. The speaker knows only half the story of how it reached England. The guide works
through the euphemisms a museum label might use and is blunt about where they land — the white
boat sailing to England, it says, reminds us of the truth of cultural theft. Then the poem turns
and speaks to the pot about itself, tells it that it is not really one of us, and asks it, twice,
to answer.

So here is what this window imports. Not the pot: it stays in the museum's collection, and what
reaches a classroom is a poem about it — a poem the guide reads as staged at the display glass.
Not a foreign literature either: a set of questions about a foreign object and whether it should
be here, asked in English by a poet the guide prints in lower case, shamshad khan, described
there as a second-generation British Asian from Manchester, writing for a museum in her own city.

That is the mechanism in miniature. The world is admitted constantly, and admitted as content; it
clears customs in the hands of a writer who already belongs. Printed at the start of every AQA
mark scheme is a statement of what the subject values: high-quality English literature displays
recognisable literary qualities and, though shaped by particular contexts, transcends them and
speaks to the universality of the human condition. The board never offers that as a reason for
setting no translated text — but hold the sentence against the shelf and the two fit. If
literature transcends its context, a menu can stay entirely English-language without its
compilers conceding a gap. A course can reach Guyana, the Pacific and a Nigerian pot without
setting a single translated page.

## No book on the desk

And in summer 2025, for the first time, a candidate of fifteen or sixteen could open the paper
and find that poem printed on it. Closed book: no anthology, no notes, nothing on the desk the
paper did not supply. The poem to set beside it came from memory, along with everything else the
subject had grown at home — Verona and the Crimea, a Bristol bus and a Guyana strewn with
styrofoam — all of it English literature, none of it translated.

An exam is not a bad place for the pot's poem. It is a room where somebody has to answer, on
behalf of something brought here and never asked. The poem asks twice — *growl if you hear me* —
and then, each word alone on its line, *pot? pot?*
