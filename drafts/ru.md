# Russia: The Staircase

A Russian child enters fifth grade at eleven, and somewhere in Moscow a document already
knows, year by year, which foreigners they are going to meet on the way up.

At eleven, the world arrives as story: the myths of the world's peoples, required;
Andersen's Snow Queen or another of his tales; a choice of Carroll or Tolkien; two
works from a childhood shelf holding Twain, London and Bradbury; two adventures with
Stevenson as the printed example. At twelve it gets serious: Homer, in fragments,
required; then Defoe's island, required; then Swift's giants and academies, required.
At thirteen, Cervantes — the knight himself, chapters of him — and the Little Prince,
whole. At fourteen, Shakespeare twice: a sonnet or two of the pupil's choosing, and
Romeo and Juliet, required, with Molière's bourgeois gentleman for comedy. And at
fifteen, the year this book measures every country at, the staircase reaches its
landing: Dante at the gates of the afterlife, Hamlet on the battlements, Faust in his
study, Byron's Childe Harold on the road — all four required — with two further slots
where a pupil picks a Byron lyric and a teacher picks Hoffmann, Hugo or Scott.

That is a real window, and it is not the one Russia is usually judged by. Judge it by
the exam and you would conclude the window barely exists: the grade-nine examination
codifier compresses all foreign literature into a single choice line, a parenthesis
holding Homer, Cervantes, Shakespeare and Molière as examples. For a while that
parenthesis was this book's Russian finding. It was the wrong document. The exam is the
door out of school; the window is on the staircase, and the staircase is written down
in the federal working program for literature — directly binding, since September 2023,
on every organization teaching a state-accredited basic general education program. The
program is not advice. It is the floor plan.

So Russia turns out to run a large mandatory foreign shelf — twelve required foreign
rows across the five grades, before any teacher makes any choice — and a narrow one.
Walk the fixed, named core of the ladder and look at who is on it. Homer is ancient
Greece; Dante is the Middle Ages; Cervantes, Shakespeare and Molière the sixteenth and
seventeenth centuries; Defoe and Swift the eighteenth; Goethe and Byron the early
nineteenth. The newest author on that named mandatory core is Saint-Exupéry, and he is
there as a fable. The choice shelves widen the picture — Twain, London, Bradbury,
Harper Lee, O. Henry, Cooper wait in the optional slots, so the named shelf as a whole
is Europe plus North America — but no named author from Asia, Africa or Latin America
appears anywhere in the twenty-six foreign rows; those worlds enter only anonymously,
in grade five's myths and tales of the world's peoples. The required window faces in
one direction: the European classics, its named core ending roughly where the
twentieth century begins. Russia's program does not import the world. It imports the
canon of Europe, as a stated inheritance.

And it imports it in pieces. Look again at how the required foreigners arrive: Homer in
fragments; Defoe and Swift in selected chapters; Cervantes in chapters; Shakespeare's
tragedy in fragments of the pupil's or teacher's choosing; Dante in at least two
fragments; Faust in at least two; Childe Harold in at least one. Ten of the eleven
named required foreign works carry that stamp — fragments, chapters, by choice. Now
look at the domestic shelf beside them in grade nine: Griboyedov's Woe from Wit, whole.
Pushkin's Eugene Onegin, whole. Lermontov's A Hero of Our Time, whole. Gogol's Dead
Souls, whole. The program's own arithmetic of attention is visible on the page: the
world is met in excerpts, the homeland in full texts. A fifteen-year-old finishes the
year having stood briefly at four of European literature's summits and having lived
inside four Russian books.

The one exception to the fragment stamp is worth a close look, because it is the
program's most tender act of admission. The single foreign work a Russian child is
required to read whole, cover to cover, is The Little Prince — at thirteen, filed by
this table under loneliness and friendship, a fable about a small person responsible
for one rose on one planet. Everything else from abroad arrives as a monument to be
visited in fragments; the one whole foreign book is the one about caring for something
because you have tamed it. That the ladder's builders required tenderness entire and
Dante in excerpts is, on this essay's reading, the most human sentence the document
never quite says.

Then there is the question of what the body keeps. The program orders memorization the
way a codifier never could: a pupil finishing grade nine must be able to recite at
least twelve poetic works not learned in earlier years, and for certain named works the
by-heart instruction is attached directly to the unit — the medieval Lay of Igor's
Campaign (domestic-coded in this table, as the program places it), Lomonosov's ode on
the empress's accession, Woe from Wit, Dead Souls. Every work carrying that instruction
is on the domestic shelf. The four required foreigners of grade nine — Dante,
Shakespeare, Goethe, Byron — carry no memorization instruction at all. In Kazakhstan,
this book found a program whose every by-heart mandate pointed at foreign, mostly
Russian, texts; in Ukraine, at foreign texts inside a subject built for them. Russia is
the mirror: the world is for reading; the memory is for what is Russia's own. On this
essay's reading — and it is a reading, the program states no such doctrine — the
staircase teaches a European inheritance to the mind and a national one to the body.

At the top of the staircase stands the exam, and the two documents part company. Five
years of differentiated requirement — this Dante, that Hamlet, those chapters of
Cervantes — collapse, in the grade-nine examination codifier, into one undifferentiated
choice line where Homer, Cervantes, Shakespeare and Molière appear as examples. The
exam does not exclude the world; it stops guaranteeing any particular piece of it. The
program proves what is taught; the codifier proves what is promised at the door out of
school; and the gap between them is the finding — this book can show the gap and cannot
explain it, because neither document explains itself.

If your schooling ran on this staircase, or on one of its Soviet-built cousins, you may
recognize the sensation from inside: the foreign classics as a sequence of guided
excursions — brief, chaperoned, in fragments — and the domestic classics as the house
you actually lived in, whose rooms you can still walk with your eyes closed, because
whole monologues of it were set into you at fifteen. The window on the staircase was
real. But the door of the house locks from the inside, and the key was learned by
heart.

One honesty paragraph to close. This is the federal program as printed for 2024, a
one-year slice: it proves what the state requires to be taught, not what any classroom
did with it, and the fragments-by-choice stamps mean two pupils on the same staircase
may have met quite different pieces of the same Dante. The exam contrast uses the
codifier only for what it is — the narrower, older instrument this chapter's first
version mistook for the whole window. And the reading of memory against inheritance is
this essay's, offered for inspection; the program mandates lists, hours and recitations,
and gives reasons for none of them.

## Limits

- One document, one year, teaching not uptake: the program mandates content; no
  classroom observation, textbook, or exam-performance data is in this corpus.
- Grades 5–8 domestic rows were deliberately not extracted; no claim above depends on
  them, and no whole-ladder domestic/foreign share is stated anywhere.
- `subject_era` is the compiler's coding (7 values adjusted in reconciliation); the
  Europe-facing characterization rests on author identities and the program's own
  section structure, not on era statistics.
- The memory-vs-inheritance reading and the staircase framing are the essay's;
  admission never motive, here as everywhere.
