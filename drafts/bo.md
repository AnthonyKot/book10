# Bolivia: A Cast, Not a Reading List

The last thing Bolivia's literature unit asks a fifteen-year-old to do is not read
anything. It is to put on a play about a period:

> Inspired by the study of literary currents, let us stage a short dramatization about one
> of them… In the dramatization, present an author and his literary production.
> — *Texto de aprendizaje*, 4th year of secondary, p. 29

So somebody has to be the author. Four pages earlier the book has supplied the cast list —
five names for the eighteenth century, printed one to a line, each with a country stamped
after it like a passport:

> Jean de la Fontaine. France. "Fables"
> Félix María Samaniego. Spain. "Fables"
> Daniel Defoe. Spain. "Robinson Crusoe"
> Jonathan Swift. Ireland. "Gulliver's Travels" Kant.
> Germany. "Critique of Pure Reason"
> — p. 25, translated; the line breaks are the book's own

Two of those lines are worth stopping on before anyone gets into costume. Daniel Defoe is
Spanish here. And Kant is standing in a line of literary works, holding the *Critique of
Pure Reason*, waiting to go on with the fabulists.

That is Bolivia's window: not a shelf of books to be read but a company of foreigners to be
impersonated, and the state printed the roster itself.

## The door is an object

Most doors in this corpus are lists — a codifier of what an examiner may ask, a
specification of set texts, a ministry program naming units. Bolivia's door is a book. One
physical volume, the learning text for the fourth year of secondary education, marked
"official text 2024" above the Minister of Education's name, and at the foot of the same
page, in capitals: *the sale of this document is prohibited*. It is not sold. It is issued.

It is also everything at once. The same covers carry the endocrine glands at page 216 and
the War of the Pacific at page 408. World literature occupies pages 25 to 36 — five pages
of tables and a few pages of extracts, which is the whole of it.

How hard does this door close? Not hard at all, and that has to be said before any name is
read off it. Every one of the twenty-nine rows this corpus codes from the volume is
anthology-only; not one is coded to an exam, not one carries a memorization mandate. The
book disclaims itself in its own foreword, calling its contents an element that energizes
learning, one that "can always be widened, deepened and contextualized from the experience
and reality of each cultural, social and educational setting" (p. 5). And it points upward
to an instrument this corpus does not hold: its contents follow the Plans and Programs
established for each educational level. There is a door above this door, and it was not
retrieved. A name printed here is present. It is not thereby required.

## The cast list

The unit sits under a heading that would be hard to invent: *Promotion of gender equity in
harmony with Mother Earth in the study of literary currents* (p. 24). Beneath it, six
numbered currents, and for each a ruled table whose final row is headed **"Emblematic works
and representative authors."** Twenty of the twenty-five foreign rows in this corpus are
names harvested from those cells, and that heading is the instruction for how to read them:
*emblematic*. A name in that cell is not a book assigned; it is evidence offered that a
period existed — which is exactly what a dramatization needs, and not at all what a reading
list is.

Read the cast in order and it walks in a direction. Neoclassicism is filed as France,
eighteenth century; Romanticism as Germany, nineteenth; Costumbrismo as Spain; Realism as
France, Spain, England, Mexico; Naturalism as France (pp. 25–28). Five currents, all
arriving from across the Atlantic. Then the compass swings west — Indigenismo, "Latin
America, twentieth century" — and the sixth current says out loud what the swing means:

> It stands out from the rest because it was born for the first time in America and is not
> an imitation of any other current. On the contrary, this movement influenced from America
> as far as Europe. — p. 29

Modernismo is the one current the book describes as born here and exported. And it is in
those last two cells, and in none of the five before them, that a Bolivian name stands as an
equal instance of the same thing as a foreign one: Ricardo Jaimes Freyre beside Rubén Darío
and José Martí; Alcides Arguedas and Jesús Lara beside Ciro Alegría of Peru. For five
currents the country is the audience. For two it is a member of the company.

Two entries are miscast in ways the document never notices. Defoe, tagged Spain. And Andrés
Bello, sole representative author of Costumbrismo — the current about local custom —
introduced not by a novel but as "Simón Bolívar's teacher", holding a treatise on the law of
nations (p. 26). The corpus codes that row as an essay. Whatever a pupil dramatizing
Costumbrismo would be performing, it is closer to a founding than to a fiction.

One more thing about this company, set against the heading over its head. Of the twenty-five
foreign rows, twenty-four name a man and one names no author at all. The only woman named
anywhere among these twenty-nine rows is Bolivian, and she stands five pages past the last
table, outside the currents entirely — Mónica Paulina Gutiérrez Jiménez, credited on p. 34
for a free adaptation of an Ayoreo legend, printed as a spelling exercise. The table records
placement. It does not record why.

## Shakespeare comes on loan

If you want to see the whole mechanism at once, look at what happens when the book stops
naming authors and actually prints one. On p. 30 a *Hamlet* fragment appears under the
instruction that the class will read it aloud "with expressiveness" — one of the best-known
texts in the history of world theatre, the book says. The heading above it reads, exactly as
printed, *"(Adapted fragment) Author: Hamlet William Shakespeare."* The source line credits
not an edition of the play but a web article explaining what dramatic texts are. The two
questions underneath are: have you heard of William Shakespeare, and do you find the
situation sad or funny?

Adapted, mis-headed, sourced from a free web page, set to be read aloud with expression, in
a volume nobody is permitted to sell. That is not neglect; it is the same logic as the cast
list. The world's literature is here to be performed and recognized, not studied from an
edition.

## The errand: a scruple, six pages later

The clearest imported feeling in this table is not on the literature pages at all. It is on
p. 36, under a heading about radio's importance in the struggle against social inequality,
where an extract from José Ignacio López Vigil's *Passion for Radio* argues against treating
a microphone as a weapon:

> For these chiefs, the microphones were cannons that fired ideas… A sad confusion of
> registers. Because on military ground one conquers. But on the ground of ideas one
> convinces. — p. 36

He keeps going: radio in the service of big capital bombards people daily with news that
misinforms — "they practise media terrorism. But we cannot and do not want to play that same
game, even in reverse. Out of ethics."

Where the tables import periods, this page imports a scruple: the difference between winning
and convincing, handed to a fifteen-year-old as a working rule. And the reflection question
printed under the last table asks the same kind of thing of the literature — pick a current
and a present-day problem, and say how we would contribute to solving it "through the
literature of that current" (p. 29). Not what the literature is like. What it can be put to.

## The object

Some readers were handed a textbook and some were sold one, and those are two different
childhoods. If you were handed yours — stamped, free, unsellable, the same volume all year
— you know that such a book is not a shelf but a fact, the way a room is a fact. Bolivia's
fifteen-year-olds turn to page 25 of theirs and find the literature of the world laid out as
a company of players: a Frenchman with fables, an Irishman with a giant's country, a German
philosopher holding the *Critique of Pure Reason* as though it were a novel, an Englishman
wearing Spain's flag by a printer's slip. Then they are asked to choose one and act him.

It is a small window and a strange one, and it is the one the state printed itself.

## Limits

- **An anthology is not a mandate.** All 29 rows are `anthology-only`; nothing here shows
  that any Bolivian pupil must read, or can be examined on, any of these works. No row
  count above is offered as a measure of openness, and none is comparable with a codifier
  or exam-specification country's.
- **The door above this door was not retrieved.** The volume defers to the Plans and
  Programs for each level; those are not in `resources/`, so what force any of this carries
  in a classroom is outside the evidence.
- **One volume, one year, one grade.** Fourth year of secondary, 2024 edition. Nothing here
  describes what a Bolivian pupil reads across a school career, and no reform history is
  receipted — the corpus holds no earlier Bolivian volume.
- **`subject_era` is the compiler's reading**, recoded six times in reconciliation; the
  groupings above rest on it, and it is not a ministry category.
- **The dramatization reading is this essay's.** The document sets the task and prints the
  cells; it nowhere says the names are there to be performed rather than read. That the
  cast list and the staging task belong to the same unit is a fact of placement; the
  connection drawn between them is mine.
- **Motive is absent.** The gender-equity heading over a company of twenty-four men, the
  misfiled nationalities, the compass swing at Indigenismo and Modernismo — all are facts of
  what is printed. The document offers no rationale, and none is inferred here.
- **Length.** ~2,150 words, inside the 2,200 cap.
