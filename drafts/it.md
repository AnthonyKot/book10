# Italy: A Poem Comes Home in Translation

A man walks out of a burning city with his father on his back and his son by the hand. His
wife is somewhere behind him in the smoke; he will turn around, and she will not be there.
He keeps moving for years, across a sea, past a queen he leaves, and when he finally stops
walking he is standing in Italy.

That poem is one of the works an Italian liceo's first two years prescribe. The ministry
calls it *l'Eneide* — the *Aeneid* — and files it inside a parenthesis of works marked "to
be read in translation." A poem written in Italy, in Latin, about the founding of Rome
enters the Italian classroom through the door reserved for foreign books. Not because
anyone doubts whose it is. Because the language it was written in is not the language of
the room.

## The door

The door is a ministerial decree of 7 October 2010: the *Indicazioni nazionali* for the
licei, issued by the Ministry of Education, Universities and Research. Not an exam list, not
a set-text list — a national statement of learning objectives, written in the future
indicative, where a row has the force of a verb in a sentence about what "the student" will
do rather than of a title an examiner may ask about.

This essay reads that decree as the saved text has it. Whether the 2010 wording still stands
unamended, and what an Italian classroom is working from in any later year, the saved pages
cannot establish; nothing below describes Italian schooling now.

And before the decree names anything at all, it says the naming is not the point: the
literary panorama will be described by "other authors and texts, besides those explicitly
mentioned, chosen in autonomy by the teacher."

So the ten rows this corpus draws from those pages are not ten assigned books. Four named
ancient works and one open pool of classical authors arrive in translation; four are
domestic — Manzoni, and three groupings of early Italian verse; one row is left open
between the two. Eight are coded mandatory, two as open formulas. None of the ten carries a
memorization mandate.

## What the parenthesis is for

The errand comes in the same sentence as the cargo. In the first two years the student
meets significant works and authors of classical antiquity, to be read in translation, "in
order to identify the principal features of the literary and cultural tradition, with
particular attention to works foundational for Western civilisation and rooted — perhaps
unawares — in the collective imagination, as it has settled over the course of the
centuries." Then the parenthesis opens: the Homeric poems, Attic tragedy of the fifth
century, the *Aeneid*, some other text by primary Greek and Latin authors, the Bible.

*Rooted, perhaps unawares.* The decree is not describing something new arriving. It is
describing something long settled, in a collective imagination the pupil stands inside
whether or not the pupil knows it — and the errand it states is to make that freight
identifiable.

Now open the book that phrase was written over. The *Aeneid* is a poem about precisely that
posture. Aeneas never chooses his errand; it is handed to him in a burning house, with an
old man on his shoulders and a child's hand in his, and he carries it across the
Mediterranean to a country he has not seen and cannot argue with. He is not the hero who
wants something. He is the man who was given something to carry and did not put it down.
What comes into the ninth-grade room with that book is not, in the end, a story about
Rome — it is the sensation of an inheritance that arrived before consent, the past as
something heavy and already on your back. The decree names the work and states the errand
and never mentions the poem's plot; the fit between them is this essay's reading, not the
ministry's claim.

The Bible sits in the same parenthesis, after a pagan epic, a tragic genre and a Roman epic,
under the same instruction to read in translation and inside the same description of works
called foundational. That placement establishes the errand the decree assigns it, and
nothing about what else the book is to anyone in the room.

## One clause, three timetables

The fourth item in the parenthesis stops naming and starts gesturing: "some other text by
primary Greek and Latin authors, especially in the Licei lacking classical disciplines." An
open pool of authors, an unspecified quantity of texts, and a target — the schools where
the ancients are not already on the timetable.

The word is *especially*, not *only*, and the three saved renderings show why that matters.
The Italian-literature paragraph runs through all three unchanged; what differs is what
stands around it. The classical liceo carries Latin and Greek as full subjects of their own.
The scientific liceo carries a Latin subject too, and a demanding one: the student is to
know the fundamental texts of Latin literature "through reading in the original and in
translation," starting as soon as possible from the comprehension-translation of original
passages, with Phaedrus and the Vulgate named as worked examples. The linguistic rendering
carries a Latin section addressed to the first two years — the fundamentals of the language,
author texts in translation with the original alongside — and no Greek subject at all.

None of the three, then, is a liceo with no classical discipline in it; which schools the
clause is aimed at, the saved pages do not say. The ministry writes one sentence, and it
comes to rest on a different timetable in each building.

## The blank

Now the other half of the sentence — the half where a modern foreign writer could enter.
"Alongside other readings from modern authors, foreign ones included," it runs, "the
student will read Manzoni's *I Promessi Sposi*."

That is the whole of it. No name, no country, no century inside the modern, no number of
books, and no requirement about origin: *foreign ones included* opens the pool without
obliging anyone to take from it. The corpus codes that row as a choice between domestic and
translated for exactly that reason. This is the finding for the first two years — the
decree opens a window onto the modern world's writing and declines to say whether anybody
comes through it.

A second door stands further off. Among the aims of the whole five years, the graduate is to
have "an adequate idea of the relations with the literatures of other countries, setting
readings of foreign authors alongside the reading of Italian authors, to be agreed if need
be with the teachers of Foreign Language and Culture." Foreign authors are called for there.
But that outcome is measured at the end of the five years; nothing in it puts one of those
books inside the two years this table covers, and even the agreement is conditional — a
conversation between colleagues that may or may not need to happen.

## The one surname

It is worth noticing what the parenthesis does with people. The ministry does not write
*Homer*; it writes "the Homeric poems." Not *Aeschylus*; "Attic tragedy of the fifth
century." Not *Virgil*; "the Aeneid." Down the whole passage the grammar is works, genres,
centuries, schools. The one human being called by surname is the Italian.

And where the decree names, it explains. Manzoni's reasons arrive in the same breath as his
title: the novel is prescribed "as a work that sums the artistic quality, the decisive
contribution to the formation of modern Italian, the exemplary realisation of the
novel-form, the breadth and variety of themes and of perspectives on the world." Four
reasons, one of them frankly linguistic — the book is partly on the desk because modern
Italian was made in it. Beside that, the modern foreign slot carries no reasons at all,
because it has no object to give reasons about. The decree sets the explained name and the
unexplained blank in a single sentence and never accounts for the difference; neither can
this table.

Past the band these rows cover, in the fifth year, the decree does name a foreigner — one.
Italian lyric poetry, it says, is "less reducible than ever to national boundaries" and
cannot but set out from Baudelaire. That row is not in this corpus and nothing above rests
on it; but the reason offered is the same kind the ancients get. He is named because the
Italian story cannot be told without him.

## The blank on the shelf

Most readers were handed a book once because a document said so, and another because a
particular adult chose it. The first kind keeps its shape for years. The second often has no
shape you can point to at all — no title on any list, nothing to look up later.

These pages leave Italy's first two years legible in exactly that way. The named shelf is
short and old: a Greek epic, an Attic stage, a Roman poem about arriving in Italy, the
Bible, one Lombard novel, three groupings of early Italian verse. Beside it stands one
clause wide enough to hold any modern writer at all, foreign or not, which the decree opens
and then hands over entire to the person at the front of the room. Whatever came through
that clause has no national shape. If it has a signature, the signature is a teacher's.

## Limits

- **This is a reading of the 2010 decree, not of Italy in any later year.** The table is
  filed as `it-2025` while `doc_year` is 2010. Nothing in `resources/` establishes that
  D.M. 211/2010 remained in force unamended after that date, and no later amendment was
  consulted. Read the label as a corpus convention; every claim above is a claim about what
  the saved decree says, and the essay is written that way throughout.
- **The saved source is not the ministry's PDF.** Every quotation is verbatim from a
  Wikisource transcription, not from the official gazette text or a MIUR PDF. Its page
  markers are reproduced faithfully but are the transcription's, and the transcription
  carries visible u-for-v OCR artifacts elsewhere on the page ("obiettiui", "diuersi"). No
  passage quoted above contains one; a check against the official text is still owed.
- **Two years, and only part of one school system.** The rows cover the *primo biennio*
  only, per this book's operational definition, coded from the classical-liceo rendering
  with selected strings checked in the scientific and linguistic renderings. The other liceo
  types were not examined; the technical and professional institutes have their own *linee
  guida*, absent here. The second biennio and fifth year name many authors, none in this
  table.
- **`mandatory` is a coding, not examinability.** The decree states objectives in the future
  indicative and the compiler read that as prescription. There is no national set-text list
  and no exam codifier behind these rows, and the *Esame di Stato* is not in this corpus. No
  count here is comparable with a country whose door is an exam list.
- **`choice-list` covers two unlike blanks** — an open pool of classical authors and an open
  pool of modern, possibly foreign ones. Neither is a menu with items on it.
- **The Aeneid reading is this essay's.** The decree assigns the work and states the errand;
  it says nothing about the poem's plot, and the correspondence drawn between them is
  interpretation, marked as such in the body.
- **No motive is available.** The decree gives reasons for Manzoni and none for leaving the
  modern foreign slot open. This table records the asymmetry and cannot explain it; nothing
  above is an account of ministerial intent, of how loosely the text was drafted, or of what
  any work is meant to mean beyond the errand the document states.
- **Length.** About 2,940 words by `wc -w` against the 2,200 cap; the essay itself, opening
  through lander, runs about 1,590. The overrun is evidence, not prose: the block of Italian
  originals that the English-first rule moves out of the body into Receipts, and the
  cross-liceo quotations behind the *especially in the Licei lacking classical disciplines*
  correction.
