# Australia: The Word Between Two Books

On a beach outside Algiers, a Frenchman shoots a man the novel never names. The book
calls him the Arab. He has a sister, a knife, and no line of dialogue; the rest of the
novel belongs to the shooter, tried less for the killing than for failing to weep at his
mother's funeral.

Year 12 students in New South Wales meet that beach — but only the ones whose class
picked the third pair, printed in a state document with the two texts stacked and a
single word between them:

> ▪ Camus, Albert, *The Stranger* (translated by Matthew Ward) …
> **AND**
> ▪ Daoud, Kamel, *The Meursault Investigation* …

The second book gives the dead man a name — Musa — and a brother who will not stop
talking about him. Camus died in 1960; the man who answered him was born ten years
later. In this table of 140 prescribed texts, that is the one pairing I read as a book
and its rebuttal both holding a row — and a class that picks it studies the two together,
in one module, because a state exam authority set the word AND between them.

## The door

The document is *HSC English Prescriptions 2019–2026*, issued by the NSW Education
Standards Authority, in its May 2024 update. It is a state document, and that is the
first thing to say plainly: in Australia the enforceable named-text list belongs to the
state, not the federation. The HSC is a New South Wales credential; Victoria's authority
publishes its own annual VCE text list for its own exam. What follows is New South Wales
standing in for a federation — the same finding-shape as Germany's Länder, where the
absence of one national list is not a gap in the evidence but the evidence.

Inside the state, the choosing is pushed down again. The foreword asks that "In selecting
specific texts for study, teachers should consider the ethos of the school and its local
community." A row in this table is not something a pupil will meet; it is something a
pupil's teacher may offer.

And there is an age line above this door that has to be drawn before anything is counted.
At fifteen — the age this book measures every other country at — Australia names no book.
The national curriculum for Years 9–10 asks for categories: wide-ranging Australian and
world authors, First Nations Australian texts, different cultures and times. The NSW
syllabus for the same years adds quantities — so many novels, so many films — and exactly
one proper noun: at least one Shakespeare play, title unnamed. That is the entire named
canon of an Australian fifteen-year-old: one surname. The 140-title menu below opens two
years later, in elective senior courses — Advanced, Extension — for the minority who have
chosen literature as a path. This table is therefore not a generation's canon. It is the
specialists' shelf, and everything counted from it below should be read that way.

The mechanics are worth being exact about, because everything else depends on them. An
Advanced student closely studies "FOUR PRESCRIBED TEXTS, one drawn from EACH of the
following categories" — Shakespearean drama, prose fiction, poetry or drama, and a fourth
that may be nonfiction, film or media. At least two more short texts come in for the
writing module, and they "do not contribute to the required pattern" of prescribed texts.
So the floor of the year is four plus two: at least six rows out of 140. A Standard
floor is five. The 140 is the length of the menu, never of anyone's reading year, and a
menu count and a mandate count are not on the same axis.

The menu-door works like this. Every Standard, Advanced and English Studies pupil does
one common module, whose list holds fourteen titles, of which they study one: Orwell's
*Nineteen Eighty-Four*, Miller's *The Crucible*, Doerr's *All the Light We Cannot See*,
Shakespeare's *The Merchant of Venice*, Slessor's poems, Winton's essays, a
reality-television series about asylum seekers. Fourteen doors into one room; each pupil
walks through one; the other thirteen are somebody else's year. The Advanced comparison
module offers seven pairs and takes one. Extension 1 spreads thirty rows across five
electives with titles like "Literary homelands" and "Worlds of upheaval."

## What is on the shelf, and in what language

The subject is called English, and that name does most of the border work before any
title is chosen. Of the 140 rows, 55 are Australian; the other 85 arrive from elsewhere,
and 77 of those 85 were written in English to begin with. Adiga, Ishiguro, Lahiri, Levy,
Tóibín, Mistry, Zadie Smith, Colum McCann, Langston Hughes: not one needed a translator
to reach a Sydney desk, and the corpus's schema is left calling them
`translated:english`, which here means only *not Australian*.

Eight rows in the whole table come from another language. Kafka and Fritz Lang from
German, Camus and Daoud from French, Guevara and Guillermo del Toro from Spanish, Calvino
from Italian, Ibsen from Norwegian. Two of the eight are films. Six are print, and two of
those six are the Algiers pair — joined by that AND, in a module where "Students choose a
pair", so that within this menu a class selecting either French novel studies both. The
second, on my reading, is a prosecution of the first.

Against that, the door carries two written selection requirements, and they appear
identically in the Standard, Advanced and EAL/D lists. Across Stage 6, the selection of
texts must give students experience of "texts which are widely regarded as quality
literature, including a range of literary texts written about intercultural experiences
and the peoples and cultures of Asia", and of "a range of Australian texts, including
texts by Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander authors and those that give insights
into diverse experiences of Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander peoples". Neither
clause carries a number.

Both leave marks on the table, and the marks are different shapes. Thirteen rows carry a
subject era naming an Asian country, city or people: Adiga's Bangalore, Forster's Raj,
Ishiguro's postwar Japan, Mistry's Bombay, Thien's Cultural Revolution, Baillie's
Tiananmen, Gavron's British-Bangladeshi London, Lahiri's Indian-American crossing,
Yousafzai's Pakistan, Noonuccal's China poems, and the arrivals written by Nam Le, Alice
Pung and Lily Chan — five of them `domestic`, Australian books about Asian pasts and
Asian-Australian arrivals. Twelve rows carry a subject era naming Indigenous Australia,
and every one is domestic: Noonuccal, Ali Cobby Eckermann, Tara June Winch, Melissa
Lucashenko, Rachel Perkins, Jane Harrison, Linda Burney, Noel Pearson among them. Asia
sits on this shelf as subject matter, written from both sides of the border; Indigenous
Australia sits on it as home. Nothing explains why the shapes differ, and the table
records placement, not reasons.

One row bends the pattern hard enough to be worth stopping on. Oodgeroo Noonuccal appears
twice — the only author with two rows in that Indigenous count — her second entry a set of
seven poems: "The Past," then "China…Woman," "Reed Flute Cave," "Entombed
Warriors," "Visit to Sun Yat-Sen Memorial Hall," "Sunrise on Huampu River," "A Lake
Within a Lake." Six of the seven come out of her 1984 visit to China. What those titles
afford a pupil is an Aboriginal poet set for study as a traveller — looking outward from
the country, not being looked at. The window opens the other way. The list prints the
titles; it does not print reasons.

## The errand: what the pair affords

The comparison module has its own teaching language, and it is unusually candid about
what the comparing is meant to produce. Students "explore the ways in which the
comparative study of texts can reveal resonances and dissonances between and within
texts", and consider "the ways that a reimagining or reframing of an aspect of a text
might mirror, align or collide with the details of another text", along with "common or
disparate issues, values, assumptions or perspectives".

*Mirror, align or collide.* Six of the seven pairs I read as chiefly mirroring: Al Pacino
documenting a staging of *Richard III*, Jane Campion filming Keats's last love, Stephen
Daldry filming Woolf writing *Mrs Dalloway*, Margaret Atwood rewriting *The Tempest*
inside a prison theatre — homages, elegies, adaptations, the canon in conversation with
its own admirers. That grouping is my reading, not a NESA label; the document sorts no
pair into a bucket.

The Algiers pair I read as a collision. Daoud's narrator is Musa's brother, older now,
drinking in Oran, addressing a stranger about a famous book in which his brother is a
corpse without a name. The novel he is angry at is the other novel on the same page of
the same list, in the same module, examined together.

What the pairing affords is not French existentialism, which Camus alone would supply. It
is the experience of reading a masterpiece and then reading the person it stepped over —
a canon being audited by the place it was set in. And the pupil who does that sits in a
state whose own prescribed shelf includes *The Secret River*, a stage adaptation about
dispossession on the Hawkesbury. Both books can go on one desk. That is what the list
allows; it is not what the list is *for*. The list proves the pairing, the module's
language proves the framing, and no statement of intention for this pair — or any pair —
appears in the document.

## The book nobody prescribed

There is a second English course in this state, for students learning English as an
additional language or dialect: EAL/D, with its own shelf of 22 rows. Its Module B,
Language, Identity and Culture, says that "Explicit, targeted English language study
centres on the Australian vernacular, idioms, colloquialisms and other forms of cultural
expression". On that same page sit two rows worth reading side by side: an anthology
called *Contemporary Asian Australian Poets*, and Langston Hughes — seven poems,
including "Theme for English B," in which a young Black student, the only one of his kind
in the class, is told to go home and write a page that will be true. A module built on
the Australian vernacular offers a pupil a poem about being asked, in an English class,
to produce something honest on demand.

Which is close to what the state asks of all of them. Every Standard, Advanced, English
Studies and EAL/D pupil "must also study ONE related text" — in the common module for the
first three, in Module A for EAL/D. The document names no list for it, no category, no
title.

So the true end of this 140-row table is not a row. In a document that specifies
publishers, editions and ISBNs, and that pauses to record how many pages one Wordsworth
edition runs to, the one textual slot required in each of these four courses is the one
the document declines to name.

You had a last year of school once, with a list someone had chosen before you walked in.
If your classroom ever let you bring a text of your own in beside the prescribed one —
what did you bring, and did anyone ask you why? A man on a beach outside Algiers got a
name because a novelist in Oran wrote it down; a class in Sydney may read both books at
one desk, because a state authority printed the word AND between them.

## Limits

- **One state, one document, one slice.** New South Wales, 2024, senior secondary — not
  Australia. Victoria, Queensland and the rest set their own texts, none of them in this
  corpus; read every sentence above about "Australia" as *the NSW door*. Presented
  deliberately as the federal finding-shape, as with Germany — but a federation described
  from one of its states is still one state.
- **Every row is `choice-list`, so no row is a mandate.** Nothing here is a text a NSW
  pupil must read; what is mandatory is a *pattern* — four categories for Advanced, three
  types for Standard, a Shakespeare among them — filled from menus. No row count above is
  offered as a measure of openness, and none is comparable to a mandated list elsewhere
  in the book.
- **No pupil meets this shelf.** At least six rows of 140 for an Advanced candidate. The
  table shows the range on offer and nothing about what any classroom read; the corpus
  holds no enrolment or teacher-selection data. Every sentence above about a class
  reading the Algiers pair is written as something a class *may* do.

- **The two selection requirements carry no number.** NESA says the selection "must give
  students experience of" ranges of texts — no quota, no threshold; the counts above
  measure the table, not compliance.
- **`translated:english` is a schema word, not a fact about translation.** For a country
  whose subject is English, 77 rows are marked "translated" from the language they were
  written in. The coding tracks *not domestic*; read it that way throughout.
- **`memorize` is `no` on all 140 rows, by rule.** The reconciliation pass required an
  explicit mandate to learn a named text by heart; this document contains no memorization
  clause at all. That absence is a property of the document, not evidence that NSW pupils
  learn nothing by heart.
- **Subject-era codings are the compiler's, and 21 changed in recon.** The shelf walk's
  groupings, including the Noonuccal reading, rest on that column — a coding of what each
  work represents, not NESA's categories. NESA prescribes by text type and module.
- **The mirror/collision sorting is the narrator's.** NESA offers "mirror, align or
  collide" and "common or disparate" as things for students to consider; it assigns no
  pair to a category, and this essay's six-and-one split is a reading offered for
  inspection, not a field in any table.
- **Motive is absent.** The Camus/Daoud pairing, the two selection requirements and the
  outward turn in the Noonuccal selection are all facts of placement. The document states
  general selection criteria — "merit and cultural significance" among them — but no
  rationale for any individual pairing was located, and none is inferred.
- **Length.** ~2,990 words by `wc -w` against the 2,200 cap; the story through the lander
  runs ~1,790. The overrun is apparatus, earned by two things this door demands and
  others do not: a menu of 140 choice-list rows needs its floor arithmetic shown (six of
  140, not 140), and the two compiler-coded subject-era counts had to be made
  reproducible row by row, since the shelf walk's groupings rest on them.
