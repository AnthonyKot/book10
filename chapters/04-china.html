Three Ways to Hand a Child the World · Chapter 04

China: The Mirror on the Shelf

Somewhere in the compulsory years of a Chinese schooling, a pupil is set a habit rather than a book. The curriculum standard requires that students "read more, read good books, read whole books," and for the long ones it offers a single example sentence. It is worth walking like a shelf, left to right.

Nine titles. The first four are Chinese: Wu Cheng'en's Journey to the West, Shi Nai'an's Water Margin, Lao She's Camel Xiangzi, then Luo Guangbin and Yang Yiyan's Red Crag. The last four are foreign: Swift's Gulliver's Travels, Charlotte Brontë's Jane Eyre, Gorky's Childhood, Ostrovsky's How the Steel Was Tempered. Between the halves, fifth of nine, stands Edgar Snow's Red Star Over China — a book translated from English whose subject is China: an account of China, written from outside it, in another language. A window is supposed to face outward. This one has been hung facing the room.

Of the printed line's five foreign-authored titles, three — Snow, Brontë, Ostrovsky — can be followed closely here; Jules Verne, printed on a different line of the same appendix, makes a fourth. A foreign shelf small enough to read closely.

A rule with two leaves

The defining document is the Compulsory Education Chinese Language Curriculum Standard, issued by China's Ministry of Education in April 2022. It governs one subject — Chinese language and literature — and it names books in two appendices, with unlike force.

The first leaf is recitation, and the window arrives there before any foreign book does, inside a subordinate clause of the sentence that establishes the mandate: students must recite from memory excellent poetry and prose ancient and modern — Chinese classical, Chinese modern and contemporary, and foreign — with the specific pieces left to textbook editors and teachers, and only 135 pieces of classical poetry and prose recommended underneath.

The clause opens a Chinese teenager's memory to the poetry of the world — and then the list beneath it names classical Chinese poetry and prose, sixty pieces in its lower-secondary stretch, not one of them foreign. The foreigners are all on the other leaf.

The second leaf is reading — suggestions for reading in and out of class. It opens by requiring the habit, then disclaims its own catalogue in the next paragraph: its recommendations are examples only. Neither leaf makes a named work examinable. Force in China is a named example inside a mandated practice: the state requires that reciting happen and that whole books be read, and leaves to editors and teachers which texts do the work.

The guests

All four foreign books sit on the reading leaf, under two of its headings.

Under popular-science and science-fiction works stands a single line naming two examples: A Hundred Thousand Whys, and Jules Verne's Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Sea. Verne enters not among the masterworks but on the line shared with popular science, and the filing speaks through what stands next to him: the submarine arrives as apparatus, a book admitted in the company of explanations.

Two of the other three stand on the masterworks line already walked. Charlotte Brontë supplies Jane Eyre, a nineteenth-century story of class and gender. Nikolai Ostrovsky supplies How the Steel Was Tempered, set in the Russian civil war — the one book on this shelf about a war fought outside China.

Ostrovsky's is the book worth opening, because the feeling it imports announces itself on the page, in the novel's most famous passage, the one that made it a school text across the Soviet world — life "is given to him once, and it must be lived so that there is no agonizing pain over years lived aimlessly." Not grief but resolve — a steelworks of the will, offered at the age when years first look spendable.

Edgar Snow, the mirror

And the fifth position. Snow is the one foreigner on the shelf not writing fiction: his book is reportage, its subject the Red Army and the Long March. Three things about it can be read closely.

First, the heading. The word the appendix uses for that line, 名著, is canon vocabulary — the established, famous work. The appendix keeps a separate line for works of poetry and prose, where Lu Xun's Dawn Blossoms Plucked at Dusk stands; Snow's book is on the masterworks line instead, wedged between the Chinese novels and the foreign ones: a book of reporting filed among the classics.

Second, the title. The Chinese does something the English gloss does not. Red Star Over China is a preposition — a star hanging in place. The Chinese title, 《红星照耀中国》, puts a verb in the middle: 照耀, to shine upon, to illuminate. The star acts, and the country is the grammatical object.

Third, an adjacency. The appendix's one stated direction about content — that teachers recommending supplementary reading should favor works reflecting revolutionary culture and advanced socialist culture — attaches to supplementary recommending in general, not to the masterworks line. But Snow's is the one foreign book on the shelf whose subject — the Red Army, the Long March — belongs to the history behind the culture that direction favors.

The inference here comes from a placement and a grammar; the ministry states no purpose. And still: a pupil who walks the printed line meets, in the middle of it, a foreign witness filed among the masters, whose Chinese title has the star illuminating the country itself. A window hung facing the room.

What is held, and what is only read

Not one of the sixty-nine named works is a mandated memory. The recitation leaf requires reciting in general, calls its pieces recommendations, and leaves the specific choices to editors and teachers.

But look where the two leaves point. The recitation shelf — Guan Ju, Mulan, Du Fu's spring, Wen Tianxiang passing Lingding Ocean — is Chinese without exception; the whole-book shelf is where the foreigners stand. On this national list, no foreign work is named for memory; the four this chapter follows are all named for reading. And the clause that opened recitation to foreign poetry and prose stands open, its filling handed to editors and teachers; a foreign poem may already be in Chinese children's mouths by the route the document describes and declines to name.

Any country's shelf of whole books can be put to the test this one teaches: which of the books came from outside, and which of those looked back at the country itself. Of the four foreign books this chapter follows, exactly one looks back — and the pupil who walks the state's example sentence meets it midway, fifth of nine, a foreign witness among the masters, holding the mirror up to the room.
