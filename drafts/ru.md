# Russia: Line Forty-Eight

The document that governs what a ninth-grader sitting the Russian literature exam can be asked
does not call itself a reading list, and it is careful not to be mistaken for one. It is a
*кодификатор* — a codifier — prepared by the federal state research institution **«Федеральный институт
педагогических измерений»** (FIPI) and published under the copyright of Rosobrnadzor, the
education-supervision service. Its opening page draws its own boundary: «В кодификатор
не включены требования к результатам освоения основной образовательной программы основного
общего образования и элементы содержания, достижение которых не может быть проверено
в рамках государственной итоговой аттестации» — "The codifier does not include requirements
for outcomes […] or content elements whose achievement cannot be checked within the framework
of the state final attestation" (p. 2; my renderings throughout, `[…]` an editorial omission).

So it is not an account of what Russian children read. It is an inventory of what an examiner
can lawfully put in front of them. Everything a teacher loves and a grader cannot grade lives
outside this text.

Inside it, Section 2 holds Table 3, «Перечень проверяемых элементов содержания» — the list of
checkable content elements — numbered 1 to 48. Line 1 is «Слово о полку Игореве», the
twelfth-century Lay. Line 48 reads, in full:

> «Произведения зарубежной литературы: по выбору (в том числе Гомера, М. Сервантеса,
> У. Шекспира, Ж.-Б. Мольера и др.)» — "Works of foreign literature: by choice (including
> Homer, M. Cervantes, W. Shakespeare, J.-B. Molière, et al.)" (Table 3, item 48)

That is the window: the last line of the table, and a parenthesis inside it. The word
*зарубежной* — foreign — is scarce in the codifier. It stands here, and it stands in the
requirement that a student be able to interpret «древнерусской, классической русской и зарубежной
литературы и современных авторов» — Old Russian, classical Russian, and foreign literature, and
contemporary authors (Table 2, item 12, p. 9). Those are the only places a string search over the
document's text finds it.

## What the door is made of

The codifier is built, it says, on two instruments: the federal state educational standard for
basic general education (order No. 287 of 31 May 2021) and the federal educational program (order
No. 370 of 18 May 2023, as amended). It has no force beyond that lineage; it converts a curriculum
into a set of things an exam item may be about.

The force of a row is therefore specific, and the document specifies it. «Если в кодификаторе
названо конкретное произведение, то задания по представленному элементу содержания могут быть
включены в любую часть экзаменационной работы» — if a specific work is named, tasks on it may
appear in any part of the paper. Where no work is named, the choice moves: «выбор осуществляет
экзаменуемый», the examinee chooses (p. 12).

Line 48 names no work. It names four writers as instances of a category. Its right-hand columns
put the material in the programs of grades 5–9 — the widest span in the table, shared only with
Pushkin's «Стихотворения» and Lermontov's «Стихотворения» — and mark it «+» for presence in
earlier years' codifiers. The world's row is not new and not narrow in years. It is narrow in
specification.

## The list it sits at the end of

The corpus table for Russia holds forty-eight rows, one per numbered element, and forty-seven of
them are domestic. That is a fact about the shape of one document under one door type, and it is
not a score: a codifier of examinable elements and a ministry program of taught units are not the
same kind of object, and this book does not rank them against each other. What the forty-seven do
is give line 48 its context.

They are arranged like a genealogy. One row's author predates 1700 (the anonymous Lay, c. 1185);
thirteen rows carry authors born in the eighteenth century, twenty-nine in the nineteenth, four in
the twentieth. The nineteenth-century block is the table's centre of gravity — and elsewhere in
this book's corpus it is an export: Belarus, Kazakhstan, Ukraine, the United States, India and
Vietnam all carry rows coded `translated:russian` naming Pushkin, Gogol, Dostoevsky, Tolstoy,
Chekhov. This is the codifier other lists borrow from.

On historical subject the table is mostly silent. Twenty-five of the forty-eight rows carry
`various/unspecified` in the `subject_era` column, the foreign row among them: a row that names no
work fixes no represented subject, so an unnamed choice cannot be read as a subject the table
admits. Two more carry a `none/` value — Karamzin's «Бедная Лиза», coded for tragic romance across
estate lines, and Krylov's fables, for moral and social satire.

The twenty-one rows left with a dated or named subject are domestic without exception: the
Rus–Polovtsy war of the twelfth century, eighteenth-century autocracy, serfdom on two rows, the
Decembrist era, the 1820s gentry life of
«Евгений Онегин», social inequality, the Petersburg flood of 1824, the Pugachev rebellion, the
*oprichnina* under Ivan the Terrible, the Caucasus conquest and the Caucasus war, Nicholaevan
autocracy and corruption, Nicholaevan bureaucracy, Nicholaevan army brutality, nineteenth-century
autocracy and class exploitation, late-tsarist stagnation, the Great Patriotic War on two rows —
Sholokhov's «Судьба человека» and Tvardovsky's «Василий Тёркин» — the post-war Soviet village,
and post-Stalin Soviet peasantry. Those last four rows — Sholokhov, Tvardovsky, Shukshin,
Solzhenitsyn — are also the four whose authors were born in the twentieth century. Every row coded
with a definite war or conquest is domestic, and the fighting on it is fighting on this territory
or by this state.

Three rows open the door wider without leaving the family. Items 45 and 46 list by name
twenty-three prose entries and nineteen poets of the late nineteenth to twenty-first centuries —
Bulgakov, Nabokov, Platonov, Akhmatova, Brodsky, Okudzhava, Vysotsky — as selectable authors
rather than fixed works. Item 47 is «Литература народов Российской Федерации», the literature of
the peoples of the Russian Federation: R. Gamzatov, M. Karim, G. Tukay, K. Kuliev «и др.» The
codifier prints that heading and those names and stops; the languages those four wrote in — Avar,
Bashkir, Tatar, Balkar — and Tukay's death before either the Soviet Union or the Russian
Federation existed are bibliography, not document. The table codes the row `domestic`, following
the codifier, and the coding is right: its line between ours and foreign is drawn by state, not by
language. That is the shape line 48 sits at the end of.

## What is actually in the room

The codifier's companion document, the *specification* from the same 2025 package, turns line 48
from a category into an object. Its Appendix 2 — «Список произведений, по которым могут
формулироваться задания КИМ ОГЭ по литературе», the list of works exam tasks may be built on —
doubles as a packing list, because the exam is open-book by furniture: «Экзаменуемые по мере
необходимости работают с текстами за отдельными столами, на которых находятся нужные книги», the
examinees work as needed at separate tables on which the necessary books lie, stocked in several
copies per room.

The appendix's last entry, item 31, is «Произведения зарубежной литературы», and here the
parenthesis finally names titles:

> «Книги с произведениями Гомера (поэмы «Илиада», «Одиссея», возможна хрестоматия
> с фрагментами поэм), М. Сервантеса (роман «Хитроумный идальго Дон Кихот Ламанчский», возможна
> хрестоматия с главами из романа), У. Шекспира (сборник сонетов, пьесы «Ромео и Джульетта»,
> «Гамлет»), Ж.-Б. Мольера (пьеса «Мещанин во дворянстве»)» — "Books with the works of Homer
> (the poems *Iliad*, *Odyssey*; an anthology of fragments is possible), M. Cervantes (the novel
> *The Ingenious Hidalgo Don Quixote of La Mancha*; an anthology of chapters is possible),
> W. Shakespeare (a book of sonnets, the plays *Romeo and Juliet*, *Hamlet*), J.-B. Molière (the
> play *The Bourgeois Gentleman*)."

Six named works and a collection of sonnets, in several copies: that is what Appendix 2 instructs
a room to supply from the literature of the rest of the world. It is a stocking order, not a
boundary — what it guarantees is on the table, not what the exam may ask. (The same appendix has
no entry at all for item 47's literature of the peoples of the Russian Federation; of its four
poets only Gamzatov appears, folded into the general lyric list at entry 30.)

The named foreigners stop early. The latest of them, Molière, died in 1673 — thirty-eight years
before Lomonosov, the earliest named individual on the domestic list, was born. Antiquity, the
Renaissance, the Baroque: the printed examples end where the printed domestic canon begins. That
is a pattern in the dates and nothing more; a list records admission and never motive, and neither
document says why these four names and no fifth.

What the examples *can* be read for is the errand a text is admitted to perform, because the
codifier prints the vocabulary in which any admitted work may be discussed. Section 2's list of
terms and concepts — applied vocabulary, it says of itself, not a scientific classification — is
what a Part 2 essay is partly scored on, under the criterion «Опора на теоретико-литературные
понятия»: «комедия», «сатира», «ирония», «гротеск», «классицизм», «речевая характеристика героя»,
«пафос (героический, трагический, комический)». Set those against the only foreign author whose
Appendix 2 entry is one named play — Molière, «Мещанин во дворянстве». Jourdain hires masters to
teach him what a gentleman knows and is told, as the great discovery of his education, that he has
been speaking prose all his life without knowing it. The joke is exactly a *речевая характеристика
героя*: a man hears his own speech named for the first time and finds it is the thing that has
been placing him all along. What comes through that row, in the door's own vocabulary, is a comic
form for the distance between what a person is and what a person is taken for. Whether that is the
errand anyone intended, the documents do not say.

Nor is the parenthesis the edge of the world; the codifier says so on the page that follows
Table 3. The poem list is open — «Перечень поэтов и стихотворений носит открытый характер:
в заданиях КИМ могут быть предложены для анализа стихотворения, не названные в кодификаторе»,
poems it does not name may be set for analysis. Part 2 may set an essay on a writer whose works it
does not name, the examinee choosing the work; sometimes the wording names neither writer nor
work, and «выбор литературного материала также осуществляет экзаменуемый». In the essay an
examinee may draw on «произведения из кодификатора ОГЭ, а также из круга своего чтения (если
формулировка темы это разрешает)» — the codifier's works and their own reading, where the topic
permits, with rap lyrics, pop songs, comics, manga, fan fiction and graphic novels excluded by
name. Two footnotes narrow the unnamed-work permissions: one to domestic prose of the twentieth
and twenty-first centuries, the other to late-nineteenth- to twenty-first-century writers, without
naming a country.

Three things therefore stay distinct. Table 3 prints four foreign examples. Appendix 2 orders
certain foreign books onto a table in the room. And the rules of choice leave openings that
neither list closes. «И др.» on its own is a small phrase; the rules around it are not. The
examinable world is not sealed at four names — and neither document names a fifth.

Section 3 of the same codifier sets out the personal outcomes of the educational program and ties
them to the exam rather than setting them apart: «Содержание и результаты выполнения заданий ОГЭ
связаны в том числе с достижением следующих личностных результатов» — the content and results of
ОГЭ tasks are connected, among other things, with achieving them. Among the outcomes it quotes are
«формирование осознанного, уважительного и доброжелательного отношения к другому человеку, его
мнению, мировоззрению, культуре, языку, вере[…] к истории, культуре, религии, традициям, языкам,
ценностям народов России и народов мира» and «развитие эстетического сознания через освоение
художественного наследия народов России и мира» — a respectful attitude toward another person's
worldview, culture, language and faith and toward the values of the peoples of Russia and of the
world; aesthetic consciousness developed through the artistic heritage of the peoples of Russia
and of the world (pp. 17–18).

These are two sections of one document, and it connects them rather than dividing them; it does
not place the outcomes beyond the exam's reach. What can be observed is a contrast in
specification. The peoples of the world are named in the outcomes, and named again; in the table
of the checkable they hold one row, and on it a category, four examples, and no title.

## Twelve, unnamed

One more line deserves reading, because it is where most canons keep their deepest hold on a
child's body. Table 2, item 8, requires «Совершенствование умения выразительно (с учётом
индивидуальных особенностей обучающихся) читать, в том числе наизусть, не менее 12 произведений
и (или) фрагментов» — improvement of the ability to read expressively, including by heart, no
fewer than 12 works and/or fragments. It does not say which. Nor does it set a floor for
memorizing: reading by heart is named as one way the expressive reading is done, «в том числе»,
with no share of the twelve assigned to it. Footnote 2 then places the skill rather than
retracting it: «Данное умение на экзамене не проверяется непосредственно, но оно востребовано при
выполнении заданий, предполагающих цитирование по памяти фрагментов литературных произведений» —
not checked directly, but called upon in tasks that require quoting fragments from memory. Not
withdrawn; relocated — a condition of doing the work rather than a thing the work scores.

Every one of the corpus's forty-eight rows therefore carries `memorize: no`: the reconciliation
rule required an explicit mandate to learn a *named* text by heart, and this language names none.
What an examinee holds in memory is real; it is decided a level below this text, and leaves no
trace in the list of the checkable.

## The parenthesis

The specification does not describe the room; it orders one. Beyond the separate tables and the
several copies, it requires that the books be prepared so that an examinee cannot work with the
commentaries and introductory articles bound in with them. «Во время проведения экзамена должны
быть обеспечены равные условия доступа к художественным текстам для всех участников экзамена» —
equal conditions of access to the literary texts for every participant.

So: separate tables, the needed books on them, several copies, no introductions. Should a
fifteen-year-old in that room want a foreign text, what the appendix has ordered onto a table for
them is two Greek epics or an anthology of fragments; a Spaniard's novel or an anthology of its
chapters; an Englishman's sonnets and two of his plays; a Frenchman's comedy about a man trying to
be taken for something he is not. Where those books sit, and which of them anyone reaches for, the
specification does not say, and neither can this table.

A parenthesis is a mark that means *this is included, and it is not the sentence*. Line 48 is the
sentence's parenthesis, and inside it the codifier puts examples where another document would put
a canon. What the two together establish is narrower and stranger than a shelf: a genealogy in one
language reaching back to the twelfth century, on which the rest of the world holds a single row;
a table at the side of a room with certain named books on it; and rules under which an examinee may
bring more than either list prints — while neither prints the more.

## Limits

- **One year, one exit, one exam package, two documents.** This is the Grade 9 ОГЭ package for
  2025, and the essay leans on both of its parts: the codifier for what may be asked, the
  specification for what must be in the room. The Grade 11 ЕГЭ codifier is a different list and is
  not in this corpus, so nothing here describes what an eighteen-year-old can be examined on, and
  the 2023 realignment's effect on that list is outside what these rows can show.
- **An examinee, not a generation.** Both documents speak of «участники экзамена», exam
  participants. Nothing in them establishes that every Russian ninth-grader sits the literature
  ОГЭ, and no claim above should be read as saying so: the subject throughout is an examinee
  taking this exam.
- **A codifier is not a classroom.** By its own scope clause it holds only checkable elements.
  Works taught, loved, or set for home reading and not gradable by this exam are invisible to it.
  A foreign novel absent from line 48's examples may still be read in a Russian classroom; this
  table cannot see it either way.
- **`exam_status` is the compiler's coding.** 40 `mandatory` / 8 `choice-list` compresses at least
  three document mechanisms: a named work (taskable in any part of the paper), a named author with
  the work left to the examinee, and an open name-list from which unnamed poems may be set. The
  split is not a ratio of anything, and no cross-country count comparison is offered here: door
  types are not commensurable.
- **The window's single row is a category, not a work.** Nothing here establishes that any
  particular foreign book is read or examined. Two different things are established and should not
  be merged: the foreign names the codifier prints as examples, and the foreign books Appendix 2
  instructs a room to supply. A book supplied is not thereby a book set; a book set is not confined
  to the books supplied, since the codifier's rules let an examinee choose material neither list
  names.
- **Motive is not in the corpus.** The contrast between the outcomes in Section 3 and the content
  of Table 3 is a description of two parts of one text, and the text connects them rather than
  dividing them. No ministry statement of reasons for the size or wording of item 48 was located in
  `resources/`, and none is implied above. The reading of Molière is a reading of the play against
  the codifier's own vocabulary; it is not evidence of anyone's intention in printing his name.
- **Memorization codings are conservative by rule.** The reconciliation pass required an explicit
  mandate to learn a *named* text by heart; the codifier's aggregate «не менее 12 произведений
  и (или) фрагментов» does not name any, so all 48 rows read `no`. Nor does that clause say how
  many of the twelve are held by heart. This does not mean Russian pupils memorize nothing — it
  means this document does not say what.
- **One cross-country fact, fenced.** Gogol is a domestic row here (three rows: «Ревизор»,
  «Шинель», «Мёртвые души»), and the same author is a *foreign-literature* row in Ukraine's
  corpus (`corpus/ua-2024.tsv`, from the Nikolenko model program). That is a placement fact about
  two documents, receipted in both tables, and it is stated here for no purpose beyond noting that
  the two doors disagree about which side of them he stands on.
