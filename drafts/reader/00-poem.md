# The Poem in Your Body

There is a poem in you that starts itself. You have not thought about it in years, and
you could not stop it if you tried: someone says the first three words and the next line
arrives on its own, in the voice of whoever made you learn it, in the classroom where
you were fifteen. You did not choose that poem. Somebody chose it for you, and this book
began with the discovery that the choosing was signed.

For a year I read the documents that do the choosing — the exam codifiers, the ministry
programs, the state anthologies of twenty school systems — looking for the world in
them: which foreign books each country lets into its classrooms, and why. The honest
answer to the second question is that no document ever says why. But somewhere in the
middle of the reading, a smaller column in my tables started to glow. Most of what a
school assigns, a teenager reads once and sets down. A few texts are different: the
state orders them carried — учить наизусть, by heart, in the body. And when I filtered
twenty systems for that one instruction, almost everything fell away. Seventeen
countries, on paper, mandate no named text for memory at all. Three do. They are
Russia, Kazakhstan, and Ukraine — three school systems that share a border, a history,
and, until recently, a shelf — and they use the same instruction to say three opposite
things.

Kazakhstan's program orders twelve works held by heart at fifteen. I expected Abai —
the national poet whose face is on the money, whose schools carry his name. He is not
there. Not one Kazakh line is mandated for memory. All twelve texts are foreign: eleven
Russian — Pushkin, Gogol, Dostoevsky, Tolstoy, Chekhov, an overcoat, a set of poor
folk, a garnet bracelet — and, standing alone at the end of the list like a tourist who
boarded the wrong train, Shakespeare's sonnets. A Kazakh child's own literature is
read, discussed, written about. What the state sets into the body is the literature of
the empire it used to belong to. The program gives no reason. Programs never do.

Ukraine mandates five texts by heart at the same age, and not one of them is Ukrainian
either — but the resemblance ends there, because none of them is Russian. A sonnet by
Dante. A sonnet by Petrarch. A sonnet by Shakespeare. A lyric by Heine. And a poem
called "To Bukovina" by Mihai Eminescu — a Romanian, writing about a green hilly region
that lies today partly inside Ukraine. Ukrainian literature itself is memorized too,
but by recommendation, with the pupil choosing the poem; the law reserves its force for
the world. This is the country that runs an entire school subject called Foreign
Literature, and its memory mandate matches: what must live in a Ukrainian body, by
statute, is Europe.

Russia is the mirror of both. Its federal program orders a pupil finishing grade nine
to recite at least twelve poetic works newly learned that year, and attaches its
by-heart instruction to named units: the medieval Lay of Igor's Campaign, a courtier's
ode from 1747, Griboyedov's Woe from Wit, Gogol's Dead Souls. Every one on the domestic
shelf. The same program marches its children up a staircase of
required foreign classics — Homer at twelve, Cervantes at thirteen, Hamlet at fifteen —
so it is not that Russia hides the world. The world is for reading, in fragments, on
the way up. The memory is for what is Russia's own. Three neighbouring states, one
Soviet inheritance, one identical pedagogic instrument — and it points outward in
Ukraine, backward in Kazakhstan, and inward in Russia.

Then there is Belarus, and Belarus is the reason I trust this column more than any
other in my tables. Its program mandates six texts by heart, and it is the only system
in all twenty that splits the instruction between two literatures: Yanka Kupala and
Yakub Kolas — the two poets every Belarusian school is practically named after — on one
side, and Pushkin and Griboyedov on the other. Two canons set into one adolescent body,
in two languages, in the same school year. Belarus runs Russian literature as a
separate subject with its own hours; a Belarusian classroom is the one place in this
corpus where a child is required to memorize both itself and its neighbour. Whether
that is a window or a mirror — or one of each, facing each other — is a question this
book will spend a whole chapter failing to settle politely.

And the other seventeen? They are not silent because their children memorize nothing.
England makes its fifteen-year-olds sit closed-book poetry exams, where quotation can
only come from memory, while its documents never once print the instruction memorize —
whatever memorizing happens hides in the exam's design, deniable. China writes the most open clause in
the corpus: recitation is required, foreign poetry is explicitly welcome in it, and the
choice of texts is handed down to textbook editors and teachers — the state mandates
the practice and declines to sign the poem. America mandates neither the practice nor
the poem. The three countries that sign their names are the exception, and that is
exactly why they matter: a memorization mandate is the one place in a school system
where the state stops recommending and writes directly onto a person.

So this book starts where you are already an expert. Before it walks you through the
twenty windows — Ukraine's room-sized one, Russia's staircase, China's mirror,
Bolivia's cast list, the empty ones that whole rich countries decline to fill — it
asks you to run the experiment that no ministry can audit. Find the poem that starts
itself in you. Say the first line. Notice the language it arrives in — because for
some of you it will not be the language of your childhood street, and by the end of
this book you will know precisely which office made that decision, in which document,
and what it says about what your country wanted you to be.

*What this chapter can't know: the mandates counted here are the ones written into
defining documents as of one school year — classrooms memorize far more than laws do,
and no table can see a teacher's Tuesday. The three-plus-one pattern is real, receipted,
and reproducible from this book's corpus; the reading of it is mine.*
