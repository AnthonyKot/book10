# Ukraine: The Door Is a Subject

Most countries admit foreign literature the way a house admits weather — through gaps: a
unit inside the national literature course, a line in an exam specification permitting "one
work in translation." Ukraine built a door.

On a Ukrainian ninth-grader's timetable there is a line that reads **«Зарубіжна
література»** — Foreign Literature — and it is not a module inside anything. It is a
subject, sitting beside Ukrainian Language and Ukrainian Literature, with its own hours,
its own model programs, its own memorization list. The Nikolenko program states the
arrangement plainly: it «вивчається окремо як самостійний і самодостатній предмет, що має
унікальну неперервну традицію викладання (з 1991 р.) в умовах Незалежності» — "is studied
separately as an independent and self-sufficient subject, one with a uniquely unbroken
teaching tradition (since 1991) under conditions of Independence" (p. 5; my translation).
The window is not a pane in a wall. It is a room.

## The door, and its hinges

"Whole separate subject" is the kind of fact that invites a country to be graded, and this
corpus cannot grade — so the door needs describing precisely. The State Standard for basic
secondary education (resolution No. 898, 30 September 2020) creates the
language-and-literature field; it does not name a single book. Books are named one level
down, in **model programs** («модельні навчальні програми») recommended by the Ministry of
Education and Science — and a school chooses which to adopt. This table draws its foreign
rows from two of them: Nikolenko et al. supply thirty-four, Voloshchuk et al. five.
Inside a chosen program, a third hinge: headings such as «ЛЮДИНА У ВИМІРІ НОВОЇ ДРАМИ
(1 твір за вибором учителя)» — "one work at the teacher's choice."

So the force of a row is this: the state mandates that world literature happen and
delegates *which* world twice, to a program and then to a teacher. Fifty-seven rows,
eighteen domestic (from the Ukrainian Literature programs, a different subject with a
different shelf) and thirty-nine translated. Of the thirty-nine, thirteen are coded
`mandatory` and twenty-six `choice-list` — a word covering three unlike mechanisms, which
makes it a caution rather than a count to lean on.

## What comes through

Ten source languages arrive. Nineteen of the thirty-nine translated rows come from English,
six from French, four from German, two each from Russian, Polish and Italian, and one
apiece from Yiddish, Romanian, Norwegian and Japanese. Everything is read in Ukrainian, and
the program makes the translation part of the lesson: the works come «в найкращих
українських перекладах із урахуванням здобутків української перекладацької школи» — "in the
best Ukrainian translations, taking account of the achievements of the Ukrainian school of
translation" (Nikolenko, p. 5). A foreign book enters this room already holding a Ukrainian
hand.

English is also where the shelf gets youngest — Rowling, Haddon, Whitehead, Foer, Zusak —
and most of those rows sit in Nikolenko's additional-reading appendix, not a taught unit.
Nineteen-of-thirty-nine is a fact about what is available in Ukrainian, not about what
fills the hour. By errand, the shelf sorts into three uneven piles.

**Europe as a destination.** The Grade 9 unit «ДОБА ПРОСВІТНИЦТВА: РОЗУМ, ПРИРОДА, ОСВІТА»
("The Age of Enlightenment: Reason, Nature, Education") carries Swift's first voyage,
Goethe's lyrics, and Schiller's «До радості». The teaching line under Schiller does not
stop at 1785: «Просвітницька ідея об'єднання людства… Пафос твору, який став гімном
Євросоюзу» — "The Enlightenment idea of the unification of humanity… The pathos of a work
that became the anthem of the European Union" (Nikolenko, p. 72). The ode arrives twice in
one sentence: as a document of 1785, as the music of a union.

**Other people's wounds.** The translated rows carry injuries the domestic shelf does not
hold: `1930s-Jim-Crow` (Lee), `US-slavery` (Whitehead), `Congo-Free-State-colonialism`
(Conrad), `WWI` (Morpurgo), `WWII-occupation` (Zusak), `9/11` (Foer). Next door, the
Ukrainian rows hold `totalitarianism` (Bahrianyi, Stus),
`1944-Crimean-Tatar-deportation` (Umerov), and `Russo-Ukrainian-war-since-2014` — a unit of
contemporary war poetry, «Поезія Вільних». What this table can say is that the current war
sits on the domestic shelf while the imported shelf carries other people's catastrophes;
what it cannot say is that either placement was designed. Rows record admission, never
intention.

**The Ukrainian pages.** This is the pile that makes the window structurally unusual, and
the programs name the principle themselves. Every unit in Nikolenko carries a standing
rubric, «Україна і світ» (УС) — "Ukraine and the World." Voloshchuk runs the equivalent as
«український мотив» (УМ), the rubric for «зв'язки зарубіжних письменників з Україною,
майстерність українських перекладачів, твори іншомовних авторів, біографічно пов'язаних з
українськими територіями» — "the ties of foreign writers to Ukraine, the mastery of
Ukrainian translators, works by foreign-language authors biographically connected with
Ukrainian territories" (p. 7). Its rationale, from the same note:

> «Слід наголосити, що такі автори поєднують Україну зі світом, несуть свої уявлення про
> неї в інші культури.» — "It should be emphasized that such authors join Ukraine to the
> world, carrying their conceptions of it into other cultures." (p. 4, on including
> foreign-language authors born or resident on Ukrainian territories; my translation)

Read the translated rows through that rubric and a second shelf appears inside the first.
Byron arrives with «Мазепа», tagged in Voloshchuk beside «Битва під Полтавою та постать
Мазепи в українській культурі». Conrad's row carries the table's own annotation — born in
Berdychiv. Sholem Aleichem's «Тев'є-молочар», the corpus's single Yiddish row, is by an
author born in Pereiaslav, and its unit's Ukraine-and-the-World line reads «Шолом-Алейхем і
Україна» (Nikolenko, p. 83).

And then the two Russian-language rows, which have to be written carefully or not at all.
Gogol's row offers «Ревізор» АБО «Шинель» — one of the two — and the program frames the
author by period, «Петербурзький етап життя і творчості письменника» ("the Petersburg stage
of the writer's life and work"), while the unit's Ukraine-and-the-World line reads
«М. Гоголь і українська культура» (Nikolenko, pp. 77, 79). Bulgakov's row is «Собаче
серце», and his entry opens «Українські сторінки життя і творчості М. О. Булгакова» —
"Ukrainian pages of the life and work of M. O. Bulgakov" (p. 81). The corpus notes describe
both as retained Russian-language works, keyed to Ukrainian origin in Gogol's case and to
Kyivan birth in Bulgakov's; those notes are the compiler's gloss, not a ministry statement
of reasons, and they stay a gloss here. What the documents do is placement: two
Russian-language authors taught inside a subject about the world, under a rubric about
Ukraine.

One thing sits outside this table, fenced: the corpus has no row for it, but both
Ukrainian-literature model programs in `resources/` set Gogol's «Сорочинський ярмарок» in
Grade 9. If that survives receipting, the same author walks through both doors of the same
school — one work as a national writer, another as a foreign one.

## The errand, close-read

The sharpest artifact in this table is not a novel — it is the memorization list.

Five rows carry `memorize: yes` — Heine, Dante, Petrarch, Shakespeare, Eminescu — and every
one is translated. The domestic rows carry `no`: the reconciliation pass stripped seven
earlier `yes`/`excerpt` codings on finding that the defining Ukrainian-literature program
makes poetry memorization recommendatory rather than mandated, and lets the learner choose
the poem. Whatever a Ukrainian teenager is *required* to hold in their body, in this
corpus, came from somewhere else.

Four of the five are what a world canon would predict: a Dante sonnet, a Petrarch sonnet
(61 or 132, learner's choice), a Shakespeare sonnet (116 or 130), one Heine lyric of three.
The fifth is Mihai Eminescu's «Буковині» — "To Bukovina." A Romanian Romantic's poem
addressed to a place that is, in part, a Ukrainian province. Beside it, the Ukrainian-motif
rubric sends the class to «М. Емінеску в Чернівцях; його подорожі Україною. Пам'ятник
Емінеску в Чернівцях» — Eminescu in Chernivtsi, his travels through Ukraine, the monument
to him there (Voloshchuk, pp. 89, 91).

So a poem a ninth-grader learns by heart, in Ukrainian translation, is a foreigner's
love-poem to their own hills. The import is not the landscape. The import is being seen
from outside — the thing a national literature cannot do for itself.

The other program names what the shelf seems built around. In Nikolenko's Grade 9, the
culture rubric over the History-and-Modernity unit reads «Культура як духовний опір
насильству» — "culture as spiritual resistance to violence" (p. 83) — printed above the
slot where a teacher picks two of Sholem Aleichem, Bulgakov, Bradbury, Zusak and Foer.

## Fifteen

You were fifteen in some room with a list — a photocopy with a column of due dates, or the
back page of a textbook you never finished. Someone chose it, and the choosing was
invisible to you. If your classroom made you learn a poem by heart, run the Ukrainian test
on it: was the poem yours?

In this table, the five works a ninth-grader must hold by memory are an
Italian's sonnet to Beatrice, another Italian's sonnet to Laura, an Englishman's sonnet, a
German's lyric — and a Romanian's poem about a Ukrainian province, learned in Ukrainian
translation, inside a subject taught without a break since 1991. Voloshchuk's Grade 9
header gives the clock: «Рекомендована кількість годин: 1,5 год на тиждень» — an hour and a
half a week, recommended (p. 67). Small, weekly, and a room of its own.

## Limits

- **One slice, and a merged one.** The foreign rows come from two *alternative* model
  programs, not one national list; a school adopts one. No ninth-grader necessarily faces
  all 39 rows; this pictures an available window, not any classroom's.
- **`choice-list` is three different things** (see Receipts): an either/or inside a
  mandatory slot, a teacher's pick-two-of-five, and Appendix 2's additional-reading list.
  Unequal force; the 13/26 split is not a ratio of anything.
- **`mandatory` overstates some rows.** The table codes Ibsen, Shaw and Maeterlinck
  `mandatory`, but the unit heading above them reads «1 твір за вибором учителя». Treat
  `exam_status` as the compiler's coding, never as examinability: Grade 9 here has no
  national exam list of the kind that defines the RU or UK tables. No cross-country count
  comparison is offered; the door types are not commensurable.
- **Motive is not in the corpus.** The `note` fields for Gogol and Bulgakov state reasons
  for retention; those are compiler's annotations, and no ministry statement of rationale
  was located in `resources/`. The programs show placement and framing only.
- **The removals question stays open.** Both foreign-literature documents postdate 2021 and
  the corpus holds no earlier Ukrainian list. What stood in these units before them — and
  whether anything was removed after February 2022 — cannot be answered here, and nothing
  above should be read as answering it.
- **Memorization codings are conservative.** The reconciliation rule required an explicit
  mandate to learn a named text by heart; recommendatory memorization, closed-book
  assessment and recitation culture were coded `no`. Nikolenko contains no memorization list
  at all, so the five `yes` rows are Voloshchuk's — the obligation to learn any poem by
  heart depends on which program a school adopted.
