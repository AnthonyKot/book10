# Mongolia: Sixteen Pages at the Back

*(Door-note for the collective interlude.)*

Open the captured Grade IX literature textbook — ministry-approved, printed 2023 — and
Byambyn Rinchen turns up in it twice. Once as an author: his novella about a letter that
gives away a secret, set in the Manchu conquest, starts on page 76. And once as a
witness. On page 130, where the last literary selection begins, a headnote tells pupils
that the work they are about to read belongs to the treasury of world classic
literature, that it has been translated into many languages and played on famous stages
including London's, and that Mongolians have known it since the fourteenth century by
way of Tibetan — which the book credits to Rinchen, scholar and writer.

The work is Kalidasa's *The Recognition of Shakuntala*, printed as an excerpt, sixteen
pages long, last of the book's fourteen selections; after it come only a student
reference section and a bibliography. It is the only one of the fourteen the table
records as arriving in translation. The other thirteen are domestic: the epics Gesar and
Jangar, the tale of Chinggis's two bay geldings, a lament for a dead khan, a queen's
oath, an inscription cut into rock in 1621, two modern poems, five works of modern
prose. All fourteen are coded anthology-only, and not one carries a memorization
requirement.

The state document behind them names no book at all. It is a set of assessment criteria,
issued by the Education Evaluation Center under the Ministry of Education and Science
and approved by ministerial order in 2021, saying grade by grade what a pupil must be
able to do with a text. For Grade IX: read and understand a work, analyse it,
write a short story, argue a position under the rules of debate, and present a work read
independently — that last one leaves the choice of book open and grades the telling. The
criteria's reading clauses do say where reading comes from, in genres and origins rather
than titles: the world's finest tales and short stories at Grade VI, the finest works of
West and East at VII and VIII, Mongolia's own ancient forms at IX, the literatures of
the different cultures of West and East at XI, Mongolian literature again at X and XII.
Titles arrive one level down, in the approved textbook — and this textbook's drama slot
is Indian.

What the excerpt is for, the book says in its own teaching voice. The drama chapter
opens by telling pupils what the unit will give them: to read Mongolian and foreign
plays for their meaning and their characters' conflicts, to study content, style and
genre, to explain and judge why a character develops — and to understand and explain the
social, religious and cultural diversity inside a work. Then, on the page where Kalidasa
starts, two questions. Why do people still want to read works composed thousands of
years ago? And what is the value of the famous Indian play? Sixteen pages later a king
has forgotten, under a curse, the girl he married in a forest; a poor fisherman has cut
a signet ring out of a fish's belly; and the king remembers.

If your own classroom's foreign unit sat at the back of the book, after the national
chapters were done with you, you know that shape without being told. Here it is one
play, in one approved book, in the terminal year of basic education: the world arrives
as a question about value and difference, and it arrives last.

## Limits

This note can see one grade of one country, in one printed book, for one year, plus the
assessment criteria that grade it. It is not a picture of a Mongolian school career: the
criteria name the world at four other grades (VI, VII, VIII and XI), and the single
Sanskrit row means "one translated work in this book," never "one foreign work in this
education."

The sources do not establish the classroom scene at all, and nothing above should be read
as supplying one. They do not show whether this is the only approved Grade IX literature
textbook, how widely it is used, whether pupils hold individual copies, whether teachers
work through the selections in printed order, or whether the independently read work of
the fifth assessment unit is distinct from the fourteen selections. No claim of ministry
motive is made or implied: placement is in the documents, motive is not.

The anthology's receipt is the weak one — a flipbook capture of the pages rather than a
ministry PDF, with a font-substituted text layer. The criteria are a properly saved
ministry publication, but its download URL was not recorded at capture, as the Receipts
state. The rows have been through the reconciliation and confirmation passes and are
cited as repaired data; the capture also carries headnotes and classroom questions, and
where the note draws on those it says so.

Three smaller cautions. Whether the possessive "Mongolia's" at the head of the Grade IX
reading clause governs the whole genre list or only its first item is a grammatical
question left open here; the note reports what the approved textbook prints, not what the
syntax requires. The `subject_era` values carrying a question mark are the compiler's
uncertain readings; the confirm pass agreed with thirteen of fourteen and flagged Jangar
— whose row now records the mid-fifteenth-century *origin* the textbook gives while
coding the era the epic depicts as legendary, an origin not being a subject — which recon
then repaired. And the memorization codings are conservative by rule: they mean that no
Grade IX criterion mandates learning a named text by heart, not that Mongolian classrooms
do not recite.

What the corpus cannot supply at all is the syllabus behind the textbook. The criteria
are an assessment instrument aligned, by their own foreword, to the curriculum's intended
outcomes; that teaching program is not in `resources/`, so this note describes what the
state measures and what one approved book prints, and stops there. No comparison of
Mongolia's fourteen rows against any other country's list is offered or implied.
