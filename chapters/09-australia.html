The Anglosphere Paradox · Chapter 09

Australia: The Word Between Two Books

On a beach outside Algiers, a Frenchman shoots a man the novel never names. The book calls him the Arab. He has a sister, a knife, and no line of dialogue; the rest of the novel belongs to the shooter, tried less for the killing than for failing to weep at his mother's funeral.

Year 12 students in New South Wales meet that beach — but only the ones whose class picked the third pair on a state list, where two titles are stacked one above the other with a single word printed between them: Camus's The Stranger, AND Kamel Daoud's The Meursault Investigation. The second book gives the dead man a name — Musa — and a brother who will not stop talking about him. Camus died in 1960; the man who answered him was born ten years later. In a table of 140 prescribed texts, that is the one pairing I read as a book and its rebuttal — and a class that picks it studies the two together, in one module, because a state exam authority set the word AND between them.

The door

The document is the HSC English prescriptions for 2019 to 2026, issued by the NSW Education Standards Authority. The first thing to say about it is that it is a state document: in Australia the enforceable named-text list belongs to the state, not the federation. Victoria's authority publishes its own annual text list for its own exam; what follows is New South Wales standing in for a federation — the same pattern as federal Germany, where the absence of one national list is not a gap in the evidence but the evidence.

Inside the state, the choosing is pushed down again. The foreword asks that teachers selecting specific texts consider the ethos of the school and its local community. A row in this table is not something a pupil will meet; it is something a pupil's teacher may offer.

And there is an age line above this door worth drawing before anything else. At fifteen — the age this book measures every other country at — Australia names no book. The national curriculum for those years asks for categories: wide-ranging Australian and world authors, First Nations texts, different cultures and times. The state syllabus adds quantities — so many novels, so many films — and exactly one proper noun: at least one Shakespeare play, title unnamed. The entire named canon of an Australian fifteen-year-old is one surname. The 140-title menu opens two years later, in elective senior courses, for the minority who have chosen literature as a path. What follows is not a generation's canon but the specialists' shelf.

The rest of the mechanics can be short, because only one of them matters here. What the state requires is a pattern, not a set of titles — a few texts, one from each of a few set categories, filled from menus — so 140 is the length of the menu, never of anyone's reading year. The module that holds the Algiers pair offers seven pairs; a class studies one. And a pair does not come apart: the word AND printed between Camus and Daoud means a class choosing either novel studies both; the choice is of the pair, never of one book.

What is on the shelf, and in what language

The subject is called English, and that name does most of the border work before any title is chosen. Adiga, Ishiguro, Lahiri, Tóibín, Zadie Smith, Langston Hughes: not one needed a translator to reach a Sydney desk. Of the 140 rows, 55 are Australian, 85 arrive from elsewhere, and 77 of those 85 were written in English to begin with — which leaves eight rows, in the whole table, from another language. Kafka and Fritz Lang from German, Guevara and Guillermo del Toro from Spanish, Calvino from Italian, Ibsen from Norwegian — and, from French, the Algiers pair: the answered book and its answer.

Against that, the door carries two written selection requirements, repeated across its course lists. Across the senior years, the selection of texts must give students experience of texts widely regarded as quality literature, including a range of literary texts written about intercultural experiences and the peoples and cultures of Asia; and of a range of Australian texts, including texts by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander authors and texts giving insight into diverse Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander experience. Neither clause carries a number.

Both leave marks on the table, and the marks are different shapes. Thirteen rows are coded to a subject naming an Asian country, city or people: Adiga's Bangalore, Ishiguro's postwar Japan, Mistry's Bombay, Thien's Cultural Revolution, Yousafzai's Pakistan, and — written from the Australian side of the border — the arrival stories of Nam Le and Alice Pung. Twelve rows are coded to Indigenous Australia, and every one is Australian: Noonuccal, Tara June Winch, Melissa Lucashenko, Linda Burney, Noel Pearson among them. Asia sits on this shelf as subject matter, written from both sides of the border; Indigenous Australia sits on it as home.

One row bends the pattern hard enough to be worth stopping on. Oodgeroo Noonuccal appears twice — the only author with two rows in that Indigenous count — her second entry a set of seven poems: "The Past," then "China…Woman," "Reed Flute Cave," "Entombed Warriors," "Visit to Sun Yat-Sen Memorial Hall," "Sunrise on Huampu River," "A Lake Within a Lake." Six of the seven come out of her 1984 visit to China. What those titles afford a pupil is an Aboriginal poet set for study as a traveller — looking outward from the country, not being looked at. The window opens the other way.

The errand: what the pair affords

The comparison module has its own teaching language, and it is unusually candid about what the comparing is meant to produce. Students explore how comparing texts can reveal resonances and dissonances between them and within them, and consider the ways a reimagining of one text might "mirror, align or collide" with the details of another.

Mirror, align or collide. Six of the seven pairs I read as chiefly mirroring — Al Pacino documenting a staging of Richard III, Jane Campion filming Keats's last love, Stephen Daldry filming Woolf writing Mrs Dalloway, Margaret Atwood rewriting The Tempest inside a prison theatre, the canon in conversation with its own admirers — and the seventh as the collision.

Daoud's narrator is Musa's brother, older now, drinking in Oran, addressing a stranger about a famous book in which his brother is a corpse without a name. The novel he is angry at is the other novel on the same page of the same list, in the same module, examined together.

What the pairing affords is not French existentialism, which Camus alone would supply. It is the experience of reading a masterpiece and then reading the person it stepped over — a canon being audited by the place it was set in. And the pupil who does that sits in a state whose own prescribed shelf includes The Secret River, a stage adaptation about dispossession on the Hawkesbury. Both books can go on one desk. That is what the list allows; it is not what the list is for.

The book nobody prescribed

There is a second English course in this state, for students learning English as an additional language or dialect, with its own shelf of 22 rows. Its module on language, identity and culture centres explicit English study on the Australian vernacular, its idioms and its colloquialisms. On that same page sit two rows worth reading side by side: an anthology called Contemporary Asian Australian Poets, and Langston Hughes — seven poems, including "Theme for English B," in which a young Black student, the only one of his kind in the class, is told to go home and write a page that will be true. A module built on the Australian vernacular offers a pupil a poem about being asked, in an English class, to produce something honest on demand.

Which is close to what the state asks of all of them. Every pupil in the state's Standard, Advanced, English Studies and EAL/D courses must also study one related text. The document names no list for it, no category, no title.

So the true end of this 140-row table is not a row. In a document that specifies publishers, editions and ISBNs, and that pauses to record how many pages one Wordsworth edition runs to, the one textual slot required in each of these four courses is the one the document declines to name.

A man on a beach outside Algiers got a name because a novelist in Oran wrote it down. A class in Sydney may read both books at one desk, because a state authority printed the word AND between them.
