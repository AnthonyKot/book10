# The United States: The Book You Bring Yourself

Picture the sitting the College Board's own sample materials describe — a sample, not an
observed room. Three essays, in a fixed order: a poem, a passage of prose, and then a
third question that stops naming books.

The first two handed the student their texts; the third hands over a concept — a
character given a gift that is also a burden — and then a sentence no ministry in this
book would write. *Either from your own reading or from the list below, choose a work of
fiction in which a character has been given a gift that is both an advantage and a
problem.* Below it, a column of titles, offered the way a waiter offers bread; the
student may take one or ignore them all. Then, with a recommended forty minutes to spend
on it, they supply the title of the book they intend to be examined on.

That sentence is the American window, and it is cut one reader at a time.

Nobody fills that title in from above — but not because nobody chooses. There is no
federal ministry with power to name a book, no national exam codifier, no enforceable
list of the kind that anchors every other chapter here; what stands in their place is
selection pushed down and dispersed. The sentence above comes from a course and exam
description published by the College Board, a private organization, which tells schools
that the works themselves are selected locally; this chapter's table comes from an
appendix to academic standards that states adopted one at a time, whose own work group
made the final choice of its exemplars. Between them they hold the two things a codifier
holds — force, and titles — and neither holds both. The exam has force over a score and
names no required book. The appendix names sixty-three titles for the 9–10 band and
disclaims, in its own front matter, being a list at all.

## The door that says it is not a door

Common Core Appendix B is called *Text Exemplars and Sample Performance Tasks*, and its
opening page explains what an exemplar is for. The samples "primarily serve to exemplify the
level of complexity and quality that the Standards require all students in a given grade
band to engage with," and the writers then close the door they appeared to be opening:
"They expressly do not represent a partial or complete reading list."

Take that sentence at face value, because the essay depends on it. Every row in this
table — all sixty-three, the whole Grades 9–10 band as printed, stories through technical
subjects — is coded `choice-list`, and none carries a memorization requirement. Not one of
these titles is a book an American fifteen-year-old must read. They are specimens of a
difficulty level, chosen to show teachers what that level feels like in the hand. The
criterion of admission the document states is complexity, quality and range. The criterion
it does not state is nationality.

The other document draws its one boundary the same way. The AP course description tells
teachers that by the end of the course "students should have studied a variety of texts by
diverse authors from a variety of time periods ranging from the English Renaissance to the
present." A real boundary, and a boundary of period. Both American doors specify a shape and
leave the passport unchecked.

So the interesting question is not what this country admits. It is what the shelf looks
like when the choosing is dispersed to a work group, districts, schools and teachers.

## What walks in

Forty-one of the sixty-three rows are domestic. Twenty-two are not, and the first thing to
say about those twenty-two is that eleven of them never met a translator. Shakespeare twice,
Donne, Shelley, Housman; Achebe's *Things Fall Apart*, Fugard's *"Master Harold"…and the
boys*, Zusak's *The Book Thief*; three works of exposition on art, music and mathematics.
They cross a border that is not a language border. Seven of the eleven are noted in the
table as British, and an eighth, Keith Devlin, as British-American — an inheritance shelf
more than an import shelf, the family books that came over with the language.

Eleven rows arrive in translation, and they are old, European, and few: three from Greek
(Homer, Sophocles, Euclid), three from French (Voltaire, Ionesco, Wiesel), two from Russian
(Gogol's "The Nose," 1836; Turgenev's *Fathers and Sons*, 1862), one each from Latin, German
and Norwegian. The appendix names the glass as well as the view — Fagles for Homer, Ronald
Wilks for Gogol, Constance Garnett for Turgenev, Derek Prouse for Ionesco — which is more
than a citation habit, since the complexity these rows exemplify is the complexity of an
English page a named translator made.

What the translated eleven do not include is worth stating carefully, and only against this
table: no row from Asia, from Latin America, from the Arabic-speaking world; Africa arrives
twice, in English. The Russian rows are the tsarist nineteenth century — a barber finding a
nose in his breakfast roll, a young nihilist arguing with his father — and the Soviet century
is not on this shelf. The corpus can report the shape of a 2010 work group's result without
assigning it a reason.

Group the twenty-two by the table's subject-era column and the shape is real but not
clean. Five foreign rows carry a state consuming people, none of it American: Nazi
Germany and the Holocaust (Zusak; Wiesel's Nobel lecture), apartheid South Africa
(Fugard), the neighbours turning into rhinoceroses under 1930s fascism (Ionesco), the
district commissioner arriving at the end of Achebe's late-nineteenth-century Igbo
village. Other foreign rows carry other injuries — Voltaire's Lisbon earthquake, Ibsen's
marriage, Turgenev's generational quarrel in reform-era Russia, *Macbeth*'s regicide — so
the column supports a lean, not a law. The domestic rows hold American injuries, and a
great many of them: Jim Crow, the Depression, Gettysburg, Wounded Knee, the Red Scare.
That placement is what the table shows; the 2010 work group recorded no reasons.

## What a foreign book is for here

Every other document in this book eventually says something to a teacher about a foreign
work: study this alongside that, note the ties to our own culture, learn these lines by
heart. The teaching language Appendix B attaches to a named work is its sample
performance tasks — which its front matter says illustrate how the Standards apply to the
exemplars already chosen — and each one closes with a code in square brackets.

> Students analyze how the character of Odysseus from Homer's *Odyssey*—a "man of twists
> and turns"—reflects conflicting motivations through his interactions with other
> characters in the epic poem.

The task runs one sentence further, on how his conflicting loyalties develop the poem's
themes, and closes with its code: [RL.9–10.3]. What the task asks of the *Odyssey* is
that RL.9–10.3 be practised on it. Two tasks later, *Macbeth* appears as raw material:
"Students analyze how the Japanese filmmaker Akira Kurosawa in his film *Throne of Blood*
draws on and transforms Shakespeare's play *Macbeth* in order to develop a similar plot
set in feudal Japan. [RL.9–10.9]" In these tasks — and they are a use of the books, not
the reason the books were chosen — an admitted foreigner's errand is to be an instance of
an operation. Not a feeling a country cannot source domestically; a skill it can be shown
performing.

And yet the appendix does something no standard requires. It prints excerpts, and somebody
chose them; the front matter concedes the awkwardness: "When excerpts appear, they serve only
as stand-ins for the full text."

The stand-in chosen for Fugard is not the scene the play is famous for. *"Master Harold"…and
the boys* ends with a white schoolboy spitting in the face of the Black man who raised him.
What the appendix prints instead is Sam, earlier, trying to explain ballroom dancing:

> None of us knows the steps and there's no music playing. And it doesn't stop with us. The
> whole world is doing it all the time. Open a newspaper and what do you read? America has
> bumped into Russia, England is bumping into India, rich man bumps into poor man. Those are
> big collisions, Hally. They make for a lot of bruises.

A South African play about a South African injury, excerpted at the exact half-page where it
turns and names the reader's own country first in a list of collisions. The choice is an
editorial act and a visible one. Its reason is not in the document, and I will not supply one.

There is one more constraint on this shelf, and it is not a state's. The same front
matter explains that "in instances where we could not employ Fair Use and have been
unable to secure permission, we have listed a title without providing an excerpt." What
that establishes is exactly this much: copyright and permission limited which excerpts
the compilers could reproduce, so some titles stand on the page without their text. Which
titles, and why each permission failed, the document does not say.

## The list nobody collects

If your classroom's reading list arrived as a photocopy — stapled, signed by a teacher who
could have chosen otherwise — you already know the argument over it happened somewhere
above you and out of sight: a board meeting, a department vote, a publisher's catalogue.
That is the American arrangement: selection everywhere, and no single national address
for it.

Which leaves the exam room. Every other window in this book can be photographed: a
codifier you can print, a program with page numbers, a spine a pupil carries in a bag.
The American window has to be reconstructed one answer at a time — a title chosen by a
student with a recommended forty minutes to spend on it, written into one essay, graded —
and this corpus holds no instrument that ever collects those titles into a list.

## Limits

- **The label says 2024; the document is 2010.** Every row carries `doc_year` 2010 and
  `retrieved` 2026-08-10: the file's 2024 is a construction label, not a document year,
  and the Common Core appendix was sixteen years old when retrieved. The one document
  here actually marked for 2024 — the AP CED, "Effective Fall 2024" — put no rows in the
  table.
- **An exemplar is not a mandate, and these rows must never be counted against a codifier's.**
  A row means a title was printed to illustrate a difficulty level in a document that says it
  is not a reading list. The door types are not commensurable, and no total above compares to
  a list-issuing country's.
- **The domestic/foreign partition is the compiler's, not the document's.** Appendix B
  tags no author with a nationality, and the blind-confirm pass validated titles and
  eras, not the `origin` column. The coding rule used: an author is `domestic` when their
  primary literary career is American, including naturalized authors (so Auden is
  `domestic`), and non-domestic otherwise (so British-American Keith Devlin is not). The
  41/22 split moves with that rule, and edge cases like these two are the proof; treat
  the partition as a coding, never as a passport fact.
- **`translated:english` is a misnomer in eleven rows.** Those works were written in
  English and never translated; the column is recording "not American" under the rule
  above — what a schema built for codifier countries does when it meets an anglophone
  shelf.
- **One band, one document, and the classroom is elsewhere.** What American pupils read is
  set by districts, schools and teachers; the scout notes name big-district approved lists as
  a third proxy, none of it receipted here. Nothing above describes a real syllabus.
- **`memorize` is a document silence.** All 63 rows are `no` because the appendix carries no
  memorization mandate, and the rule required an explicit instruction to learn a named text by
  heart — not evidence about American practice.
- **Adoption is not in the table.** Common Core was adopted state by state and later revised
  or renamed in several states; with no state-level data in the corpus, no claim is made about
  where, or whether, this appendix was in force.
- **Motive is absent by design.** The appendix states selection criteria and explains no
  individual choice — which work was proposed, which excerpt cut, whose permission refused.
  Printing and placement are visible; reasons are not.
- **Length accounting.** The essay runs about 1,680 words, inside the cap; Receipts and
  Limits carry the file to roughly 2,550, the apparatus being heavier than usual because
  this country's instrument has to be disclaimed three times — as an exemplar, as a 2010
  document under a 2024 label, and as a compiler-partitioned shelf.
