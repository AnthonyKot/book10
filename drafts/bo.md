# Bolivia: The Ministry Prints the Book

Most doors in this corpus are lists. A codifier names what an examiner may ask; a
specification names set texts; a ministry program names units and works, and the books
themselves are somebody else's business — a publisher's, a school's, a teacher's. Bolivia's
door is not a list. It is a book. One physical volume, issued by the Ministry of Education,
carrying several school subjects between one set of covers; and inside it, across pages 25
to 36, the twenty-nine works this corpus codes from it.

The volume is the *Texto de aprendizaje, 4to. Año de Educación Secundaria Comunitaria
Productiva*, marked «Texto oficial 2024» above the Minister of Education's name (p. 2). At
the foot of the same page: «LA VENTA DE ESTE DOCUMENTO ESTÁ PROHIBIDA» — the sale of this
document is prohibited. It is not sold. It is issued.

## The door, and how hard it closes

A window this easy to see is easy to over-read, so the force of a row needs stating first.
Every one of the twenty-nine rows in this table is coded `anthology-only`. Not one is coded
to an exam. Not one carries `memorize: yes`. There is no Bolivian exam list in this corpus
against which a fifteen-year-old could be tested on Flaubert.

The book itself says as much. Its Presentación describes the contents as «un elemento
dinamizador del aprendizaje, que siempre puede ampliarse, profundizarse y contextualizarse
desde la experiencia y la realidad de cada contexto cultural, social y educativo» — an
element that energizes learning, one that can always be widened, deepened and put in context
from the experience and reality of each cultural, social and educational setting (p. 5; my
rendering). And it points upward to something this corpus does not hold: the contents are
organized «de acuerdo con los Planes y Programas establecidos para cada nivel educativo» —
in accordance with the Plans and Programs established for each educational level (p. 5).
There is a door above this door, and it was not retrieved.

So: a printed page in a state-issued book, framed by that book as expandable, under a
curricular instrument not in evidence. A name here is present. It is not thereby required.

## What a cell is

The literature material sits in the second trimester of the language area, under a heading
that is worth reading in full: «PROMOCIÓN DE LA EQUIDAD DE GÉNERO EN ARMONÍA CON LA MADRE
TIERRA EN EL ESTUDIO DE LAS CORRIENTES LITERARIAS» — promotion of gender equity in harmony
with Mother Earth in the study of literary currents (p. 24). Under it, a definition: a
literary current is «el conjunto de obras literarias de similares características tanto en el
estilo, temas e ideologías que expresan el espíritu de una determinada época» — the set of
literary works of similar characteristics in style, themes and ideologies, which express the
spirit of a given epoch (p. 24).

Then six numbered sections, 1 through 6, printing seven ruled tables between them — section
4, «Realismo y naturalismo», gives Realism and Naturalism a table each (pp. 27–28). Every
table carries the same lettered rows — origin, historical context, ideology, themes, styles,
characters, literary genres — and then a last row, lettered `h)` in six of the tables and
`i)` in Romanticism's, headed:

> «Obras emblemáticas y Autores representativos» — Emblematic works and representative
> authors. (p. 25)

Twenty of the twenty-five foreign rows coded here sit in those seven cells — most of the
door's aperture, and the part that says what the admissions are *for*. A name there is not a
book assigned; it is offered as evidence that a period existed. The Neoclassicism cell
prints five entries, one to a line (the fourth line running Swift and Kant together, as
printed):

> «Jean de la Fontaine. Francia. “Fábulas”
> Félix María Samaniego. España. “Fábulas”
> Daniel Defoe. España. “Robinson Crusoe”
> Jonathan Swift. Irlanda. “Los viajes de Gulliver” Kant.
> Alemania. “Crítica de la Razón Pura”» (p. 25)

Two things follow. First, the cell tags nationality itself — the document does this book's
own country-labeling for it, writer by writer. (Where tag and author diverge, the table
follows the author: Defoe's row is coded `origin: translated:english` though the cell prints
«España».) Second, the *Critique of Pure Reason* stands in a row of emblematic literary
works beside a shipwreck novel and a satire. The heading over the cell is the instruction
for how to read it: *emblematic*. Kant is printed there the way the fables and the
shipwreck are printed there, as one of the things the eighteenth century contained.

The unit's closing exercise names the same object. «Inspirados en el estudio de las
corrientes literarias, realicemos una dramatización corta acerca de una de ellas… En la
dramatización presentar a un autor y su producción literaria» — let us stage a short
dramatization about one of the currents; present *an author and his literary production*
(p. 29). What the task asks students to put on stage is a writer and a body of work.

## The shelf, and which way it points

Twenty-five of the twenty-nine rows are foreign; four are Bolivian. By the language the work
was written in, the twenty-five run: Spanish 10, French 6, English 5, German 2, Russian 2.
By genre: ten novels, five short-story rows, four essays, three plays, two poem-cycles, one
poem. Poe is the only foreign author holding two rows. Six rows carry no historical subject
at all, coded `none/` — the fables, the gothic, the romance, the modernist.

That the largest single language group is Spanish is the first structural fact about this
window: ten of the twenty-five foreign works are printed here in a book whose language is
their own. Whatever else this door is, it is not primarily a translation door — though the
English, German and Russian rows do arrive in translation, as the French ones do. What the
French rows carry most densely is French political time. Five of the six — Stendhal, Hugo,
Balzac, Flaubert, Zola — are coded to the Bourbon Restoration, the July Monarchy,
post-Napoleonic France, the Second Empire, and four of those five keep the trailing question
mark this table uses for a reading it will not yet call settled. The sixth French row, La
Fontaine's fables, carries no historical subject at all. Two Russian rows, Tolstoy and
Dostoevsky, sit inside the same Realism cell as Balzac and Flaubert, printed as «León
Tolstoi. Rusia» and «Fiodor Dostoievsky. Rusia» (p. 27), with no framing that distinguishes
them from their French neighbours.

Read the origin rows in order and the shelf turns out to have a direction. Neoclassicism:
«Francia. Siglo XVIII» (p. 25). Romanticism: «Alemania. Siglo XIX» (p. 25). Costumbrismo:
«España. Siglo XIX» (p. 26). Realism: «Francia, España, Inglaterra, México. Finales del Siglo
XIX» (p. 27). Naturalism: «Francia. Segunda mitad del Siglo XIX» (p. 28). Then Indigenismo
swings the compass west — «Latinoamérica. Siglo XX» (p. 28) — and the sixth section,
Modernismo, says out loud what that swing is:

> «Destaca entre las demás porque nació por primera vez en América y no es una imitación de
> ninguna otra corriente. Por el contrario, este movimiento influyó desde América hasta
> Europa.» — It stands out from the rest because it was born for the first time in America
> and is not an imitation of any other current. On the contrary, this movement influenced
> from America as far as Europe. (p. 29)

That sentence is the document's own, and Modernismo is the one current it describes as born
in America and exported rather than received. Modernismo's emblematic authors
are Rubén Darío of Nicaragua, José Martí of Cuba, Horacio Quiroga — printed «Uruguay», a
Uruguayan-born writer whose stories in this table are coded to the Río de la Plata and the
Misiones frontier — and Ricardo Jaimes Freyre, «Bolivia» (p. 29). The Indigenismo cell just
above holds Ciro Alegría of Peru alongside Alcides Arguedas and Jesús Lara of Bolivia
(p. 28). In these last two cells, and in none of the five before them, a Bolivian name and a
foreign name stand as equal instances of the same thing.

The Costumbrismo cell is the thinnest and the strangest. One name: «Andrés Bello.
Venezolano. Maestro de Simón Bolívar. “Principio del derecho de gentes”» (p. 26). The single
representative author of the current about local custom is identified not by a novel but by
his relation to the Liberator, and the work named is a treatise on the law of nations —
coded `essay` in the table. Read that label and that title together and the cell looks,
to this reader, closer to founding than to fiction; what the ministry meant by it the
document does not say.

One count from the table sits oddly against the unit's own heading about gender equity: of
the twenty-five foreign rows, twenty-four name a man and one names no author at all. The
sole woman named anywhere in these twenty-nine rows is Bolivian, and she appears five pages
after the last table, outside them — Mónica Paulina Gutiérrez Jiménez, credited on p. 34
under a text headed «Leyenda del pueblo ayoreo de Bolivia, adaptación libre de la autora»,
printed in full as a spelling exercise. The table records the placement. It does not record
why.

## What is actually printed

Seven rows carry a note recording that text is printed or extracted on the page, and six of
the seven are foreign. That is the shelf this volume sets out in words rather than in names:
a Samaniego fable (p. 25); the closing lines of «El cuervo» (p. 25); an adapted page of
*Hamlet* (p. 30); a four-speech playlet about litter, headed «Yo amo la tierra», for which
the document gives a website and no author (p. 32); and, on p. 36, two passages about radio.

The *Hamlet* page shows the whole mechanism in miniature. The instruction is «Con
expresividad leemos en clase y el siguiente fragmento, el cual es uno de los textos más
conocidos en la historia del teatro universal» — with expressiveness we read in class the
following fragment, one of the best-known texts in the history of world theatre (p. 30). The
heading over it reads «(Fragmento adaptado) Autor: Hamlet William Shakespeare», and the
source line credits not an edition of the play but a web article on what dramatic texts are.
The questions after it are «¿Has escuchado hablar de William Shakespeare?» and «¿Te resulta
triste o cómica la situación que se plantea?» — have you heard of Shakespeare; do you find
the situation sad or funny. Shakespeare enters Bolivia's official book under a section
heading about what a dramatic text is, on loan from a free web page, in a volume nobody is
permitted to sell.

## The errand, close-read

The clearest imported feeling in this table is not on the literature pages at all. It is on
p. 36, under the heading «LA IMPORTANCIA DE LOS MEDIOS RADIOFÓNICOS EN LA LUCHA CONTRA LA
DESIGUALDAD SOCIAL» — the importance of radio media in the struggle against social
inequality. Two foreign-authored extracts sit there. One, from Jimmi García Camargo's *La
radio por dentro y por fuera*, is coded to the invention of radio and runs through Hertz,
Marconi, Fleming and Lee de Forest. The other is from José Ignacio López Vigil's *Pasión por
la Radio*, and it is an argument against treating a microphone as a weapon:

> «Para estos jefes, los micrófonos eran cañones que disparaban ideas… Triste confusión de
> planos. Porque en el terreno militar se vence. Pero en el terreno de las ideas se
> convence.» — For these chiefs, microphones were cannons firing ideas… A sad confusion of
> registers. Because on military ground one conquers. But on the ground of ideas one
> convinces. (p. 36)

He does not stop there. Radio serving big capital, he writes, «nos bombardean a diario con
noticias que desinforman… Hacen terrorismo mediático. Pero nosotros no podemos ni queremos
jugar ese mismo juego, aunque sea al revés. Por ética» — they bombard us daily with news
that misinforms… They practise media terrorism. But we cannot and do not want to play that
same game, even in reverse. Out of ethics (p. 36).

That is what this window imports where the literature tables import periods: a scruple. The
unit's own reflection question, printed beneath the last table, asks students to pick a
current and a present-day problem and explain «¿Cómo contribuiríamos a resolver / aportar
soluciones a través de la literatura de dicha corriente?» — how would we contribute to
solving, or offer solutions, through the literature of that current (p. 29). The question
does not ask what the literature of that current is like. It asks what it could be put to.

## The object

Some readers were handed a textbook and some were sold one, and the two are different
childhoods. This is a book with the Minister's name on page 2 and *no sale* stamped beneath
it, one volume carrying the endocrine glands at page 216 and the War of the Pacific at page
408 and, at pages 25 through 29, seven tables in which the literature of the world has been
set down in something a person could read in an afternoon.

Call that a printed window. Printed is the strongest word the evidence will carry. This is a
volume that may not be sold, and the book that prints Tolstoy's name says of itself that it
«siempre puede ampliarse» — that it can always be widened. Whether any of it must be taught,
whether anyone is ever examined on it, is settled by the Plans and Programs that page 5
defers to, and those were not retrieved — the question
stops at a door this corpus could not open. What the volume itself does is narrower and
still not nothing. It prints the names. Kant is in there. Tolstoy is in there. A
fifteen-year-old in Bolivia turns to page 25 of the official text for their year, and the
literature of the world is five pages long, in a book nobody is allowed to sell them.

## Limits

- **One document, one year, one grade.** This table is the 2024 fourth-year *Texto de
  aprendizaje* and nothing else. It is not a national reading list, and the Plans and
  Programs it says it follows (p. 5) were not retrieved, so what force those give these pages
  is unknown here. Other grades' volumes would show other shelves; none were coded.
- **Presence is not obligation, and the force of these pages is unknown here.** All 29 rows
  are `anthology-only`; there is no Bolivian examination document in this corpus, and this
  document states no reading, examination or memorization mandate of its own. But it defers
  to Plans and Programs that were not retrieved (p. 5), so nothing above should be read as
  establishing either that a Bolivian student must read Flaubert, Kant or Shakespeare or
  that no instrument requires it. What is established is that those names are printed in a
  state-issued book, in cells whose stated function is to exemplify a period.
- **Printed presence, not teaching and not receipt.** No evidence here shows that any
  classroom opened these pages, that any teacher used them, or how the volume was
  distributed — how many copies, to whom, at what price or none. The essay's claims are
  about what this book prints.
- **No count here is comparable to another country's.** The door type is an anthology, not a
  codifier or a specification; the counts describe pages in one volume and are not offered as
  a measure of openness against any other table in this book.
- **`translated:` is doing unusual work in this table.** Ten of the 25 foreign rows are
  Spanish-language and are printed in this Spanish-language volume untranslated;
  `translated:spanish` here means foreign-origin, not rendered. The word should not be read
  as a translation claim.
- **One row's origin is a coding, not a finding.** «Yo amo la tierra» names no author and no
  original language; the document gives only a website. It is coded `translated:spanish` and
  counted among the 25, and it could as easily be domestic. Read the 25/4 split with that one
  row's worth of give.
- **The subject-era codings are provisional by their own notation.** Fifteen of the 29
  `subject_era` values carry a trailing question mark, and the confirm pass flagged 6 for
  revision, all 6 of which the reconciliation applied. Era groupings in the shelf walk lean on
  those codings and inherit their softness — including the French political-time reading,
  where four of the five historically coded rows keep the question mark, and Lara's *El
  repete*, whose link to the Chaco War is an analyst coding and not an indexed unit of the
  book.
- **Attribution inside the document is uneven, and this essay does not adjudicate it.** The
  tables print «Daniel Defoe. España», head the Modernismo table «EL INDIGENISMO» (p. 29),
  render Balzac as «Honoré de Balza» beside a fragment (p. 27) and Shakespeare as «Autor:
  Hamlet William Shakespeare» (p. 30). These are reported as observed features of what the
  cell is used for. No inference about the ministry's intentions, competence or motives is
  drawn from them, and none is available from this corpus.
- **Motive is absent, as everywhere in this book.** The document places names and states
  teaching purposes; it nowhere explains why these foreigners and not others, and nothing
  above should be read as supplying that reason. The shelf walk's sequence is a reading of
  the tables' own «a) Origen» rows, and it is not uniform: Indigenismo's origin (p. 28) is
  already «Latinoamérica», before Modernismo's. The one directional claim the document makes
  itself is the sentence in its Modernismo introduction about influence running from America
  to Europe (p. 29), quoted as printed.
