## REQUIRED

1. **Keep the Bavarian count corpus-scoped.**

   > Ten rows are all that Bavaria's tenth grade comes to, between the Realschule and the
   > Gymnasium, and eight of them are built this way: a category with the name left open.

   The authorities establish ten rows in this book's combined Grade 10 table, drawn
   from the Realschule and Gymnasium curricula. They do not establish that all of
   Bavarian tenth-grade literature “comes to” those ten rows. Recast this as a finding
   about the table or the two captured curricula, without widening it into the whole
   grade.

2. **Do not turn “not literary authors” into a biographical absolute.**

   > The only two people the Bavarian plan names are not writers at all.

   > The plan will name the men who diagram how
   > messages travel. It will not name the author of a single message.

   The DE fact sheet supports “not literary authors” and identifies Watzlawick and
   Schulz von Thun as communication theorists. It does not support the broader claim
   that they were “not writers at all,” and “message” makes the supported absence of a
   named literary author needlessly ambiguous. Keep the contrast explicitly literary.

3. **Scope the Belgian and Swiss frameworks instead of presenting two sampled doors as
   complete national answers.**

   > Belgium answers the book's question twice, once per school system, and both answers
   > are nobody.

   > Switzerland also answers twice.

   The BE authorities cover the francophone and Flemish frameworks; they do not state
   that those two exhaust Belgium's school systems. The CH authorities expressly say
   that Switzerland has no single
   national codifier and test Zürich plus the common plan for French-speaking
   Switzerland. Name those jurisdictions; neither fact sheet authorizes a complete
   country-level “twice.”

4. **Remove the unsupported motive and genre total from Norway.**

   > Norway went furthest and made the refusal statutory.

   > Two written forms of its own language, an Indigenous language in translation, every
   > major genre — a statute thorough about everything except a name.

   NOR01-07 is a statutory no-list curriculum; that does not prove a deliberate
   “refusal.” Its cited Year 10 aim names novels, short stories, poetry, and other texts,
   not “every major genre.” State the statutory category mandate and the absence of a
   prescribed author without assigning intent or exhaustiveness.

5. **Do not call Italy's foreign shelf fully named or wholly ancient.**

   > Italy names its foreign shelf in full, and everything on it is ancient.

   The Italian table contains four mandatory named ancient items or corpora (the
   Homeric epics, Attic tragedy, the *Aeneid*, and the Bible), one open choice of
   primary Greek and Latin authors, and an additional open row for modern authors
   including foreign ones. The defensible finding is that
   the named foreign individuals/items are ancient while the modern foreign option is
   unnamed; “in full” erases both open formulas.

6. **Do not narrate Japan's combined course/publisher table as one exhaustive national
   anthology.**

   > Japan's ninth-grade table is drawn from the national course of study and the two big
   > publishers' anthologies, and it names two foreign texts, both mandatory, both Chinese:

   > Thirteen domestic selections surround them.

   > Everything else arrives, if it arrives, without a name.

   The first sentence correctly discloses three source types, but the next two collapse
   their fifteen retained rows into one shelf. The authorities also do not support the
   adjective “big.” The JP fact sheet expressly says the
   sources do not establish a single national title list. “Surround them” invents one
   anthology arrangement, and “everything else” widens this combined slice into a
   national exhaustive claim. Preserve the two foreign rows and their force while
   keeping the national course and publisher selections distinct.

7. **Keep Kazakhstan as a partial, mixed-force corridor and state the memorization
   finding at document level.**

   > Kazakhstan closes this interlude as its opposite. Its grade-nine program names
   > generously: sixty-two rows, twenty-seven of them foreign — twenty-one Russian, four
   > English, two Kyrgyz.

   > Twelve rows carry a by-heart requirement, and all twelve are
   > foreign: eleven Russian, and one excerpt of Shakespeare's sonnets.

   > Even so, the corridor is legible: a state that names the world
   > wall to wall, and signs only foreign words into its children's memory.

   The counts are `origin` categories and must be called Russian-coded, English-coded,
   and Kyrgyz-coded rather than nationalities or classroom languages. The 62 rows come
   from four appendices, with 32 mandatory and 30 choice-list rows; the
   fact sheet further warns that Appendices 47 and 48 are not complete transcriptions
   and that force in Appendix 47 is unsettled for ten rows. “A state ... wall to wall”
   overextends that partial mixed-force slice. “Into its children's memory” also sounds
   like observed uptake, which the authorities forbid. The supported consequence is:
   in these transcribed rows, all twelve work-specific by-heart requirements are
   foreign-coded, while none of the thirty-five domestic rows has one.

8. **Repair the closing aggregate, which contradicts the chapter's own five-name
   finding.**

   > The eight countries before Kazakhstan are not indifferent to reading. Their mandates
   > are strict — two complete works a year in French-speaking Switzerland, four or five in
   > francophone Belgium, a laureate in Bavaria — strict about everything but the name.

   Italy, Japan, and Mongolia do name the five foreign figures counted earlier, and the
   eight systems do not share one force type. The aggregate finding can be that the
   named foreign shelf contracts to five people across the eight captured tables while
   open formulas, quotas, and anthology choices carry much of the remaining reading;
   it cannot say all eight are strict about everything except names.

9. **Remove unsupported claims about who makes every downstream choice.**

   > Vietnam, chapters ago, required eight named
   > foreign literatures and left the name-slots for a textbook author to fill; the systems
   > gathered here mostly decline to build slots at all.

   > Whoever fills those slots, it will not be the ministry.

   > The choosing this book set out to catch does not stop happening in these systems. It moves
   > one desk down: to the publisher's anthology, the teacher's cupboard, the pupil's
   > declared interests, where no ministry document records it.

   The instrument memo supports eight Vietnamese national-literature author-group rows,
   but these authorities do not establish that a textbook author fills all of them.
   Nor do they prove who later fills every open slot, that the same institutional
   handoff occurs in all eight systems, or that no ministry records a later choice.
   “Decline to build slots at all” is also too broad unless “slots” is narrowed to named
   national-literature quotas: these systems do build open formulas, annual book quotas,
   and anthology places. The present wording makes the prohibited move from list
   architecture to unobserved practice.

10. **Restore a human/object spine and remove writer/table process as protagonist.**

    > Most chapters of this book exist because a state wrote names down. This interlude
    > collects the systems that would not.

    > Three of this book's twenty tables are empty — Belgium, Switzerland, Norway — not
    > because nothing is read there, but because the defining documents name nothing that
    > could become a row.

    The Bavarian pupil supplies a viable opening, but then disappears while chapters,
    tables, programs, plans, and instruments perform almost every action. That breaches
    the binding story-spine rule. Keep the aggregate as the finding, but let unnamed
    books, a pupil, or another physical/human continuity carry the reader across the
    door-notes; documents and counts should testify inside that continuity.

11. **Reorder the count-led passages so consequence arrives first.**

    > Ten rows are all that Bavaria's tenth grade comes to, between the Realschule and the
    > Gymnasium, and eight of them are built this way: a category with the name left open.

    > Three of this book's twenty tables are empty — Belgium, Switzerland, Norway — not
    > because nothing is read there, but because the defining documents name nothing that
    > could become a row.

    > Mexico's synthetic program of 2024 gives the third year of secondary school nine rows,
    > every one of them open. Six of the nine permit a translated work; none names one.

    These are the clearest instances of the cold sweep's count-before-consequence
    disease. Lead with what the unnamed category, statutory requirement, or open choice
    does to the pupil's encounter; place the row arithmetic afterward.

12. **Reduce the visible boundary apparatus to one load-bearing sentence.**

    > Three of this book's twenty tables are empty — Belgium, Switzerland, Norway — not
    > because nothing is read there, but because the defining documents name nothing that
    > could become a row.

    > Four appendices of a much larger ministerial order stand behind these counts — the
    > appendices for the Abai schools and for Uyghur-, Uzbek-, and Tajik-language literature
    > were not transcribed — so this is one corridor of a multilingual school system, not
    > the whole building.

    Both are load-bearing scope fences, before the ending adds a further statement about
    what ministry documents do not record. The cold-sweep ruling allows at most one.
    Retain the Kazakhstan boundary because it prevents a national overclaim; integrate
    the empty-table distinction as the interlude's positive finding rather than another
    forensic qualification.

13. **Remove the running page key.**

    > The fourteenth and
    > last, sixteen pages beginning at page 130, is the excerpt of a Sanskrit play:
    > Kalidasa's Recognition of Shakuntala.

    “Beginning at page 130” is citation freight. The prose can keep the concrete
    sixteen-page extent and the last-selection position without the page reference.

14. **Use the authority's wording for the Swiss age condition.**

    > French-speaking Switzerland's common plan is the most
    > precise counting of unnamed books in this corpus: at least two complete works a year,
    > adapted to the pupils' age.

    > Complete, counted, age-rated — and untitled.

    The quota and age condition are supported, but “most precise” is an ungrounded
    superlative (Belgium also supplies a numerical complete-work requirement). PER says
    the works are “adapted to the pupils' age”; “age-rated” suggests a formal rating
    system the fact sheet does not establish. Drop the superlative and use “age-suited”
    or “adapted to their age.”
