Three Ways to Hand a Child the World · Chapter 05

Vietnam: A Bookcase, and Eight Names for the World

Before it says anything at all about foreign literature, Vietnam's national program for Ngữ văn — the single subject that carries the Vietnamese language and its literature — furnishes the room. The minimum equipment of the subject, it says, is a reference bookcase: stories, poetry, drama, reportage, argument, picture books. It also wants portraits of the program's major writers, and pictures illustrating some of the major works — six titles follow as examples, between a such as and an ellipsis, and the program sets no number of pictures and no place to hang them. At the desks it wants whole books, telling teachers to limit teaching by extract.

So furnish the room yourself, because the document won't. Put the bookcase where you like and the pictures wherever you last saw pictures in a school. The six titles under them are not arbitrary: the same six appear earlier in the program as its compulsory works, the only literature it obliges both a textbook's author and a teacher to carry out. An anonymous poem from the Lý dynasty; Trần Quốc Tuấn's address to his officers; Nguyễn Trãi's great proclamation; Truyện Kiều; Nguyễn Đình Chiểu's funeral oration for the fighters of Cần Giuộc; Hồ Chí Minh's declaration of independence. Four of the six sit against an invasion or an occupation — Song, Mongol, Ming, French — and a fifth against the end of a world war.

The rest of the world's literature is in this room too. It is not among the six, and on the day the program was signed it was not yet a book. It was one line.

The line

It instructs: choose at least one work for each of the following literatures — English, French, American, Greek, Russian, Japanese, Chinese, Indian.

Eight literatures. A floor of one work apiece. No titles, no authors, no centuries — eight names, and not one book.

The two lines above it are built the other way round. Vietnamese folk literature gets five categories, each with a minimum attached — folk-tale genres, folk verses, an epic, an ethnic-minority verse narrative, a script for one of the traditional theatre forms. Vietnamese written literature gets thirteen names, Nguyễn Trãi and Nguyễn Du and Hồ Xuân Hương among them. Then, in the same tier and the same grammar, foreign literature gets eight countries. At home the program guarantees particular writers. Abroad it guarantees only the literatures a work must come from. Nothing in the program explains the difference, and a list cannot be made to give a reason for itself.

Nor is the floor a promise of space. The program asks for a relative balance between Vietnamese and foreign literature, between East and West, and then defines its own word against the obvious reading of it: balance means a suitable proportion, not an equal one. A ministry writing down that a quota is a floor and not a scale.

Whose job is a name

Take one of the eight names — Nga, Russia — and follow it toward the bookcase. The program grades its own force in a single sentence, and the addressee changes as you go down: compulsory works bind textbook authors and teachers alike; compulsory-selection works, the eight names among them, bind one person — the textbook author, who must choose a work from each named literature; suggested works bind nobody. The circular that promulgates all this, signed by the Minister of Education and Training at the end of 2018, addresses its foreign-literature instruction not to a country's teachers but to whoever is writing a book for them. The word Nga lands on somebody's desk.

And the program's own suggestions, at the back of the document, do not answer it. They are a menu grouped by genre, alphabetical by Vietnamese title, calling itself only illustrative examples: in the grade 10–12 fiction list Hemingway sits between Odysseus and Số đỏ, and nothing says he is the American one. The quota names literatures, the menu names books, and no line runs between them — One Hundred Years of Solitude is there, a Vietnamese title and a bracketed G. Marquez, written in Spanish, which answers to none of the eight names, while India, one of the eight, gets no named example in the grades covered here. A suggestion matching no requirement; a requirement with no suggestion.

Two teachers

The two Russian-language stories in the menu are printed as flatly as the rest — a dash, a Vietnamese title, a bracket: The First Teacher, C. Aitmatov; The Man in a Case, A. Chekhov. That both were written in Russian is a fact about language and nothing more. The document does not say that either answers to Nga, or that anything else does. Chinghiz Aitmatov, for his part, was Kyrgyz, and wrote in both Kyrgyz and Russian. In this list a foreign author is an initial, a surname and a bracket — unhoused.

The titles rhyme before anyone opens the books: one names a teacher, the other a man who has put himself away. Open them and the rhyme holds. Chekhov's Belikov teaches Greek and keeps every part of himself in a case, and his fear settles over a whole town. Aitmatov's Duishen is the opposite man and the opposite failure — a demobilised soldier with barely more schooling than his pupils, who turns a stable into a classroom and teaches from almost nothing, carrying children across an icy stream on his back.

Now look at what surrounds them. The grade 10–12 Vietnamese fiction here is heavy with the wars: Anh Đức, Nguyễn Thi, Phan Tứ, Nguyễn Minh Châu, Bảo Ninh, Nguyễn Khải — six entries whose subject is the anti-US war or its aftermath. Beside a domestic shelf that keeps returning to the war, these two imports are both about a room with a teacher in it, one of them a warning and one of them a debt.

The last instruction is the loosest

Come back to the room. There is one more thing the program asks a pupil to do with a book, and from grade 6 to grade 12 it asks it in the same sentence, word for word: "Learn by heart some favourite verse passages or poems from the program."

Seven grades, seven identical lines. No work is named, no number is set, and the sentence does not say who decides that a poem is a favourite — the pupil, the textbook, or the teacher. The primary grades are written the other way: grade 1 asks for four or five short passages already studied, and the count climbs year by year to at least ten or twelve longer ones by grade 5. The counting stops when primary school does. What replaces it is the favourite.

So no work here carries a memorization mandate of its own — which means only that no text is named and no quantity tied to one, not that nothing is learned by heart in Vietnam. Learning by heart is itself one of the outcomes the program requires.

That is the shape of this window, top to bottom: a named domestic canon; eight unnamed literatures, addressed to a person with a manuscript; a menu the document says is only examples; and an instruction to learn a poem by heart with the poem left blank.

The blank is the last thing this document does, and it is the same move it made in the quota line: require the thing, decline to name it. The Vietnamese program says only that the blank must be filled. It does not say by whom.
