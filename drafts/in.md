# India: The Lesson That Won't Be Asked

Before an Indian fifteen-year-old reads a line of Anton Chekhov, they are asked to fill in a
table. The columns are printed for them: **Customs similar to Indian ones** and **Customs
different from Indian ones**. Above the table sits a short article about Russian weddings —
the groom climbing an apartment block's stairs, answering a riddle at each landing. Then the
question: "Do
you think Indian and Russian weddings have any customs in common? With the help of a partner,
fill in the table below."

Only then does the play begin: Lomov arrives at Chubukov's house in a dress-suit to propose
to Natalya, and the three of them quarrel about a meadow and a dog until the proposal is
nearly lost. The unit ends with a group discussion — "the qualities one should look for in a
marriage partner," under headings including *Attitude to money and wealth*.

The errand is written on the page before the work starts: a Russian farce about two
landowning families, brought into an Indian classroom to be set beside an Indian wedding, in
ruled columns, by a child with a partner.

## The door is a book, and a subtraction

India's window has an unusual hinge. The Central Board of Secondary Education issues a
curriculum for each subject code, and that curriculum's last page carries a list headed
**निर्धारित पुस्तकें** — *prescribed books* — and a note naming lessons from which no questions
will be asked. The books themselves are published by the National Council of Educational
Research and Training. The board that examines names the anthology, and then it subtracts.

The English syllabus prints its chapter list in full, prose and poems, book by book. The
Hindi syllabi do not. Hindi Course A names *Kshitij Part 2* and *Kritika Part 2* and stops —
then names, from *Kritika*, only the lessons it removes, one a satire called "George the
Fifth's Nose". That is the hole in this corpus: no saved defining document lists *Kritika*'s
remaining contents, so the rows once coded from it were quarantined. The only *Kritika*
chapters the syllabus names are the ones it takes away.

No defining document here mandates
memorisation of any named row — not a title, not an excerpt, not a stated quantity of texts
by heart. What the English paper asks for is an extract and a short written answer.

## Streams in one corridor

A school's window here is not one window. In the same Class 10 corridor a pupil may sit Hindi
Course A — mother-tongue Hindi, with *Kshitij* and *Kritika* — or Hindi Course B, with
*Sparsh* and *Sanchayan*; English Language and Literature is a separate code with its own two
readers. Same board, different worlds. How many pupils walk which door, and when each stream
sits its paper, these documents do not say.

This corpus speaks for the English stream, partly for Hindi Course A, not at all for Hindi
Course B: forty-three rows, thirty-one from the English syllabus — twenty-two in *First
Flight*, nine in *Footprints Without Feet* — and twelve from *Kshitij Part 2*, every one
Class 10, prescribed, unmarked for memorisation.

## What comes through the English door

Twenty of the thirty-one English-syllabus rows arrive from outside India, already in
English. Nineteen rows across the whole corpus are domestic — the twelve Hindi ones, plus
seven Indian pieces inside the English readers: a Goan baker, Coorg, Assam tea, a Tamil
girl's bus ride, the sermon at Benares, Ruskin Bond's thief, Bholi.

And four rows — four, out of forty-three — come from a language that is neither English nor
Indian: a Mexican story from Spanish, a Dutch diary, a Russian one-act play, a French short
story. López y Fuentes, Anne Frank, Chekhov, Maupassant.

The syllabus names this shape itself, in its objectives, as an aim: the course sets out to
"develop sensitivity to, and appreciation of world literature representing varieties of
English and cultures embedded in lt." (the typo is the document's). World literature, in the
sentence that admits it, is scoped to varieties of English. Further down, the objectives turn
homeward: "Develop appreciation for Indian languages(multilingualism), translations and
Indian Literature."

The poetry section reads clearest: *First Flight* carries ten poems, every one from abroad
in English — seven by American poets (Frost twice, Wells, Berryman, Rich, Sandburg,
Nash), one each from a Welsh, an Australian and an Irish poet. No Indian poet holds a poetry
slot in the English reader. The Indian poetry is next door, in Hindi, where Surdas and
Tulsidas open *Kshitij* and all twelve rows are domestic.

By subject the table supports one split, and only one: each row's `subject_era` code either
names a place-and-period setting or begins `none/`. Thirteen of the twenty-four rows from
abroad have no historical setting at all — a tiger, a fog, a dragon, a snowfall — while
fourteen of the nineteen domestic rows do carry one, from ancient Benares to Bismillah Khan's
shehnai in 1947. Whatever else that is, it is not foreign history set against Indian
timelessness. And no row here is coded to Partition — a fact about one Class 10 year with a
quarantined reader in it, and nothing larger.

## Chekhov, twice

What this corpus can do that a list cannot is watch one writer walk through two doors of the
same school. Through the English door, Chekhov is a chapter with a page number —
*First Flight*'s last unit, printed on the contents page, the one author in the book who
wrote in Russian, as ANTON CHEKOV. The reader's own blurb tells the class what the farce is
about: "the tendency of wealthy families to seek ties with other wealthy families, to increase
their estates by encouraging marriages that make good economic sense." He is examinable: the
paper draws extracts from drama and prose and short answers from *First Flight*.

Then the unit closes, and the last page is addressed to the teacher rather than the child.
Its first heading: "WHAT WE HAVE DONE — Given you a play by the famous Russian writer, Anton
Chekhov." Its second: "WHAT YOU CAN DO — Dictate the biogrophical information given below.
Students should then guess the name of the playwright." What follows is a paragraph with a
hole where a name should be — born at Stratford-on-Avon, married to an older woman, retired,
dead, "leaving behind a body of work that still stands as a pinnacle in
world literature."

The one Russian in the book is handed over, and the class is invited to guess the Englishman.
The exercise assumes recognition: it withholds the name because it expects the name back.
That is what a shared shelf looks like from inside — not a text on the syllabus, but a riddle
whose answer is assumed to be in the room already.

Through the Hindi B door, Chekhov appears exactly once, and not as a chapter. On the last
page of that curriculum, under the heading "Note: questions will not be asked from the
following lessons," in the block for the prescribed reader *Sparsh Part 2*, between Bihari's
couplets and a Mahadevi Varma poem, is the line **अंतोन चेखव- गिरगिट** — Anton Chekhov,
"Girgit" — and beside it in brackets, *the whole lesson*.

The corpus holds no row for it, and whether the current *Sparsh* reprint still prints the
story these documents do not settle. What they settle is the naming. In one stream
Chekhov is an examinable text with a warm-up table about weddings; in the stream across the
corridor, his name is printed for the sole purpose of being fenced off the paper. Placement
is all a syllabus shows. It gives no reasons, and none is inferred here.

## Underneath the syllabus

There is a layer below the rows, and it is where some of the world actually lives. In the
exercises after "A Tiger in the Zoo" a second poem is printed whole — Rilke's "The Panther,"
in Stephen Mitchell's English — set beside Leslie Norris's tiger. It is not a chapter and not
a listed set-text row; whether an exercise insert can reach the paper is not a question these
documents answer.

The layer runs the other way too. Inside the Chekhov unit, the writing exercise hands the
class a passage from Tagore's *The Home and the World* to turn into a script — Bimala
demanding that foreign goods be cleared out of the market, her husband refusing: "To
tyrannise for the country is to tyrannise over the country." A swadeshi argument, in the
middle of the one foreign-language play in the book. The anthology put them on facing pages;
it does not say why, and neither will I.

The shelf closes on paperwork. *Footprints Without Feet* still tells its teachers, in a
preface, that it represents "writers from across the world—Indian, British, French, American
and Japanese." Its nine rows here are Indian, British, French and American. And the English
reader's last printed page, after Chekhov and after Shakespeare's unnamed biography, is the
Fundamental Rights of the Constitution of India, including the right "for protection of
interests of minorities to conserve their language, script and culture."

## The lesson that won't be asked

If you ever watched a teacher go down the contents page with a pen — not this one, not this
one — you saw the only editorial act in a school year a child witnesses directly: the shelf
shrinking in front of them, for reasons nobody explains, shortly before the syllabus becomes
real.

India's documents perform that act in writing, at the bottom of the page, in a box, and those
chapters keep a strange afterlife: still printed, still in the bag, weightless. In the
syllabus that governed Class 10 in 2024-25, one such name in the Hindi B box is Anton
Chekhov's — and one corridor away, in English, the same writer arrives with a warm-up table
about weddings.

## Limits

- **An edition seam runs through this essay.** The three curricula are the 2024-25 documents,
  and they prove prescription, exclusion, exam weight and objectives. Everything
  quoted from inside a reader — the wedding table, the Chekhov blurb and teacher's page, Rilke,
  Tagore, the *Footprints* preface, the Fundamental Rights page — comes from **Reprint 2026-27**
  archives: later printings of the same 2022 revised edition the 2024-25 reprints carry, but no
  2024 printing was retrieved. Pagination and apparatus are unverified for the book a 2024-25
  pupil held, and the 12 *Kshitij* rows are syllabus titles matched to a 2026-27 contents page.
  Read the syllabus claims as 2024-25, the printed-page claims as the current reprint's.
- **One grade, one year.** Every row is Class 10, 2024-25. The same syllabus prescribes
  *Beehive* and *Moments* for Class 9; this is not a pupil's secondary shelf, only the year
  they are examined on at 15–16.
- **The Hindi side is under-receipted.** Hindi Course A prescribes *Kritika Part 2* and this
  corpus has zero rows from it — three rows were quarantined because no saved defining document
  names their titles — so *Kritika* is missing from every number above. Hindi Course B's readers
  were not receipted at all, only its prescribed-books line and exclusion note, which makes the
  Chekhov finding one about two documents rather than two bookshelves. Read the domestic/foreign
  split as incomplete on the Hindi side, with no inference either way.
- **The Hindi PDFs' text layer is corrupt.** Both extract as scrambled Devanagari, so every
  Hindi quotation was read off the rendered page image; those pages are named in Receipts for
  the transcheck.
- **`mandatory` means prescribed, not certain.** The paper's short answers are drawn from the
  English readers as choices, so any row may go unasked. `exam_status` records only that a
  work is prescribed and not excluded.
- **The rows are chapters; the anthology is bigger.** Rilke's "The Panther", Etzioni's "Good
  Grief" and the Tagore extract sit in the exercise apparatus and hold no rows. No inventory of
  that apparatus was made, so the row count is not a count of everything physically printed —
  and nothing here establishes which way the gap runs.
- **No comparison of openness, no motive.** An anthology door and an exam-codifier door are not
  commensurable, and no row count here is offered against another country's. Why the Chekhov
  story is off the Hindi B paper, why the English reader's poetry carries no Indian poet, and
  why a swadeshi passage sits inside the Russian unit are questions no document answers.
- **Length.** This draft passes the 2,200-word cap, and Receipts earned the excess: two
  curricula in two scripts plus a third cited for its exclusion note, three separately editioned
  readers each pinned to an archive, and a corrupt Devanagari text layer forcing every Hindi
  quotation to be receipted twice, original and rendering.
