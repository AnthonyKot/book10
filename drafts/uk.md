# England: The Door Opens Inward

There is a pot in Manchester. It was made in Nigeria, a museum in that city holds it, and a poet
from that city wrote a whole poem talking to it — not about it, to it. The exam board's teacher
guide puts it in one flat line: the poem "was commissioned for the city's museum and is
written as a conversation with a Nigerian pot in their collection."

Since 2023 that poem has been available to English classrooms as a set text, and in summer 2025
the first cohort sat an exam that could ask them about it — closed book, nothing on the desk but
what the paper supplies. The pot is the only thing in this story that crossed a border.

All seventy-three rows in this table are coded `domestic`. Not one arrived in translation.
Measured through this document, the window England opens on world literature is shut — and yet
the room is full of elsewhere.

## The door

This table is the AQA GCSE English Literature specification (8702), and AQA is an awarding body,
not a ministry: "Assessment objectives (AOs) are set by Ofqual and are the same across all GCSE
English Literature specifications and all exam boards." Each board writes its own menu, and
nothing below is England's shelf: it is one shelf in England, in one year. "England" is my word,
not the file's — the corpus is coded `uk`, and nothing in it covers Scotland, Wales or Northern
Ireland.

And it is a menu, not a mandate. A student takes one play from a list of six, one nineteenth-
century novel from a list of seven, one modern text from a choice of twelve, and one poetry
cluster — "students should study all 15 poems in their chosen cluster and be prepared to write
about any of them in the examination." Six, seven, twelve, forty-five: the 2025 menu runs to
seventy items. The table carries seventy-three, because it also keeps the three modern texts whose
last exam was 2024 — a list caught mid-change. So seventy rows are the space of the possible; the
set-text list a real teenager carries is eighteen of them, fifteen of those poems. Eighteen rows,
not eighteen works: one modern row, *Telling Tales*, is an anthology of seven stories.

Two more sentences shape the door. Page 8: "All assessments are closed book: any stimulus
materials required will be provided as part of the assessment." And the aim the specification
writes for itself, that students should come to "appreciate the depth and power of the English
literary heritage." Hold that aim beside a table with no translated row and the shelf's
Englishness looks less like omission than like the subject's own definition — my framing, not a
reason the board gives. What the document proves is the measurement.

## What comes through it anyway

Sixteen of the seventy-three rows name, in their era code, a specific place outside Britain and
Ireland. More rows carry foreign material; those sixteen are where the table itself says so.
They arrive in three unlike ways.

**Elsewhere as stage.** Of the six Shakespeare plays a school can set, not one is set in England.
The era column gives Verona, Venice, Messina, Rome, eleventh-century Scotland — and, for
*The Tempest*, `renaissance-colonialism?`, question mark and no country at all. Add Browning's
Ferrara, and a pattern appears: England's most domestic authors do their thinking abroad. The
passport is the author's, never the setting's.

**Wars with a foreign address.** Tennyson takes the Crimea in 1854; Owen the Western Front;
Orwell the Russian revolution; Armitage the 2003 invasion of Iraq; Duffy a photographer's
cold-war conflict zones; Rumens an exile from a homeland the table records as unspecified.
Britain was a belligerent in the Crimean War, on the Western Front and in Iraq; the allegory, the
photographs and the exile the codes leave elsewhere or unplaced. What the repaired codes fix is
address: each puts a conflict, a revolution or an exile outside Britain, or at a foreign location
the table declines to specify. The sharpest is Beatrice Garland's *Kamikaze* — the corpus's only
Pacific-war row, the interior life of a Japanese pilot's family. Imtiaz Dharker's *A Century
Later* does the same office for the 2012 Taliban attack on a schoolgirl. Both are coded
`domestic`, because both are English poems.

**The world as citizens.** The 2023–2025 exchange of texts grew this pile. The Worlds and
Lives cluster, first taught in 2023 and first examined in 2025, puts fifteen poems in front of a
class, and several of the new ones reach migration, heritage or elsewhere through people living
in Britain and their families. A conversation on an afternoon train from Purley to Victoria in
1955. Leeds in 1869, a great-great-grandfather with eyes "dry with dust / from twelve hours
combing flax", imagined by a descendant born and raised in the same city. Guyana revisited and
found full of "car tyres, plastic bottles, styrofoam cups." Trinidad carried in a pocket.

The modern-text list moved the same way and left a datable seam: three rows marked *last exam
2024* — Bennett, Stephens, Ishiguro — and three *first teaching 2023, first exam 2025*: Odimba's
*Princess & The Hustler*, on the 1963 Bristol bus boycott; de Waal's *My Name is Leon*; Pinnock's
*Leave Taking*. The board's taster booklet for it prints the scene it wants taught, a daughter
turning on her mother: "You're too busy bowing and scraping to your beloved England. And where's it got you?"

England's classroom did not import that line. It grew it.

## The errand: one pot, one collection

The teacher guide published with Collins walks through the poem. Its summary: "The pot is used to
explore cultural repatriation, colonialism, and migration." The speaker "knows only 'half of the story' of how it reached England." The guide works through the
euphemisms a museum label might use, and is blunt about where they land: "the 'white' boat going
to 'England' reminds us of the truth of cultural theft." Then the poem speaks to the pot about
itself, "you're not really one of us," and asks it, twice, to answer: "growl if you hear me," and then, each on its own line, "pot? / pot?"

So here is what this window imports. Not the pot: the guide places it in the museum's collection,
and what reaches a classroom is a poem about it — a poem that same guide reads as staged at the
display glass. Not a foreign literature either: a set of questions about a foreign object and
whether it should be here, asked in English by a poet the guide prints in lower case, shamshad
khan, described there as "a second generation British Asian from Manchester", writing for a
museum in her own city.

That is the mechanism in miniature. The world is admitted constantly, and admitted as content; it
clears customs in the hands of a writer who already belongs. And printed at the start of every AQA
mark scheme is a Statement of importance: "High-quality English literature is writing which
displays recognisable literary qualities and, although shaped by particular contexts, transcends
them and speaks about the universality of the human condition." AQA nowhere offers that as its
reason for setting no translated text. But hold the sentence against the shelf — my reading, not
the board's argument — and the two fit: if literature transcends its context, one board's menu can
stay entirely English-language without its compilers conceding a gap. You can reach Guyana, the Pacific and a Nigerian pot without setting a
translated page.

## Where the door does open

One part of this course has no set texts at all. Unseen poetry is a whole section of Paper 2 and
cannot appear in a corpus row, because nothing is prescribed: two poems, unseen, sat cold. The
specification guarantees the two poems; it says nothing about where they come from. The board's teaching guide for the section offers a starter
collection — one it calls non-prescriptive, telling teachers they may substitute poems of their
own — and alongside Blake, Hardy, Rossetti and Owen it prints Langston Hughes's 'Mother to Son'
and Kyle Dargan's 'Poem Resisting Arrest'. The Worlds and Lives guide does the same at the foot of
every set-cluster entry, under "Other poems for comparison": Czeslaw Milosz beside Raman Mundair,
Rabindranath Tagore beside Liz Berry, Maya Angelou beside Caleb Femi.

None of those names is a row in this table, and none can be: a suggestion in a guide is not a set
text. So the finding is narrower than an admission. AQA's guides suggest foreign poets without
prescribing them, and the unseen section — assessed, heavily — leaves the identity of the examined
poems open until the paper is opened. What matters here is prescription, not assessment.

## Fifteen, sixteen, and no book on the desk

You were fifteen or sixteen once, in a room with a list that looked like the only list there was.
It never is. Somebody chose a board, somebody chose one play from six, and the set texts you would
be tested on narrowed to eighteen rows — one play, one novel, one modern text, fifteen poems —
before you walked in. Two more poems, named to nobody, waited on the paper.

If your own classroom's list ran on a subject named after your country, the test is not how many
foreigners were let in but how they travelled: as scenery, as wars the codes place abroad, and —
in the rows first examined in 2025 — as family history carried by people living in Britain. Not
one came carrying a translator's name.

And in summer 2025 a teenager could be asked about a pot in a Manchester collection that a poet
had spent a whole poem begging to answer: *growl if you hear me.* No anthology and no book was
allowed on the desk; the named poem was printed on the paper, and the poem you set beside it, you
brought in your head. An exam is not a bad place to be asked that question: it is a place where
you have to answer, on behalf of something brought here and never asked.

## Limits

- **One board, one menu, one year — caught mid-change.** AQA 8702 is one awarding body's
  specification; other boards set different books under the same Ofqual conditions, and this
  corpus holds none of them. No row count here may be set against another country's document as a
  measure of openness: a board menu and a ministry programme are different objects. Coding claims
  are made of all 73 rows, claims about a 2025 pupil of the 70; the three-row seam no cohort chose
  from sits in the first count only. Every row is `choice-list`: the piles above describe what is
  available to set, never what a classroom read.
- **No 2025 question paper is in `resources/`.** The specification proves the cluster was first
  examinable in summer 2025; nothing saved here shows that any pupil was asked about *pot*. The
  opening and closing scenes are what the documents make possible, not a report of the paper. Nor
  is the pot itself ever in a classroom, or observed by anyone here: the guide places it in the
  museum's collection, and a school receives a poem about it.
- **The memorization column is empty by ruling, not observation.** Reconciliation changed all 73
  `memorize` values from `excerpt` to `no`; the rule required an explicit mandate to learn a named
  text, excerpt or quantity by heart. AQA's exam is closed book and supplies the Paper 1 extract
  and the named Paper 2 poem, but requires "textual references, including quotations" — which in
  practice sends students in holding quotations in their heads. Practice, not a mandate.
- **The codes are interpretive, and code the work rather than the writer.** Seventeen `subject_era`
  values changed in reconciliation, several toward `none/…` or `unspecified` (*Bayonet Charge*,
  *Poppies*, *The Emigree*, *Tissue*); the count of 16 rests on those codes and on a criterion of
  mine, and undercounts works whose foreign setting the code does not name.
  `origin: domestic` records a text set and examined here as English Literature — several authors
  on this shelf were born outside the United Kingdom.
- **Motive is outside the corpus.** The specification states aims but never explains why a shelf
  is composed as it is, and no rationale for any set text was located in `resources/`. The
  Statement of importance says what the subject values, not why a text was or was not set; the
  link drawn above is the essay's own reading, as is the reading of the subject's title. The
  2023–2025 exchange of three texts for three is datable from row notes; its reasons are not.
  Guides suggest and do not set: their foreign names cannot be receipted as anything a student is
  examined on.
- **The label says `uk`; the essay says England.** Flagged at first use above. No document covering
  Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland is in `resources/`.
- **Length.** `AGENT.md` caps an essay at 2,200 words and allows about 3,000 where the shelf earns
  the overrun, provided Limits says what earned it. This file runs to about 2,990 words, under
  that ceiling; the prose alone is about 1,700, inside the cap, and the overrun is apparatus. That
  is what the receipting costs here — a 73-row corpus across four exam components, a menu change
  affecting six rows, four AQA documents quoted, and a criterion for the count of 16 with its
  exclusions. The 73 / 70 / 18 distinction, formerly restated in four places, is now given once.
