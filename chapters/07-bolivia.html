Latin Doors · Chapter 07

Bolivia: A Cast, Not a Reading List

The last thing Bolivia's literature unit asks a fourth-year secondary pupil to do is not read anything. It is to put on a play about a period. Inspired by the study of literary currents, the book says, let us stage a short dramatization about one of them — and in the dramatization, present an author and their literary production.

So somebody has to be the author. Four pages earlier the book has printed, for the eighteenth century, five names one to a line, each with a country stamped after it like a passport. Jean de la Fontaine, France, fables. Félix María Samaniego, Spain, fables. Daniel Defoe, Spain, Robinson Crusoe. Jonathan Swift, Ireland, Gulliver's Travels. And Kant, Germany, the Critique of Pure Reason — his name left stranded at the end of Swift's line by the book's own typesetting.

Two of those lines are worth stopping on. Daniel Defoe is Spanish here. And Kant is standing in a line of literary works, holding the Critique of Pure Reason.

Read those five lines next to that staging task, and Bolivia's window stops looking like a shelf of books to be read and starts looking like a company of foreigners available to be played.

The door is an object

Most doors in this corpus are lists: a codifier of what an examiner may ask, a specification of set texts, a ministry program naming units. Bolivia's door is a book. One physical volume, the learning text for the fourth year of secondary education, marked official text 2024 above the Minister of Education's name, and at the foot of the same page, in capitals, a line prohibiting the sale of this document.

It is also everything at once. The same covers carry the endocrine glands and the War of the Pacific. Of it all, world literature is a short stretch: five pages of tables and a few pages of extracts.

Every one of the twenty-nine rows coded from the volume is anthology-only: not one is tied to an exam, not one carries a memorization mandate. The book disclaims itself in its own foreword — its contents an element that energizes learning, one that can always be widened, deepened and contextualized — and points upward to an instrument it does not print, the Plans and Programs established for each educational level. There is a door above this door. A name printed here is present; it is not thereby required.

The names, and how they are labelled

The unit sits under a heading that would be hard to invent: the promotion of gender equity in harmony with Mother Earth in the study of literary currents. Beneath it, six numbered sections cover the currents and print seven ruled tables between them — the fourth section gives Realism and Naturalism a table each. The final row of every table is headed emblematic works and representative authors, and twenty of the twenty-five foreign rows in this corpus are names harvested from those cells. That heading is the instruction for how to read them: emblematic. A name there is not a book assigned; it is offered as evidence that a period existed.

Read the tables in order and they walk in a direction. Neoclassicism is filed as France, eighteenth century; Romanticism as Germany, nineteenth; Costumbrismo as Spain; Realism as France, Spain, England, Mexico; Naturalism as France. All of it arriving from across the Atlantic. Then the compass swings west — Indigenismo, Latin America, twentieth century — and the last section says out loud what the swing means. Modernismo, the book explains, stands out from the rest because it was born for the first time in America and is not an imitation of any other current; on the contrary, its influence reached Europe from the Americas.

It is the one current the book describes as born here and exported. And it is in those last two tables, and in none of the five before them, that a Bolivian name stands as an equal instance of the same thing as a foreign one: Ricardo Jaimes Freyre beside Rubén Darío and José Martí; Alcides Arguedas and Jesús Lara beside Ciro Alegría of Peru. For five currents the country is the audience. For two it is a member of the company.

Two entries are labelled in ways the document never notices. Defoe, tagged Spain. And Andrés Bello, the sole representative author of Costumbrismo — the current about local custom — introduced not by a novel but as Simón Bolívar's teacher, holding a treatise on the law of nations. Whatever a pupil presenting Costumbrismo would have to present, it is closer to a founding than to a fiction.

One more thing about this company, set against the heading over its head. Of the twenty-five foreign rows, twenty-four name a man and one names no author at all. The only woman named anywhere among the twenty-nine rows is Bolivian, and she stands five pages past the last table, outside the currents entirely — Mónica Paulina Gutiérrez Jiménez, credited for a free adaptation of an Ayoreo legend printed under the heading spelling workshop.

Shakespeare comes on loan

If you want to see the mechanism at once, look at what happens when the book stops naming authors and prints one. A Hamlet fragment appears under an instruction that the class read it with expressiveness — one of the best-known texts in the history of world theatre, the book says. The heading above it reads, exactly as printed, "(Adapted fragment) Author: Hamlet William Shakespeare." The source line credits not an edition of the play but a 2019 web article about what dramatic texts are, on a popular psychology site. Two of the questions underneath are: have you heard of William Shakespeare, and do you find the situation sad or funny?

Adapted, mis-headed, and sourced from an article about dramatic form rather than from the play. The world's literature arrives on this page to be recognized and read out, and no edition of the play stands behind it.

The object

A book stamped official and forbidden to be sold — one volume for the whole subject, however it reaches the desk — is not a shelf but a fact, the way a room is a fact. Bolivia's official text for the fourth year sets out the literature of the world as a line of names with countries after them: a Frenchman with fables, an Irishman with a giant's country, a German philosopher holding the Critique of Pure Reason as though it were a novel, an Englishman wearing Spain's flag by a printer's slip. Four pages on, the unit asks for a dramatization presenting an author and their literary production.

The document never says those names are the cast for that play. It prints them, and it prints the task, and the pages invite the comparison.
