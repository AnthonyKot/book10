# China: The Mirror on the Shelf

Somewhere in the compulsory years of a Chinese schooling — the document that would say
exactly when is not in this file — a pupil is set a habit rather than a book. The
curriculum standard requires that students "read more, read good books, read whole
books," and for the long ones it offers a single example sentence. It is worth walking
like a shelf, left to right.

Nine titles. The first four are Chinese: Wu Cheng'en's *Journey to the West*, Shi Nai'an's
*Water Margin*, Lao She's *Camel Xiangzi*, then Luo Guangbin and Yang Yiyan's *Red Crag*.
The last four are foreign-authored: Swift's *Gulliver's Travels*, Charlotte Brontë's *Jane
Eyre*, Gorky's *Childhood*, Ostrovsky's *How the Steel Was Tempered*. Between the halves,
fifth of nine, stands Edgar Snow's *Red Star Over China* — 《红星照耀中国》 — a book this
table codes as translated from English, and whose subject is China: an account of China,
written from outside it, in another language. A window is supposed to face outward. This
one has been hung facing the room.

The corpus under this essay is narrower than the printed line, and the seam should be
visible: of the line's five foreign titles, three carry rows here — Snow, Brontë,
Ostrovsky — while Swift and Gorky do not; the fourth foreign row, Jules Verne, is printed
on a different line of the same appendix. Four translated rows against sixty-five
domestic — a shelf small enough to read closely.

## The door, which has two leaves

The defining document is the Compulsory Education Chinese Language Curriculum Standard,
2022 edition, issued by the Ministry of Education of the People's Republic of China in
April 2022. It governs one subject, 语文 — Chinese language and literature — and this
table holds its lower-secondary rows. It names books in two appendices, with unlike force.

Appendix 1 is the recitation leaf, and the window arrives there before any foreign book
does, inside a subordinate clause of the sentence that establishes the mandate. The
compulsory-education stage, it says, requires students to recite from memory excellent
poetry and prose ancient and modern — Chinese classical, Chinese modern and contemporary,
and foreign; the specific pieces may be recommended by textbook editors and
Chinese-language teachers; and here only 135 pieces of classical poetry and prose are
recommended.

The clause opens a Chinese teenager's memory to the poetry of the world — and then the
list underneath names classical Chinese poetry and prose, and hands the specific pieces to
editors and teachers. Of its Grade 7–9 segment, sixty rows in this table, the number that
are foreign is zero. The foreigners are all on the other leaf.

Appendix 2 is the reading leaf — suggestions concerning reading materials in and out of
class. It opens by requiring the habit, then disclaims its own catalogue in the next
paragraph: the reading materials recommended below are examples only. Neither leaf makes a
named work examinable, so every row in this table is a choice-list row. A row's force in
China is not examinability but a named example inside a mandated practice: the state
requires that reciting happen and that whole books be read, and leaves to editors and
teachers which texts do the work.

The nine whole-book rows this table retains — five domestic, four translated, a set that
is *not* the printed nine-title line — take their grade placement from a second source:
the guided-reading-of-masterworks units of the People's Education Press unified textbook,
whose pages this corpus does not hold. Appendix 2 assigns no grades at all. So this essay
makes no use of the grade column, and the pupil in the opening went ungraded for exactly
that reason. Nor is four foreign works read as a measurement of welcome: an example list
and an examination codifier are different instruments. What the four rows carry is a
filing — and the filing is the document's own.

## The guests, in the document's own categories

Sixty-nine rows: sixty-five domestic, four translated — two from English, one from French,
one from Russian. Every foreign row sits in Appendix 2, under two of its headings.

Under popular-science and science-fiction works stands a single line holding two items: *A
Hundred Thousand Whys*, and Jules Verne's *Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Sea*. Verne
(1828–1905) enters not among the masterworks but on the line shared with popular science.
That heading covers science fiction expressly, so nothing here says the ministry declines
to call the book a novel; what the filing says is what stands next to it. Reading that
placement — this essay's reading, not a stated purpose — a submarine arrives as apparatus:
a book admitted in the company of explanations.

The other two rows stand on the masterworks line already walked. Charlotte Brontë
(1816–1855) supplies *Jane Eyre*, coded to nineteenth-century Victorian class and gender.
Nikolai Ostrovsky (1904–1936) supplies *How the Steel Was Tempered*, coded to the Russian
civil war — the one row on this shelf whose coded subject is a war fought outside China.

Ostrovsky's is the row worth opening, because the feeling it imports announces itself on
the page, in the novel's most famous passage, the one that made it a school text across
the Soviet world: "The most precious thing a person has is life. It is given to him once,
and it must be lived so that there is no agonizing pain over years lived aimlessly." A
steelworks of the will, offered at the age when years first look spendable. That the
feeling on this shelf's one foreign war-book is not grief but resolve is this essay's
reading of the work's own sentence; the standard prints only the title.

## Edgar Snow, the mirror

And the fifth position. Snow (1905–1972) is the only foreign row not coded as fiction: its
genre is essay, its subject era the Red Army and the Long March. Three things about this
row can be read closely, and only three.

First, the heading. The word the appendix uses for that line, 名著, is canon vocabulary —
the established, famous work. The appendix keeps a separate line for poetry-and-prose
works, where Lu Xun's *Dawn Blossoms Plucked at Dusk* stands; Snow's book is on the
masterworks line instead, wedged between the Chinese novels and the foreign ones, and the
table's coding of it as an essay records the mismatch: a book of reporting filed among the
classics.

Second, the title. The Chinese does something the English gloss does not. *Red Star Over
China* is a preposition — a star hanging in place. The Chinese title puts a verb in the
middle: 照耀, to shine upon, to illuminate. The star acts, and the country is the
grammatical object.

Third, an adjacency. The appendix's one stated direction about content — that teachers
recommending *supplementary* reading should favor works reflecting revolutionary culture
and advanced socialist culture — attaches to supplementary recommending in general, not to
the masterworks line, and not to Snow. The document never connects the two, and neither
does this essay; the table can say only that Snow's is the one foreign row whose coded
subject era belongs to the history that instruction names.

So the payoff stays at the level of the page. A pupil who walks the printed line meets, in
the middle of it, a foreign witness filed among the masters, whose Chinese title has the
star illuminating the country itself. The effect of that juxtaposition — a window hung
facing the room — is this essay's reading of a placement and a grammar. Whatever reason
the ministry has, it is not in the corpus.

## What is held, and what is only read

Not one of the sixty-nine rows is a mandated memory. The reconciliation pass required an
explicit mandate to learn a *named* text by heart, and Appendix 1's headnote requires
recitation in general, calls its pieces recommendations, and leaves the specific pieces to
editors and teachers.

But look where the two leaves point. The recommended recitation shelf — Guan Ju, Mulan, Du
Fu's spring, Wen Tianxiang passing Lingding Ocean — is Chinese without exception across
its sixty Grade 7–9 rows; the whole-book shelf is nine rows, five Chinese and four
foreign. On this national list, no foreign work is named for memory, and four are named
for reading. And the clause that opened recitation to foreign excellent poetry and prose
stands open, its filling handed to editors and teachers; whatever they choose, this corpus
never sees. A foreign poem may already be in Chinese children's mouths by the route the
document describes and declines to name.

If your classroom set a list of whole books for the year, run the Chinese test on it:
which was written by an author from outside your country, and which of those was about
your country anyway. This corpus produces the second kind exactly once — and hangs it in
the middle of the state's own example sentence.

What this chapter can't know: this is one document and one subject — the 2022 standard for
Chinese language and literature, lower secondary — so foreign works inside the
English-language subject, provincial supplementary lists and senior secondary lie outside
it, and every row here is a named example rather than something a pupil can be examined
on. Four foreign rows are not China's whole foreign presence: Swift and Gorky stand on the
printed line without rows, the fairy-tale and fable lines name Andersen, the Grimms and
Aesop, and three works were held back for want of a work-level receipt — none of which is
evidence they are absent from Chinese classrooms, only that this corpus cannot carry them.
The submarine-as-apparatus, resolve-not-grief and window-facing-the-room readings are this
essay's own, drawn from placement and grammar, not purposes the ministry states.
