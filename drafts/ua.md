# Ukraine: The Door Is a Subject

A ninth-grader stands up to say a poem by heart. The poem is «Буковині» — "To Bukovina" —
and it was written in Romanian, by Mihai Eminescu. Bukovina is a historical region now
divided between Ukraine and Romania; the pupil is saying the poem in Ukrainian
translation. And the lesson is not Ukrainian Literature. On the timetable this hour reads
**«Зарубіжна література»**: Foreign Literature.

A foreign book can enter a classroom by several routes — a unit inside the national
literature course, a line in an exam specification permitting one work in translation.
Ukraine built a door. Foreign Literature sits beside Ukrainian Language and Ukrainian
Literature with its own hours, its own programs, its own list of poems to be learned by
heart. The program says so in its own voice: the subject «вивчається окремо як самостійний
і самодостатній предмет, що має унікальну неперервну традицію викладання (з 1991 р.) в
умовах Незалежності» — "is studied separately as an independent and self-sufficient
subject, one with a uniquely unbroken teaching tradition (since 1991) under conditions of
Independence."

The window is not a pane in a wall. It is a room.

## The door, and its hinges

Who decides what stands in the room? Not the top of the stack, as far as this corpus can
see. Both foreign-literature programs open by naming the instrument they were built under
— the Державний стандарт базової середньої освіти, the State Standard for basic secondary
education, a Cabinet of Ministers resolution — and neither reproduces its contents. The
Standard itself is not among the saved documents, so what it does and does not name is
outside this table.

Books are named a level down. Each of these documents is a **модельна навчальна програма**,
a model program, carrying the line «Рекомендовано Міністерством освіти і науки України» on
its title page above a ministry order number. Recommended, in its own word — and there is
more than one for the same subject and the same grades.

Inside a program, the shelf then splits. Nikolenko's divides its own contents in two:
«Обов’язковий компонент – це художні твори класики й сучасності, які передбачені для
обов’язкового вивчення. Варіативний компонент містить перелік художніх текстів, із яких
учитель може вибрати один або декілька…» — a mandatory component of works set for
compulsory study, and a variable component from which the teacher may pick one or several.
And it grants a further step outward: «учителі можуть використати цю модельну навчальну
програму як основу для створення власних навчальних програм…» — teachers may use this model
program as the basis for programs of their own.

So the state mandates that world literature happen and hands part of the *which* down the
stack: to a program, and inside the program, to a teacher. Only part. The obligatory works
stay put; headings like «ЛЮДИНА У ВИМІРІ НОВОЇ ДРАМИ (1 твір за вибором учителя)» — one
work, at the teacher's choice — mark where the handing-down happens, and they do not stand
over everything.

This table is a hybrid, and the seam matters. Thirty-four of its translated rows are the
Grade 9 shelf of the Nikolenko program. The other five are not Voloshchuk's Grade 9 shelf:
that program also teaches Tristan and Isolde, Romeo and Juliet, Hugo, Mickiewicz, none of
which are here. The five are exactly Voloshchuk's Grade 9 memorization list, taken in
because the other program has no memorization list at all and the comparison is worth
having. Thirty-nine translated rows, then, beside eighteen domestic rows from the Ukrainian
Literature programs next door — a selection, not an inventory. No pupil meets all
thirty-nine, and no program contains this particular thirty-nine either. A school works
from one program; a teacher chooses inside it.

## What comes through

Ten source languages come through. English supplies nineteen of the thirty-nine rows,
French six, German four; the rest arrive in ones and twos — Italian, Polish, Russian,
Yiddish, Romanian, Norwegian, Japanese. Everything is read in Ukrainian, and the program
makes the translation part of the lesson: the works come «в найкращих українських перекладах
із урахуванням здобутків української перекладацької школи» — "in the best Ukrainian
translations, taking account of the achievements of the Ukrainian school of translation." A
foreign book enters this room already holding a Ukrainian hand.

The latest birth year on any row in this table belongs to an English-language row, and
English holds four of the five latest — Foer, Zusak, Whitehead, Rowling. Most of those rows
sit in an additional-reading appendix rather than a taught unit. Nineteen-of-thirty-nine is
a fact about what exists in Ukrainian, not about what fills the hour.

The shelf sorts into three uneven piles.

**Europe as a destination.** The Grade 9 Enlightenment unit — «ДОБА ПРОСВІТНИЦТВА: РОЗУМ,
ПРИРОДА, ОСВІТА» — carries Swift's first voyage, Goethe's lyrics, and Schiller's «До
радості». The teaching line under Schiller does not stop at the Enlightenment. It reads:
«Просвітницька ідея об’єднання людства… Пафос твору, який став гімном Євросоюзу» — "The
Enlightenment idea of the unification of humanity… The pathos of a work that became the
anthem of the European Union." Whether the anthem is Schiller's words or Beethoven's
setting is not this essay's to settle, and the European Union's own anthem is instrumental.
What the table records is the program's sentence, which gives the ode two lives in one
breath: an idea out of its own century, and a work the program says became an anthem.

**Other people's wounds.** Line up the named injuries on each side and they do not mirror.
On the translated rows: Jim Crow (Lee), American slavery (Whitehead), the Congo Free State
(Conrad), the First World War (Morpurgo), wartime Germany (Zusak), 9/11 (Foer). On the
domestic rows next door: totalitarianism (Bahrianyi, Stus), the 1944 deportation of the
Crimean Tatars (Umerov), and a unit of poetry from the war that began in 2014, «Поезія
Вільних». Among those specifically named categories, the current war appears only on the
domestic side.

That is a comparison of named categories inside a selective table, not a partition of the
world. Subjects do cross the seam: the translated shelf also carries totalitarianism
(Bradbury) and the late Russian Empire the domestic shelf also records (Gogol, Sholem
Aleichem, Bulgakov), and Byron's «Мазепа» puts Cossack-era Ukraine on a translated row.
Whether either placement was designed, this table cannot say; rows record admission, never
intention. And the wound labels are a compiler's reading of what each work is about, not a
ministry category — see Limits.

**The Ukrainian pages.** Every unit in Nikolenko carries a standing rubric, «Україна і світ»
— "Ukraine and the World." Voloshchuk runs the equivalent as «український мотив», the
Ukrainian motif. It covers «зв’язки зарубіжних письменників з Україною, майстерність
українських перекладачів, твори іншомовних авторів, біографічно пов’язаних з українськими
територіями» — "the ties of foreign writers to Ukraine, the mastery of Ukrainian
translators, works by foreign-language authors biographically connected with Ukrainian
territories." Its reason, from the same note:

> «Слід наголосити, що такі автори поєднують Україну зі світом, несуть свої уявлення про
> неї в інші культури.» — "It should be emphasized that such authors join Ukraine to the
> world, carrying their conceptions of it into other cultures."

Read the shelf through that rubric and a second shelf appears inside the first. Byron
arrives with «Мазепа», tagged in Voloshchuk beside «Битва під Полтавою та постать Мазепи в
українській культурі». Conrad's row carries the table's own annotation: born in Berdychiv.
Sholem Aleichem's «Тев’є-молочар», the corpus's single Yiddish row, sits under a
Ukraine-and-the-World line that opens «Шолом-Алейхем і Україна».

Two rows are Russian-language. Gogol's offers «Ревізор» АБО «Шинель» — one or the other —
and the program frames the author by period, «Петербурзький етап життя і творчості
письменника», the Petersburg stage of his life and work; the rubric line over the unit reads
«М. Гоголь і українська культура». Bulgakov's row is «Собаче серце», and his entry opens
«Українські сторінки життя і творчості М. О. Булгакова» — "Ukrainian pages of the life and
work of M. O. Bulgakov." The corpus notes call both retained Russian-language works, keyed
to Ukrainian origin in Gogol's case and to Kyivan birth in Bulgakov's; those notes are the
compiler's gloss, not a ministry statement of reasons, and they stay a gloss here. What the
documents do is placement: two Russian-language authors taught inside a subject about the
world, under a rubric about Ukraine.

One thing sits outside this table, fenced. The corpus has no row for it, but both
Ukrainian-literature model programs in `resources/` set Gogol's «Сорочинський ярмарок» in
Grade 9. So one author walks through both doors of the same school — a national writer down
one corridor, a foreign one down the other.

## The poem in the body

Back to the ninth-grader on their feet.

Five rows in this table carry a memorization mandate, and every one of them is translated:
Heine, Dante, Petrarch, Shakespeare, Eminescu. The domestic rows carry none. The
reconciliation pass stripped seven earlier codings on finding that the defining
Ukrainian-literature program makes poetry memorization recommendatory, and lets the learner
choose the poem. Whatever a Ukrainian teenager is *required* to hold in their body, in this
corpus, came from somewhere else.

Four of the five are lyric, and four of the five are about a person: a Dante sonnet to
Beatrice, a Petrarch sonnet (61 or 132, learner's choice), a Shakespeare sonnet (116 or
130), one Heine lyric of three. The fifth is «Буковині», and it is the only one whose
subject is a place.

The program does not print the poem and walk away. Under the title it sets «Стислі
відомості про автора. М. Емінеску та Буковина» — brief information about the author;
Eminescu and Bukovina — and then what the hour is for: «Образ Буковини та її природи», the
image of Bukovina and its nature, and «Романтичні настрої та мотиви у творі», the romantic
moods and motifs in the work, and the inner world of the lyric speaker. Beside it the
Ukrainian-motif rubric sends the class out of the poem and into the geography: «М. Емінеску
в Чернівцях; його подорожі Україною. Пам’ятник Емінеску в Чернівцях» — Eminescu in
Chernivtsi, his travels through Ukraine, the monument to him there.

So the text a ninth-grader must hold by heart, in Ukrainian translation, inside a subject
about the world, is a Romanian poet's address to a region that lies partly inside their own
country's border — and what the teaching line asks them to find in it is an image of
Bukovina and its nature, while the rubric beside it asks them to find a statue in
Chernivtsi.

The reading is mine and I will mark it as mine: what this placement imports is a Ukrainian
place, described in a language that is not Ukrainian. Voloshchuk states its own version, and
states it more narrowly than I have — of foreign-language authors connected with Ukraine,
that «такі автори поєднують Україну зі світом», such authors join Ukraine to the world. That
sentence is the program's. The evidence stops there.

One unit of the other program names something adjacent. Over Nikolenko's Grade 9
history-and-modernity unit — «ІСТОРІЯ І СУЧАСНІСТЬ У ЛІТЕРАТУРІ (2 твори за вибором
учителя)», two works at the teacher's choice — stands a culture rubric: «Культура як
духовний опір насильству», "culture as spiritual resistance to violence." Printed beneath
it is the slot where the teacher picks those two from five: Sholem Aleichem, Bulgakov,
Bradbury, Zusak, Foer. That heading belongs to that unit and those five books. It is not the
program's statement of purpose for the whole shelf, and nothing in the documents makes it
one.

## Fifteen

You were fifteen in some room with a list — a photocopy with a column of due dates, or the
back page of a textbook you never finished. Someone chose it, and the choosing was invisible
to you. If your classroom made you learn a poem by heart, run the Ukrainian test on it: was
the poem yours?

In this table the five texts a ninth-grader must hold by memory are an Italian's sonnet to
Beatrice, another Italian's sonnet to Laura, an Englishman's sonnet, a German's lyric — and
a Romanian's poem to Bukovina, learned in Ukrainian, inside a subject that its own program
describes as having «унікальну неперервну традицію викладання (з 1991 р.)», a uniquely
unbroken teaching tradition since 1991. That is a curriculum author's claim about the
subject, not a finding of this table. Voloshchuk's Grade 9 header gives the clock:
«Рекомендована кількість годин: 1,5 год на тиждень» — an hour and a half a week,
recommended. Small, weekly, and a room of its own.

## Limits

- **No grading, and no cross-country comparison.** Door types are not commensurable, and
  no claim is made here about how other countries admit foreign books, or about where
  Ukraine sits among them. A whole separate subject is an administrative arrangement, not a
  score. Whether this shelf converges on a world canon is a question for the admission
  matrix, not for one country's table.
- **One slice, and a selective one.** The foreign rows come from two *alternative* model
  programs, and the table takes different things from each: Nikolenko's Grade 9 shelf entire
  (34 rows), Voloshchuk's Grade 9 memorization list only (5 rows). Voloshchuk teaches Grade 9
  works that are not in this table (see Receipts). So the 39 are neither a national list nor
  any single program's shelf, and no ninth-grader faces all of them. Which program a school
  works from also decides whether any poem must be learned by heart at all: Nikolenko
  contains no memorization list anywhere in its text, so the five `yes` rows are Voloshchuk's.
- **`subject_era` is an interpretation.** The wound labels are the compiler's reading of
  what each work represents, recoded during reconciliation (22 of them); they are not
  ministry categories and appear nowhere in the programs. Comparisons of "wounds" above are
  comparisons of those labels.
- **`choice-list` is three different things** (see Receipts): an either/or inside a
  mandatory slot, a teacher's pick-two-of-five, and Appendix 2's additional-reading list.
  Unequal force; the 13/26 split is not a ratio of anything.
- **`mandatory` overstates some rows.** The table codes Ibsen, Shaw and Maeterlinck
  `mandatory`, but the unit heading above them reads «1 твір за вибором учителя». Treat
  `exam_status` as the compiler's coding, never as examinability: Grade 9 here has no
  national exam list of the kind that defines the RU or UK tables.
- **Program force is recommendation.** Both foreign-literature documents are model programs
  carrying a ministry recommendation, and Nikolenko says teachers may use it as the basis for
  programs of their own. How a school selects among model programs, and with what
  obligation, is not established by the documents in `resources/`; nothing above should be
  read as describing that procedure.
- **Motive is not in the corpus.** The `note` fields for Gogol and Bulgakov state reasons
  for retention; those are compiler's annotations, and no ministry statement of rationale
  was located in `resources/`. The programs show placement and framing only.
- **What "2024" names.** The slice's `doc_year` values run 2022 (34 rows), 2023 (22) and
  2024 (1); it is named for the latest, and it describes the set of programs available for
  Grade 9 as retrieved on 2026-08-10, not a document issued in 2024. Two dates do not
  reconcile with their documents. Nikolenko's `doc_year` is 2022 while its title page records
  the ministry recommendation on 12 July 2021; and the single 2024-labelled row comes from a
  ЗНО program whose own approving order predates 2024. Both fields are the compiler's and are
  not supported by the documents' title pages; the essay uses the title-page dates.
- **The removals question stays open.** Both foreign-literature programs are dated 2021 or
  later on their title pages (Nikolenko, 12 July 2021; Voloshchuk, 16 August 2023) and the
  corpus holds no earlier Ukrainian list. What stood in these units before them — and
  whether anything was removed after February 2022 — cannot be answered here, and nothing
  above should be read as answering it.
- **Memorization codings are conservative.** The reconciliation rule required an explicit
  mandate to learn a named text by heart; recommendatory memorization, closed-book
  assessment and recitation culture were coded `no`.
