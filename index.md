# The Poem in Your Body

There is a poem in you that starts itself. You have not thought about it in years, and
you could not stop it if you tried: someone says the first three words and the next line
arrives on its own, in the voice of whoever made you learn it, in the classroom where
you were fifteen. You did not choose that poem. Somebody chose it for you, and this book
began with the discovery that the choosing was signed.

For a year I read the documents that do the choosing — the exam codifiers, the ministry
programs, the state anthologies of twenty school systems — looking for the world in
them: which foreign books each country lets into its classrooms, and why. The honest
answer to the second question is that no document ever says why. But somewhere in the
middle of the reading, a smaller column in my tables started to glow. Most of what a
school assigns, a teenager reads once and sets down. A few texts are different: the
state orders them carried — учить наизусть, by heart, in the body. And when I filtered
twenty systems for that one instruction, almost everything fell away. Seventeen
countries, on paper, mandate no named text for memory at all. Three do. They are
Russia, Kazakhstan, and Ukraine — three school systems that share a border, a history,
and, until recently, a shelf — and they use the same instruction to say three opposite
things.

Kazakhstan's program orders twelve works held by heart at fifteen. I expected Abai —
the national poet whose face is on the money, whose schools carry his name. He is not
there. Not one Kazakh line is mandated for memory. All twelve texts are foreign: eleven
Russian — Pushkin, Gogol, Dostoevsky, Tolstoy, Chekhov, an overcoat, a set of poor
folk, a garnet bracelet — and, standing alone at the end of the list like a tourist who
boarded the wrong train, Shakespeare's sonnets. A Kazakh child's own literature is
read, discussed, written about. What the state sets into the body is the literature of
the empire it used to belong to. The program gives no reason. Programs never do.

Ukraine mandates five texts by heart at the same age, and not one of them is Ukrainian
either — but the resemblance ends there, because none of them is Russian. A sonnet by
Dante. A sonnet by Petrarch. A sonnet by Shakespeare. A lyric by Heine. And a poem
called "To Bukovina" by Mihai Eminescu — a Romanian, writing about a green hilly region
that lies today partly inside Ukraine. Ukrainian literature itself is memorized too,
but by recommendation, with the pupil choosing the poem; the law reserves its force for
the world. This is the country that runs an entire school subject called Foreign
Literature, and its memory mandate matches: what must live in a Ukrainian body, by
statute, is Europe.

Russia is the mirror of both. Its federal program orders a pupil finishing grade nine
to recite at least twelve poetic works newly learned that year, and attaches its
by-heart instruction to named units: the medieval Lay of Igor's Campaign, a courtier's
ode from 1747, Griboyedov's Woe from Wit, Gogol's Dead Souls. Every one on the domestic
shelf. The same program marches its children up a staircase of
required foreign classics — Homer at twelve, Cervantes at thirteen, Hamlet at fifteen —
so it is not that Russia hides the world. The world is for reading, in fragments, on
the way up. The memory is for what is Russia's own. Three neighbouring states, one
Soviet inheritance, one identical pedagogic instrument — and it points outward in
Ukraine, backward in Kazakhstan, and inward in Russia.

Then there is Belarus, and Belarus is the reason I trust this column more than any
other in my tables. Its program mandates six texts by heart, and it is the only system
in all twenty that splits the instruction between two literatures: Yanka Kupala and
Yakub Kolas — the two poets every Belarusian school is practically named after — on one
side, and Pushkin and Griboyedov on the other. Two canons set into one adolescent body,
in two languages, in the same school year. Belarus runs Russian literature as a
separate subject with its own hours; a Belarusian classroom is the one place in this
corpus where a child is required to memorize both itself and its neighbour. Whether
that is a window or a mirror — or one of each, facing each other — is a question this
book will spend a whole chapter failing to settle politely.

And the other seventeen? They are not silent because their children memorize nothing.
England makes its fifteen-year-olds sit closed-book poetry exams, where quotation can
only come from memory, while its documents never once print the instruction memorize —
whatever memorizing happens hides in the exam's design, deniable. China writes the most open clause in
the corpus: recitation is required, foreign poetry is explicitly welcome in it, and the
choice of texts is handed down to textbook editors and teachers — the state mandates
the practice and declines to sign the poem. America mandates neither the practice nor
the poem. The three countries that sign their names are the exception, and that is
exactly why they matter: a memorization mandate is the one place in a school system
where the state stops recommending and writes directly onto a person.

So this book starts where you are already an expert. Before it walks you through the
twenty windows — Ukraine's room-sized one, Russia's staircase, China's mirror,
Bolivia's cast list, the empty ones that whole rich countries decline to fill — it
asks you to run the experiment that no ministry can audit. Find the poem that starts
itself in you. Say the first line. Notice the language it arrives in — because for
some of you it will not be the language of your childhood street, and by the end of
this book you will know precisely which office made that decision, in which document,
and what it says about what your country wanted you to be.

*What this chapter can't know: the mandates counted here are the ones written into
defining documents as of one school year — classrooms memorize far more than laws do,
and no table can see a teacher's Tuesday. The three-plus-one pattern is real, receipted,
and reproducible from this book's corpus; the reading of it is mine.*

# Ukraine: The Door Is a Subject

A ninth-grader stands up to say a poem by heart. The poem is «Буковині» — "To Bukovina" —
and it was written in Romanian, by Mihai Eminescu. Bukovina is a historical region now
divided between Ukraine and Romania; the pupil is saying the poem in Ukrainian
translation. And the lesson is not Ukrainian Literature. On the timetable this hour reads
Foreign Literature.

A foreign book can enter a classroom by several routes: a unit inside the national
literature course, a line in an exam specification. Ukraine built a door. Foreign Literature sits beside Ukrainian Language and Ukrainian
Literature with its own hours, its own programs, its own list of poems to be learned by
heart. The program says so in its own voice: the subject is studied separately, as an
independent and self-sufficient one, with a uniquely unbroken teaching tradition since 1991,
under conditions of Independence.

The window is not a pane in a wall. It is a room.

## The door, and its hinges

Who decides what stands in the room? Not the top of the stack, as far as this corpus can
see: both foreign-literature programs open by naming the instrument they were built under —
the State Standard for basic secondary education, a Cabinet of Ministers resolution — and
neither reproduces its contents. The Standard is not among the saved documents, so what it
names is outside this table.

Books are named a level down. Each of these documents is a model program, carrying on its
title page a line recommending it, from the Ministry of Education and Science of Ukraine,
above an order number. Recommended, in its own word — and there is more than one for the
same subject and the same grades.

Inside a program, the shelf then splits. The Nikolenko program divides its own contents in
two: a mandatory component of works set for compulsory study, and a variable component from
which the teacher may pick one or several. And it grants a further
step outward — teachers may use this model program as the basis for programs of their own.

So the state mandates that world literature happen and hands part of the *which* down the
stack: to a program, and inside the program, to a teacher. Only part. The obligatory works
stay put; unit headings that read one work at the teacher's choice mark where the
handing-down happens, and they do not stand over everything.

This table is a hybrid, and the seam matters. Thirty-four of its translated rows are the
Grade 9 shelf of the Nikolenko program. The other five are exactly the Voloshchuk program's
Grade 9 memorization list — taken in because the other program has no memorization list at
all — and not its Grade 9 shelf, which also teaches Tristan and Isolde, Romeo and Juliet,
Hugo and Mickiewicz. Thirty-nine translated rows, then, beside eighteen domestic rows from
the Ukrainian Literature programs next door: a selection, not an inventory. No pupil meets
all thirty-nine, and no program contains this particular thirty-nine.

## What comes through

Ten source languages come through. English supplies nineteen of the thirty-nine rows, French
six, German four; the rest arrive in ones and twos — Italian, Polish, Russian, Yiddish,
Romanian, Norwegian, Japanese. Everything is read in Ukrainian, and the program makes the
translation part of the lesson: the works are to come in the best Ukrainian translations,
taking account of the achievements of the Ukrainian school of translation. A foreign book
enters this room already holding a Ukrainian hand.

English holds four of the five latest-born authors — Foer, Zusak, Whitehead, Rowling — and
most of those rows sit in an additional-reading appendix rather than a taught unit.
Nineteen-of-thirty-nine is a fact about what exists in Ukrainian, not about what fills the
hour.

The shelf sorts into three uneven piles.

**Europe as a destination.** The Grade 9 Enlightenment unit — reason, nature, education —
carries Swift's first voyage, Goethe's lyrics, and Schiller's ode to joy. The teaching line
under Schiller does not stop at the Enlightenment. It names the Enlightenment idea of the
unification of humanity, and then the pathos of a work that became the anthem of the
European Union. Whether the anthem is Schiller's words or Beethoven's instrumental setting is
not this essay's to settle. What the table records is the program's sentence, which gives the
ode two lives in one breath: an idea out of its own century, and an anthem.

**Other people's wounds.** Line up the named injuries on each side and they do not mirror.
On the translated rows: Jim Crow in Lee, American slavery in Whitehead, the Congo Free State
in Conrad, the First World War in Morpurgo, wartime Germany in Zusak, 9/11 in Foer. On the
domestic rows next door: totalitarianism in Bahrianyi and Stus, the 1944 deportation of the
Crimean Tatars in Umerov, and a unit of poetry from the war that began in 2014. Among those
named categories, the current war appears only on the domestic side.

That is a comparison of labels inside a selective table, not a partition of the world — and
the labels are this essay's reading of what each work is about, not a ministry category.
Subjects do cross the seam. The translated shelf also carries totalitarianism, in
Bradbury; the late Russian Empire appears on both sides; and Byron's «Мазепа» puts
Cossack-era Ukraine on a translated row. Rows record admission, never intention.

**The Ukrainian pages.** Every unit in the Nikolenko program carries a standing rubric,
Ukraine and the World; the Voloshchuk program runs the equivalent as the Ukrainian motif,
covering the ties of foreign writers to Ukraine, the mastery of Ukrainian translators, and
authors biographically connected with Ukrainian territories. Its reason, in the
program's own words: such authors "join Ukraine to the world," carrying their conceptions of
it into other cultures.

Read the shelf through that rubric and a second shelf appears inside the first. Byron
arrives with «Мазепа», tagged beside the battle of Poltava and the figure of Mazepa in
Ukrainian culture. Conrad's row notes that he was born in Berdychiv. Sholem Aleichem's Tevye
the Dairyman, the corpus's single Yiddish row, sits under a Ukraine-and-the-World line that
opens on Sholem Aleichem and Ukraine.

Two rows are Russian-language. Gogol's offers The Government Inspector or The Overcoat, and
the program frames the author by the Petersburg stage of his life and work, with the rubric
over the unit reading Gogol and Ukrainian culture. Bulgakov's row is
Heart of a Dog, and his entry opens on the Ukrainian pages of his life and work. Corpus notes
call both retained works — Ukrainian origin in Gogol's case, Kyivan birth in Bulgakov's — but
that is a gloss, not a ministry statement of reasons. What the documents do is placement: two
Russian-language authors taught inside a subject about the world, under a rubric about
Ukraine.

One thing sits outside this table. There is no row for it, but both Ukrainian-literature
model programs set Gogol's Sorochyntsi Fair in Grade 9 — so one author walks through both
doors of the same school, a national writer down one corridor, a foreign one down the other.

## The poem in the body

Back to the ninth-grader on their feet.

Five rows carry a memorization mandate, and every one of them is translated: Heine, Dante,
Petrarch, Shakespeare, Eminescu. The domestic rows carry none — the defining
Ukrainian-literature program makes memorization recommendatory and lets the learner choose
the poem. Whatever a Ukrainian teenager is *required* to hold in their body, in this corpus,
came from somewhere else.

Four of the five are lyric, and four of the five are about a person: a Dante sonnet to
Beatrice, a Petrarch sonnet (61 or 132, learner's choice), a Shakespeare sonnet (116 or
130), one Heine lyric of three. The fifth is «Буковині», and it is the only one whose
subject is a place.

The program does not print the poem and walk away. Under the title it sets brief information
about Eminescu and Bukovina, and then what the hour is for: the image of Bukovina and its
nature, the romantic moods and motifs, the inner world of the lyric speaker. Beside it the
Ukrainian-motif rubric sends the class out of the poem and into the geography — Eminescu in
Chernivtsi, his travels through Ukraine, the monument to him there.

So the text a ninth-grader must hold by heart, in Ukrainian translation, inside a subject
about the world, is a Romanian poet's address to a region that lies partly inside their own
country's border — and what the teaching line asks them to find in it is an image of
Bukovina and its nature, while the rubric beside it asks them to find a statue in
Chernivtsi.

The reading is mine and I will mark it as mine: what this placement imports is a Ukrainian
place, described in a language that is not Ukrainian. The program states its own version,
and states it more narrowly than I have — that such authors join Ukraine to the world. The
evidence stops there.

The other program names something adjacent. Over the Grade 9 history-and-modernity unit,
where the teacher picks two works from five — Sholem Aleichem, Bulgakov, Bradbury, Zusak,
Foer — stands a culture rubric: culture as spiritual resistance to violence. That heading
belongs to those five books, not to the whole shelf, and nothing in the documents makes it
more.

## Fifteen

You were fifteen in some room with a list — a photocopy with a column of due dates, or the
back page of a textbook you never finished. Someone chose it, and the choosing was invisible
to you. If your classroom made you learn a poem by heart, run the Ukrainian test on it: was
the poem yours?

In this table the five texts a ninth-grader must hold by memory are an Italian's sonnet to
Beatrice, another Italian's sonnet to Laura, an Englishman's sonnet, a German's lyric — and
a Romanian's poem to Bukovina, learned in Ukrainian, inside a subject that its own program
describes as having a uniquely unbroken teaching tradition since 1991. That is the
curriculum author's claim, not a finding of this table. And the clock beside it is an hour
and a half a week, recommended. Small, weekly, and a room of its own.

These thirty-nine come from two *alternative* model programs — one's Grade 9 shelf entire,
the other's memorization list only — so they are neither a national list nor any single
program's shelf, and which program a school works from decides whether any poem must be
learned by heart at all. Both programs carry a
ministry recommendation rather than a mandate, one says outright that teachers may build
their own program on it, and Grade 9 here has no national exam list. What stood in these
units before 2021, and whether anything was removed after February 2022, this corpus cannot
say.

# Russia: The Staircase

A Russian child enters fifth grade at eleven, and somewhere in Moscow a document already
knows, year by year, which foreigners they are going to meet on the way up.

At eleven, the world arrives as story: the myths of the world's peoples, required;
Andersen's Snow Queen or another of his tales; a choice of Carroll or Tolkien; two
works from a childhood shelf holding Twain, London and Bradbury; two adventures with
Stevenson as the printed example. At twelve it gets serious: Homer, in fragments,
required; then Defoe's island, required; then Swift's giants and academies, required.
At thirteen, Cervantes — the knight himself, chapters of him — and the Little Prince,
whole. At fourteen, Shakespeare twice: a sonnet or two of the pupil's choosing, and
Romeo and Juliet, required, with Molière's bourgeois gentleman for comedy. And at
fifteen, the year this book measures every country at, the staircase reaches its
landing: Dante at the gates of the afterlife, Hamlet on the battlements, Faust in his
study, Byron's Childe Harold on the road — all four required — with two further slots
where a pupil picks a Byron lyric and a teacher picks Hoffmann, Hugo or Scott.

That is a real window, and it is not the one Russia is usually judged by. Judge it by
the exam and you would conclude the window barely exists: the grade-nine examination
codifier compresses all foreign literature into a single choice line, a parenthesis
holding Homer, Cervantes, Shakespeare and Molière as examples. For a while that
parenthesis was this book's Russian finding. It was the wrong document. The exam is the
door out of school; the window is on the staircase, and the staircase is written down
in the federal working program for literature — directly binding, since September 2023,
on every organization teaching a state-accredited basic general education program. The
program is not advice. It is the floor plan.

So Russia turns out to run a large mandatory foreign shelf — twelve required foreign
rows across the five grades, before any teacher makes any choice — and a narrow one.
Walk the fixed, named core of the ladder and look at who is on it. Homer is ancient
Greece; Dante is the Middle Ages; Cervantes, Shakespeare and Molière the sixteenth and
seventeenth centuries; Defoe and Swift the eighteenth; Goethe and Byron the early
nineteenth. The newest author on that named mandatory core is Saint-Exupéry, and he is
there as a fable. The choice shelves widen the picture — Twain, London, Bradbury,
Harper Lee, O. Henry, Cooper wait in the optional slots, so the named shelf as a whole
is Europe plus North America — but no named author from Asia, Africa or Latin America
appears anywhere in the twenty-six foreign rows; those worlds enter only anonymously,
in grade five's myths and tales of the world's peoples. The required window faces in
one direction: the European classics, its named core ending roughly where the
twentieth century begins. Russia's program does not import the world. It imports the
canon of Europe, as a stated inheritance.

And it imports it in pieces. Look again at how the required foreigners arrive: Homer in
fragments; Defoe and Swift in selected chapters; Cervantes in chapters; Shakespeare's
tragedy in fragments of the pupil's or teacher's choosing; Dante in at least two
fragments; Faust in at least two; Childe Harold in at least one. Ten of the eleven
named required foreign works carry that stamp — fragments, chapters, by choice. Now
look at the domestic shelf beside them in grade nine: Griboyedov's Woe from Wit, whole.
Pushkin's Eugene Onegin, whole. Lermontov's A Hero of Our Time, whole. Gogol's Dead
Souls, whole. The program's own arithmetic of attention is visible on the page: the
world is met in excerpts, the homeland in full texts. A fifteen-year-old finishes the
year having stood briefly at four of European literature's summits and having lived
inside four Russian books.

The one exception to the fragment stamp is worth a close look, because it is the
program's most tender act of admission. The single foreign work a Russian child is
required to read whole, cover to cover, is The Little Prince — at thirteen, filed by
this table under loneliness and friendship, a fable about a small person responsible
for one rose on one planet. Everything else from abroad arrives as a monument to be
visited in fragments; the one whole foreign book is the one about caring for something
because you have tamed it. That the ladder's builders required tenderness entire and
Dante in excerpts is, on this essay's reading, the most human sentence the document
never quite says.

Then there is the question of what the body keeps. The program orders memorization the
way a codifier never could: a pupil finishing grade nine must be able to recite at
least twelve poetic works not learned in earlier years, and for certain named works the
by-heart instruction is attached directly to the unit — the medieval Lay of Igor's
Campaign (domestic-coded in this table, as the program places it), Lomonosov's ode on
the empress's accession, Woe from Wit, Dead Souls. Every work carrying that instruction
is on the domestic shelf. The four required foreigners of grade nine — Dante,
Shakespeare, Goethe, Byron — carry no memorization instruction at all. In Kazakhstan,
this book found a program whose every by-heart mandate pointed at foreign, mostly
Russian, texts; in Ukraine, at foreign texts inside a subject built for them. Russia is
the mirror: the world is for reading; the memory is for what is Russia's own. On this
essay's reading — and it is a reading, the program states no such doctrine — the
staircase teaches a European inheritance to the mind and a national one to the body.

At the top of the staircase stands the exam, and the two documents part company. Five
years of differentiated requirement — this Dante, that Hamlet, those chapters of
Cervantes — collapse, in the grade-nine examination codifier, into one undifferentiated
choice line where Homer, Cervantes, Shakespeare and Molière appear as examples. The
exam does not exclude the world; it stops guaranteeing any particular piece of it. The
program proves what is taught; the codifier proves what is promised at the door out of
school; and the gap between them is the finding — this book can show the gap and cannot
explain it, because neither document explains itself.

If your schooling ran on this staircase, or on one of its Soviet-built cousins, you may
recognize the sensation from inside: the foreign classics as a sequence of guided
excursions — brief, chaperoned, in fragments — and the domestic classics as the house
you actually lived in, whose rooms you can still walk with your eyes closed, because
whole monologues of it were set into you at fifteen. The window on the staircase was
real. But the door of the house locks from the inside, and the key was learned by
heart.

One honesty paragraph to close. This is the federal program as printed for 2024, a
one-year slice: it proves what the state requires to be taught, not what any classroom
did with it, and the fragments-by-choice stamps mean two pupils on the same staircase
may have met quite different pieces of the same Dante. The exam contrast uses the
codifier only for what it is — the narrower, older instrument this chapter's first
version mistook for the whole window. And the reading of memory against inheritance is
this essay's, offered for inspection; the program mandates lists, hours and recitations,
and gives reasons for none of them.

# China: The Mirror on the Shelf

Somewhere in the compulsory years of a Chinese schooling — the document that would say
exactly when is not in this file — a pupil is set a habit rather than a book. The
curriculum standard requires that students "read more, read good books, read whole
books," and for the long ones it offers a single example sentence. It is worth walking
like a shelf, left to right.

Nine titles. The first four are Chinese: Wu Cheng'en's *Journey to the West*, Shi Nai'an's
*Water Margin*, Lao She's *Camel Xiangzi*, then Luo Guangbin and Yang Yiyan's *Red Crag*.
The last four are foreign-authored: Swift's *Gulliver's Travels*, Charlotte Brontë's *Jane
Eyre*, Gorky's *Childhood*, Ostrovsky's *How the Steel Was Tempered*. Between the halves,
fifth of nine, stands Edgar Snow's *Red Star Over China* — 《红星照耀中国》 — a book this
table codes as translated from English, and whose subject is China: an account of China,
written from outside it, in another language. A window is supposed to face outward. This
one has been hung facing the room.

The corpus under this essay is narrower than the printed line, and the seam should be
visible: of the line's five foreign titles, three carry rows here — Snow, Brontë,
Ostrovsky — while Swift and Gorky do not; the fourth foreign row, Jules Verne, is printed
on a different line of the same appendix. Four translated rows against sixty-five
domestic — a shelf small enough to read closely.

## The door, which has two leaves

The defining document is the Compulsory Education Chinese Language Curriculum Standard,
2022 edition, issued by the Ministry of Education of the People's Republic of China in
April 2022. It governs one subject, 语文 — Chinese language and literature — and this
table holds its lower-secondary rows. It names books in two appendices, with unlike force.

Appendix 1 is the recitation leaf, and the window arrives there before any foreign book
does, inside a subordinate clause of the sentence that establishes the mandate. The
compulsory-education stage, it says, requires students to recite from memory excellent
poetry and prose ancient and modern — Chinese classical, Chinese modern and contemporary,
and foreign; the specific pieces may be recommended by textbook editors and
Chinese-language teachers; and here only 135 pieces of classical poetry and prose are
recommended.

The clause opens a Chinese teenager's memory to the poetry of the world — and then the
list underneath names classical Chinese poetry and prose, and hands the specific pieces to
editors and teachers. Of its Grade 7–9 segment, sixty rows in this table, the number that
are foreign is zero. The foreigners are all on the other leaf.

Appendix 2 is the reading leaf — suggestions concerning reading materials in and out of
class. It opens by requiring the habit, then disclaims its own catalogue in the next
paragraph: the reading materials recommended below are examples only. Neither leaf makes a
named work examinable, so every row in this table is a choice-list row. A row's force in
China is not examinability but a named example inside a mandated practice: the state
requires that reciting happen and that whole books be read, and leaves to editors and
teachers which texts do the work.

The nine whole-book rows this table retains — five domestic, four translated, a set that
is *not* the printed nine-title line — take their grade placement from a second source:
the guided-reading-of-masterworks units of the People's Education Press unified textbook,
whose pages this corpus does not hold. Appendix 2 assigns no grades at all. So this essay
makes no use of the grade column, and the pupil in the opening went ungraded for exactly
that reason. Nor is four foreign works read as a measurement of welcome: an example list
and an examination codifier are different instruments. What the four rows carry is a
filing — and the filing is the document's own.

## The guests, in the document's own categories

Sixty-nine rows: sixty-five domestic, four translated — two from English, one from French,
one from Russian. Every foreign row sits in Appendix 2, under two of its headings.

Under popular-science and science-fiction works stands a single line holding two items: *A
Hundred Thousand Whys*, and Jules Verne's *Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Sea*. Verne
(1828–1905) enters not among the masterworks but on the line shared with popular science.
That heading covers science fiction expressly, so nothing here says the ministry declines
to call the book a novel; what the filing says is what stands next to it. Reading that
placement — this essay's reading, not a stated purpose — a submarine arrives as apparatus:
a book admitted in the company of explanations.

The other two rows stand on the masterworks line already walked. Charlotte Brontë
(1816–1855) supplies *Jane Eyre*, coded to nineteenth-century Victorian class and gender.
Nikolai Ostrovsky (1904–1936) supplies *How the Steel Was Tempered*, coded to the Russian
civil war — the one row on this shelf whose coded subject is a war fought outside China.

Ostrovsky's is the row worth opening, because the feeling it imports announces itself on
the page, in the novel's most famous passage, the one that made it a school text across
the Soviet world: "The most precious thing a person has is life. It is given to him once,
and it must be lived so that there is no agonizing pain over years lived aimlessly." A
steelworks of the will, offered at the age when years first look spendable. That the
feeling on this shelf's one foreign war-book is not grief but resolve is this essay's
reading of the work's own sentence; the standard prints only the title.

## Edgar Snow, the mirror

And the fifth position. Snow (1905–1972) is the only foreign row not coded as fiction: its
genre is essay, its subject era the Red Army and the Long March. Three things about this
row can be read closely, and only three.

First, the heading. The word the appendix uses for that line, 名著, is canon vocabulary —
the established, famous work. The appendix keeps a separate line for poetry-and-prose
works, where Lu Xun's *Dawn Blossoms Plucked at Dusk* stands; Snow's book is on the
masterworks line instead, wedged between the Chinese novels and the foreign ones, and the
table's coding of it as an essay records the mismatch: a book of reporting filed among the
classics.

Second, the title. The Chinese does something the English gloss does not. *Red Star Over
China* is a preposition — a star hanging in place. The Chinese title puts a verb in the
middle: 照耀, to shine upon, to illuminate. The star acts, and the country is the
grammatical object.

Third, an adjacency. The appendix's one stated direction about content — that teachers
recommending *supplementary* reading should favor works reflecting revolutionary culture
and advanced socialist culture — attaches to supplementary recommending in general, not to
the masterworks line, and not to Snow. The document never connects the two, and neither
does this essay; the table can say only that Snow's is the one foreign row whose coded
subject era belongs to the history that instruction names.

So the payoff stays at the level of the page. A pupil who walks the printed line meets, in
the middle of it, a foreign witness filed among the masters, whose Chinese title has the
star illuminating the country itself. The effect of that juxtaposition — a window hung
facing the room — is this essay's reading of a placement and a grammar. Whatever reason
the ministry has, it is not in the corpus.

## What is held, and what is only read

Not one of the sixty-nine rows is a mandated memory. The reconciliation pass required an
explicit mandate to learn a *named* text by heart, and Appendix 1's headnote requires
recitation in general, calls its pieces recommendations, and leaves the specific pieces to
editors and teachers.

But look where the two leaves point. The recommended recitation shelf — Guan Ju, Mulan, Du
Fu's spring, Wen Tianxiang passing Lingding Ocean — is Chinese without exception across
its sixty Grade 7–9 rows; the whole-book shelf is nine rows, five Chinese and four
foreign. On this national list, no foreign work is named for memory, and four are named
for reading. And the clause that opened recitation to foreign excellent poetry and prose
stands open, its filling handed to editors and teachers; whatever they choose, this corpus
never sees. A foreign poem may already be in Chinese children's mouths by the route the
document describes and declines to name.

If your classroom set a list of whole books for the year, run the Chinese test on it:
which was written by an author from outside your country, and which of those was about
your country anyway. This corpus produces the second kind exactly once — and hangs it in
the middle of the state's own example sentence.

What this chapter can't know: this is one document and one subject — the 2022 standard for
Chinese language and literature, lower secondary — so foreign works inside the
English-language subject, provincial supplementary lists and senior secondary lie outside
it, and every row here is a named example rather than something a pupil can be examined
on. Four foreign rows are not China's whole foreign presence: Swift and Gorky stand on the
printed line without rows, the fairy-tale and fable lines name Andersen, the Grimms and
Aesop, and three works were held back for want of a work-level receipt — none of which is
evidence they are absent from Chinese classrooms, only that this corpus cannot carry them.
The submarine-as-apparatus, resolve-not-grief and window-facing-the-room readings are this
essay's own, drawn from placement and grammar, not purposes the ministry states.

# Vietnam: A Bookcase, and Eight Names for the World

Before it says anything at all about foreign literature, Vietnam's national program for Ngữ
văn — the single subject that carries the Vietnamese language and its literature — furnishes
the room. The minimum equipment of the subject, it says, is a reference bookcase: stories,
poetry, drama, reportage, argument, picture books. It also wants portraits of the program's
major writers, and pictures illustrating some of the major works — six titles follow as
examples, between a *such as* and an ellipsis, and the program sets no number of pictures and
no place to hang them. At the desks it wants whole books, telling teachers to limit teaching by
extract.

So furnish the room yourself, because the document won't. Put the bookcase where you like and
the pictures wherever you last saw pictures in a school. The six titles under them are not
arbitrary: the same six appear earlier in the program as its compulsory works, the only
literature it obliges both a textbook's author and a teacher to carry out. An anonymous poem
from the Lý dynasty; Trần Quốc Tuấn's address to his officers; Nguyễn Trãi's great
proclamation; *Truyện Kiều*; Nguyễn Đình Chiểu's funeral oration for the fighters of Cần Giuộc;
Hồ Chí Minh's declaration of independence. Four of the six sit against an invasion or an
occupation — Song, Mongol, Ming, French — and a fifth against the end of a world war.

The rest of the world's literature is in this room too. It is not among the six, and on the
day the program was signed it was not yet a book. It was one line.

## The line

Foreign literature: choose at least one work for each of the following literatures — English,
French, American, Greek, Russian, Japanese, Chinese, Indian.

Eight literatures. A floor of one work apiece. No titles, no authors, no centuries — eight
names, and not one book.

The two lines above it are built the other way round. Vietnamese folk literature gets five
categories, each with a minimum attached — folk-tale genres, folk verses, an epic, an
ethnic-minority verse narrative, a script for one of the traditional theatre forms. Vietnamese
written literature gets thirteen names, Nguyễn Trãi and Nguyễn Du and Hồ Xuân Hương among them.
Then, in the same tier and the same grammar, foreign literature gets eight
countries. At home the program guarantees particular writers. Abroad it guarantees only the
literatures a work must come from. Nothing in the program explains the difference, and a list
cannot be made to give a reason for itself.

Nor is the floor a promise of space. The program asks for a relative balance between Vietnamese
and foreign literature, between East and West, and then defines its own word against the
obvious reading of it: "'Balance' is understood as a suitable proportion, not an equal one." A
ministry writing down that a quota is a floor and not a scale.

## Whose job is a name

Take one of the eight names — Nga, Russia — and follow it toward the bookcase. Somebody has to
turn it into a page, and the program is unusually clear about who: it grades its own force in a
single sentence. There are three tiers, and the addressee changes as you go down.
Compulsory works bind textbook authors and teachers alike, but only *as the program provides* —
and the program provides by grade, so what binds a particular teacher is whatever it has placed
in the grades they teach. Compulsory-selection works, the eight names among
them, bind one person: the textbook author, who must choose a work by an author the program has
named. Suggested works bind nobody.

This is the door, and it opens onto a desk rather than onto a classroom. The circular that
promulgates it, signed by the Minister of Education and Training at the end of 2018 and in
force from February 2019, addresses its foreign-literature instruction not to a country's
teachers but to whoever is writing a book for them. The word *Nga* lands on somebody's desk.
The teacher will meet it later, bound into a volume, as a story with a title, and the program
that required it nowhere in sight.

## The back of the book

Suppose that person looks for help. The program's own suggestions are at the back, grouped by
genre and then, by its own rule, arranged alphabetically by the Vietnamese title, with the
author's name in brackets after it. Nothing there says which entry answers which of the eight
names. The two halves of the document are never joined: the quota
names literatures, the menu names books, and no line runs between them.

The menu also rations its authors. Each one gets a single line across twelve years of
schooling, and the only exemption belongs to the authors of the six compulsory works, all of
them Vietnamese. And it declines to be a schedule: it calls itself not all the material for the
grades but only illustrative examples, and the same page lets textbook authors go outside it
for equivalent texts. The program states the thought elsewhere as a principle: it has an open
orientation as to materials.

So in the grade 10–12 fiction list, sorted by first letter of the Vietnamese title, Hemingway
sits between *Odysseus* and *Số đỏ*, and nowhere does the document say that he is the American
one. Across the two grade bands this corpus covers, twenty-one entries name a foreign author or
a foreign origin: six English, five French, four Chinese, two Greek, two Russian, one Japanese,
one Spanish. An entry is not the same thing as a work — La Fontaine's line prints two fables,
Lu Xun's prints three, and *Sherlock Holmes* stands in for a shelf. Not one is matched to a
slot. Eight names in one half of the document, twenty-one entries scattered through the other,
and no line drawn between them by anyone but the reader.

Two edges make the point sharply. *One Hundred Years of Solitude* is printed like everything
else, a Vietnamese title and a bracketed G. Marquez, and it is the one named foreign entry here
written in a language — Spanish — that answers to none of the eight names. India is the one
name of the eight for which the grades covered here offer no named example at all. A suggestion
matching no requirement; a requirement with no suggestion.

## Two teachers

The two Russian-language stories in the menu are printed as flatly as the rest — a dash, a
Vietnamese title, a bracket: *The First Teacher*, C. Aitmatov; *The Man in a Case*, A. Chekhov.
That both were written in Russian is a fact about language and nothing more. The document does
not say that either answers to *Nga*, or that anything else does; and the label will not even
carry a nationality, since Chinghiz Aitmatov was Kyrgyz and wrote in both Kyrgyz and Russian.
In this list a foreign author is an initial, a surname and a bracket — unhoused.

The titles rhyme before anyone opens the books: one names a teacher, the other a man who has
put himself away. Open them and the rhyme holds. Chekhov's Belikov teaches Greek and keeps
every part of himself in a case, and his fear settles over a whole town. Aitmatov's Duishen is
the opposite man and the opposite failure — a demobilised soldier with barely more schooling
than his pupils, who turns a stable into a classroom and teaches from almost nothing, carrying
children across an icy stream on his back.

Now look at what surrounds them. The grade 10–12 Vietnamese fiction here is heavy with the
wars: Anh Đức, Nguyễn Thi, Phan Tứ, Nguyễn Minh Châu, Bảo Ninh, Nguyễn Khải — six entries whose
subject is the anti-US war or its aftermath. That describes what those books are about, not
what the ministry wants them for, and the two Russian stories carry no such tag. So what
follows is this essay's reading and not the document's: beside a
domestic shelf that keeps returning to the war, these two imports are both about a room with a
teacher in it, one of them a warning and one of them a debt. Whether that ever crossed anyone's
mind in Hà Nội, the circular does not say, and a list cannot be made to say it.

## The last instruction is the loosest

Come back to the room. There is one more thing the program asks a pupil to do with a book, and
from grade 6 to grade 12 it asks it in the same sentence, word for word: "Learn by heart some
favourite verse passages or poems from the program."

Seven grades, seven identical lines. No work is named, no number is set, and the sentence does
not say who decides that a poem is a favourite — the pupil, the textbook, or the teacher. The
primary grades are written the other way: grade 1 asks for four or five short passages already
studied, and the count climbs year by year to at least ten or twelve longer ones by grade 5.
The counting stops when primary school does. What replaces it is the favourite.

So no work here carries a memorization mandate of its own — which means only that no text is
named and no quantity tied to one, not that nothing is learned by heart in Vietnam. Learning by
heart is itself one of the outcomes the program requires.

That is the shape of this window, top to bottom: a named domestic canon; eight unnamed
literatures, addressed to a person with a manuscript; a menu the document says is only
examples; and an instruction to learn a poem by heart with the poem left blank.

The blank is the last thing this document does, and it is the same move it made in the quota
line: require the thing, decline to name it. If you can still recite something from when you
were fifteen, there was a document behind it too, somewhere, with a blank in it — and somebody
filled that blank in. A ministry, an editor, a teacher, or you. The Vietnamese program says
the blank must be filled. It does not say by whom.

What this chapter can't know: this is one multi-grade national program as promulgated in 2018,
not a school year's syllabus and not an examination list, and of the grade bands its menu is
published in, only the upper two are counted here. Because the quota binds a textbook's author
rather than a teacher, what any pupil actually read depends on a book this corpus does not
contain — an available window, then, not a taught one. And where the essay says what a work is
about, that is a reading of the work, never the ministry's statement of what it is for.

# India: The Lesson That Won't Be Asked

Before an Indian fifteen-year-old reads a line of Anton Chekhov, they are asked to fill in a
table. The columns are printed for them: customs similar to Indian ones, customs different
from Indian ones. Above the table sits a short article about Russian weddings — the groom
climbing an apartment block's stairs, answering a riddle at each landing — and then the
question of whether Indian and Russian weddings have any customs in common, to be worked out
with a partner.

Only then does the play begin: Lomov arrives at Chubukov's house in a dress-suit to propose to
Natalya, and the three of them quarrel about a meadow and a dog until the proposal is nearly
lost. The unit ends with a group discussion of the qualities one should look for in a marriage
partner, under headings that include attitude to money and wealth.

The errand is written on the page before the work starts: a Russian farce about two landowning
families, brought into an Indian classroom to be set beside an Indian wedding, in ruled
columns, by a child with a partner.

## The door is a book, and a subtraction

India's window has an unusual hinge. The Central Board of Secondary Education issues a
curriculum for each subject code, and the last page of that curriculum carries a list of
prescribed books and a note naming the lessons from which no questions will be asked. The
books themselves are published by the National Council of Educational Research and Training.
The board that examines names the anthology, and then it subtracts.

The English syllabus prints its chapter list in full, prose and poems, book by book. The Hindi
syllabi do not. Hindi Course A names *Kshitij Part 2* and *Kritika Part 2* and stops — then
names, from *Kritika*, only the lessons it removes, one of them a satire called "George the
Fifth's Nose". That is the hole in this corpus: no defining document held here lists
*Kritika*'s remaining contents, so the rows once coded from it were quarantined. The only
*Kritika* chapters the syllabus names are the ones it takes away.

No defining document here mandates memorisation of any named row — not a title, not an
excerpt, not a stated quantity of texts by heart. What the English paper asks for is an
extract and a short written answer.

## Streams in one corridor

A school's window here is not one window. In the same Class 10 corridor a pupil may sit Hindi
Course A — mother-tongue Hindi, with *Kshitij* and *Kritika* — or Hindi Course B, with
*Sparsh* and *Sanchayan*; English Language and Literature is a separate code with its own two
readers. Same board, different worlds. How many pupils walk which door, and when each stream
sits its paper, these documents do not say.

This corpus speaks for the English stream, partly for Hindi Course A, not at all for Hindi
Course B: forty-three rows, thirty-one from the English syllabus — twenty-two in *First
Flight*, nine in *Footprints Without Feet* — and twelve from *Kshitij Part 2*, every one
Class 10, prescribed, unmarked for memorisation.

## What comes through the English door

Twenty of the thirty-one English-syllabus rows arrive from outside India, already in English.
Nineteen rows across the whole corpus are domestic — the twelve Hindi ones, plus seven Indian
pieces inside the English readers: a Goan baker, Coorg, Assam tea, a Tamil girl's bus ride,
the sermon at Benares, Ruskin Bond's thief, Bholi.

And four rows — four, out of forty-three — come from a language that is neither English nor
Indian: a Mexican story from Spanish, a Dutch diary, a Russian one-act play, a French short
story. López y Fuentes, Anne Frank, Chekhov, Maupassant.

The syllabus names this shape itself, in its objectives, as an aim. The course sets out to
develop sensitivity to and appreciation of world literature — and then, in the same sentence,
scopes that world to "varieties of English" and the cultures embedded in it. Further down, the
objectives turn homeward, to Indian languages, multilingualism, translations and Indian
literature.

The poetry section reads clearest. *First Flight* carries ten poems, every one from abroad in
English — seven by American poets (Frost twice, Wells, Berryman, Rich, Sandburg, Nash), one
each from a Welsh, an Australian and an Irish poet. No Indian poet holds a poetry slot in the
English reader. The Indian poetry is next door, in Hindi, where Surdas and Tulsidas open
*Kshitij* and all twelve rows are domestic.

By subject the table supports one split, and only one: each row either carries a
place-and-period setting or carries none. Thirteen of the twenty-four rows from abroad have no
historical setting at all — a tiger, a fog, a dragon, a snowfall — while fourteen of the
nineteen domestic rows do carry one, from ancient Benares to Bismillah Khan's shehnai in 1947.
Whatever else that is, it is not foreign history set against Indian timelessness. And no row
here is coded to Partition — a fact about one Class 10 year with a quarantined reader in it,
and nothing larger.

## Chekhov, twice

What this corpus can do that a list cannot is watch one writer walk through two doors of the
same school.

Through the English door, Chekhov is a chapter with a page number: *First Flight*'s last unit,
printed on the contents page, the one author in the book who wrote in Russian, his surname
spelled Chekov there. The reader's own blurb tells the class what the farce is about — the
tendency of wealthy families to seek ties with other wealthy families, increasing their estates
by encouraging marriages that make good economic sense. He is examinable: the paper draws
extracts from drama and prose and short answers from *First Flight*.

Then the unit closes, and the last page is addressed to the teacher rather than the child.
What we have done, it says: given you a play by the famous Russian writer, Anton Chekhov. What
you can do: dictate the biographical information below, and let the students guess the name of
the playwright. What follows is a paragraph with a hole where a name should be — born at
Stratford-on-Avon, married to an older woman, retired, dead, leaving behind a body of work
that still stands as a pinnacle in world literature.

The one Russian in the book is handed over, and the class is invited to guess the Englishman.
The exercise assumes recognition: it withholds the name because it expects the name back. That
is what a shared shelf looks like from inside — not a text on the syllabus, but a riddle whose
answer is assumed to be in the room already.

Through the Hindi B door, Chekhov appears exactly once, and not as a chapter. On the last page
of that curriculum, under the note saying questions will not be asked from the following
lessons, in the block for the prescribed reader *Sparsh Part 2*, between Bihari's couplets and
a Mahadevi Varma poem, stands Anton Chekhov's name in Devanagari beside a story called
"Girgit" — and in brackets after it, the whole lesson.

The corpus holds no row for it, and whether the current *Sparsh* reprint still prints the story
these documents do not settle. What they settle is the naming. In one stream Chekhov is an
examinable text with a warm-up table about weddings; in the stream across the corridor, his
name is printed for the sole purpose of being fenced off the paper. Placement is all a syllabus
shows. It gives no reasons, and none is inferred here.

## Underneath the syllabus

There is a layer below the rows, and it is where some of the world actually lives. In the
exercises after "A Tiger in the Zoo" a second poem is printed whole — Rilke's "The Panther," in
Stephen Mitchell's English — set beside Leslie Norris's tiger. It is not a chapter and not a
listed set-text row; whether an exercise insert can reach the paper is not a question these
documents answer.

The layer runs the other way too. Inside the Chekhov unit, the writing exercise hands the class
a passage from Tagore's *The Home and the World* to turn into a script — Bimala demanding that
foreign goods be cleared out of the market, her husband refusing: "To tyrannise for the country
is to tyrannise over the country." A swadeshi argument, in the middle of the one
foreign-language play in the book. The anthology put them on facing pages; it does not say why,
and neither will I.

The shelf closes on paperwork. *Footprints Without Feet* still tells its teachers, in a
preface, that it represents writers from across the world — Indian, British, French, American
and Japanese. Its nine rows here are Indian, British, French and American. And the English
reader's last printed page, after Chekhov and after Shakespeare's unnamed biography, is the
Fundamental Rights of the Constitution of India, including the right of minorities to conserve
their language, script and culture.

## The lesson that won't be asked

If you ever watched a teacher go down the contents page with a pen — not this one, not this
one — you saw the only editorial act in a school year a child witnesses directly: the shelf
shrinking in front of them, for reasons nobody explains, shortly before the syllabus becomes
real.

India's documents perform that act in writing, at the bottom of the page, in a box, and those
chapters keep a strange afterlife: still printed, still in the bag, weightless. In the syllabus
that governed Class 10 in 2024-25, one such name in the Hindi B box is Anton Chekhov's — and
one corridor away, in English, the same writer arrives with a warm-up table about weddings.

What this chapter can't know: this is one board, one grade and one year — every row is Class 10
under the Central Board of Secondary Education in 2024-25 — and while the syllabus claims come
from those 2024-25 documents, everything described from inside a reader was read in a later
reprint of the same revised edition, so the pages a 2024-25 pupil held are not verified here.
The Hindi side is under-receipted: *Kritika Part 2* contributes no rows at all and Hindi Course
B was consulted only for its prescribed-books line and its exclusion note, which makes the
Chekhov finding one about two documents rather than two bookshelves. Why that story is off the
Hindi B paper, why the English reader's poetry carries no Indian poet, and why a swadeshi
passage sits inside the Russian unit are questions no document here answers, and none is
guessed at.

# Bolivia: A Cast, Not a Reading List

The last thing Bolivia's literature unit asks a fourth-year secondary pupil to do is not read
anything. It is to put on a play about a period. Inspired by the study of literary currents,
the book says, let us stage a short dramatization about one of them — and in the
dramatization, present an author and their literary production.

So somebody has to be the author. Four pages earlier the book has printed, for the eighteenth
century, five names one to a line, each with a country stamped after it like a passport. Jean
de la Fontaine, France, fables. Félix María Samaniego, Spain, fables. Daniel Defoe, Spain,
*Robinson Crusoe*. Jonathan Swift, Ireland, *Gulliver's Travels*. And Kant, Germany, the
*Critique of Pure Reason* — his name left stranded at the end of Swift's line by the book's own
typesetting.

Two of those lines are worth stopping on. Daniel Defoe is Spanish here. And Kant is standing
in a line of literary works, holding the *Critique of Pure Reason*.

Read those five lines next to that staging task — the pairing is this essay's, not something
the document states — and Bolivia's window stops looking like a shelf of books to be read and
starts looking like a company of foreigners available to be played.

## The door is an object

Most doors in this corpus are lists: a codifier of what an examiner may ask, a specification of
set texts, a ministry program naming units. Bolivia's door is a book. One physical volume, the
learning text for the fourth year of secondary education, marked *official text 2024* above the
Minister of Education's name, and at the foot of the same page, in capitals, a line prohibiting
the sale of this document. What that imprint establishes is the rule about sale — not how
copies reach anyone, not that any classroom opened them.

It is also everything at once. The same covers carry the endocrine glands and the War of the
Pacific. The world-literature material this corpus codes is a short stretch of it: five pages
of tables and a few pages of extracts.

How hard does this door close? The honest answer is that the corpus cannot say. Every one of
the twenty-nine rows coded from the volume is anthology-only; not one is tied to an exam, not
one carries a memorization mandate. The book disclaims itself in its own foreword, calling its
contents an element that energizes learning, one that can always be widened, deepened and
contextualized from the experience and reality of each cultural, social and educational
setting. And it points upward to an instrument this corpus does not hold: its contents, it
says, follow the Plans and Programs established for each educational level. There is a door
above this door, and it was not retrieved — so the force of what follows is not light, it is
unknown. A name printed here is present. It is not thereby required.

## The names, and how they are labelled

The unit sits under a heading that would be hard to invent: the promotion of gender equity in
harmony with Mother Earth in the study of literary currents. Beneath it, six numbered sections
cover the currents and print seven ruled tables between them — the fourth section gives Realism
and Naturalism a table each. The final row of every table is headed *emblematic works and
representative authors*, and twenty of the twenty-five foreign rows in this corpus are names
harvested from those cells. That heading is the instruction for how to read them: emblematic. A
name there is not a book assigned; it is offered as evidence that a period existed.

Read the tables in order and they walk in a direction. Neoclassicism is filed as France,
eighteenth century; Romanticism as Germany, nineteenth; Costumbrismo as Spain; Realism as
France, Spain, England, Mexico; Naturalism as France. All of it arriving from across the
Atlantic. Then the compass swings west — Indigenismo, Latin America, twentieth century — and
the last section says out loud what the swing means. Modernismo, the book explains, stands out
from the rest because it was born for the first time in America and is not an imitation of any
other current; on the contrary, its influence reached Europe from the Americas.

It is the one current the book describes as born here and exported. And it is in those last two
tables, and in none of the five before them, that a Bolivian name stands as an equal instance
of the same thing as a foreign one: Ricardo Jaimes Freyre beside Rubén Darío and José Martí;
Alcides Arguedas and Jesús Lara beside Ciro Alegría of Peru. For five currents the country is
the audience. For two it is a member of the company.

Two entries are labelled in ways the document never notices. Defoe, tagged Spain. And Andrés
Bello, the sole representative author of Costumbrismo — the current about local custom —
introduced not by a novel but as Simón Bolívar's teacher, holding a treatise on the law of
nations. The corpus codes that row as an essay. Whatever a pupil presenting Costumbrismo would
have to present, it is closer to a founding than to a fiction.

One more thing about this company, set against the heading over its head. Of the twenty-five
foreign rows, twenty-four name a man and one names no author at all. The only woman named
anywhere among the twenty-nine rows is Bolivian, and she stands five pages past the last table,
outside the currents entirely — Mónica Paulina Gutiérrez Jiménez, credited for a free
adaptation of an Ayoreo legend printed under the heading *spelling workshop*. The table records
placement. It does not record why.

## Shakespeare comes on loan

If you want to see the mechanism at once, look at what happens when the book stops naming
authors and prints one. A *Hamlet* fragment appears under an instruction that the class read it
with expressiveness — one of the best-known texts in the history of world theatre, the book
says. The heading above it reads, exactly as printed, "(Adapted fragment) Author: Hamlet
William Shakespeare." The source line credits not an edition of the play but a 2019 web article
about what dramatic texts are, on a popular psychology site. Two of the questions underneath
are: have you heard of William Shakespeare, and do you find the situation sad or funny?

Adapted, mis-headed, and sourced from an article about dramatic form rather than from the play.
The world's literature arrives on this page to be recognized and read out, and the corpus
records no edition behind it.

## The errand: a scruple, six pages later

The clearest imported feeling in this table is not on the literature pages at all. It is later
in the unit, under a heading about the importance of radio media in the struggle against social
inequality, where an extract from José Ignacio López Vigil's *Passion for Radio* argues against
treating a microphone as a weapon. For these chiefs, he writes, the microphones
were cannons that fired ideas — a sad confusion of levels, because "on military ground one
wins. But on the ground of ideas one persuades."

He keeps going. Radio in the service of big capital bombards us daily with news that
misinforms; they practise media terrorism. But we cannot and do not want to play that same
game, he writes, even in reverse. For ethical reasons.

Where the tables offer periods, this page offers a scruple: the difference between winning and
persuading, set in front of a fourth-year pupil as a working rule. And the reflection question
printed under the last table asks the same kind of thing of the literature — pick a current and
a present-day problem, and say how we would contribute to solving it through the literature of
that current. Not what the literature is like. What it can be put to.

## The object

Some readers were handed a textbook and some were sold one, and those are two different
childhoods. If you were handed yours — stamped, unsellable, one volume for the whole subject —
you know that such a book is not a shelf but a fact, the way a room is a fact. Bolivia's
official text for the fourth year sets out the literature of the world as a line of names with
countries after them: a Frenchman with fables, an Irishman with a giant's country, a
German philosopher holding the *Critique of Pure Reason* as though it were a novel, an
Englishman wearing Spain's flag by a printer's slip. Four pages on, the unit asks for a
dramatization presenting an author and their literary production.

The document never says those names are the cast for that play. It prints them, and it prints
the task, and the pages invite the comparison.

What this chapter can't know: this is one volume, one year, one grade — the 2024 learning text
for the fourth year of secondary — and everything in it is an anthology rather than a mandate,
deferring upward to Plans and Programs this corpus does not hold, so the door's force is
undetermined rather than demonstrably light. That a book prohibits its own sale says nothing
about how copies reach pupils or whether any teacher ever taught these pages, and nothing here
describes a Bolivian classroom. The cast-list reading is this essay's: the document prints the
emblematic-works cells, and four pages later sets a dramatization task, and never joins the
two.

# Brazil: Nine Books and a Door You Have to Want

The guide that names Brazil's nine obligatory books greets its reader twice, once in each
gender: the same Portuguese noun printed a second time with its ending changed, feminine
and then masculine. The guide declines to address only half its readers. And the noun is
not *pupil*. A *vestibulando* is somebody standing outside a university, trying to get in.
You have in your hands, the greeting says, your Journey Guide.

Deep in the same book, nine titles are printed under Leituras Obrigatórias, Obligatory
Readings — what the journey costs in reading. Gonzaga's *Marília de Dirceu*. Machado's
*Quincas Borba*. Graciliano Ramos's *Angústia*. Drummond's *Alguma Poesia*. Pessoa's
*Mensagem*. Honwana's *Nós matamos o cão tinhoso!* Guimarães Rosa's *Campo Geral*. Cecília
Meireles's *Romanceiro da Inconfidência*. Milton Hatoum's *Dois irmãos*.

No national mandate is in evidence; nothing here obliges a Brazilian to read them. It
obliges a route: whoever sits the FUVEST vestibular for a place at the University of São
Paulo reads the nine.

## Whose door this is

Most doors in this book are the state's — a codifier, a national program, a state
anthology, reaching into a compulsory classroom to tell a fifteen-year-old what will be on
the paper. This one belongs to a university. FUVEST runs the entrance exam, the
*vestibular*, for USP, and the guide states its legal footing plainly: exam and guides
alike rest on a 2023 resolution of the university's own council. One state, one
institution, one year — and not even all of USP's doors, since the same guide points to two
other ways in, and the nine belong to neither.

That does not shrink the list's force; it relocates it. The candidate is not a captive
audience but a volunteer; the reading is not homework but a toll. The exam runs in two
rounds: a day of multiple-choice questions, then, for whoever clears the bar, a second
phase opening with discursive questions in Portuguese — on text interpretation, grammar and
literature — and an essay.

Two lists sit in the same chapter, and they do not weigh the same. The syllabus offers a
repertoire and hedges it in one phrase: these readings are included "among others."
Forty-eight rows of this window come from there — exemplars, not a menu and not a fence.
The other list is the nine, and its sentence carries no hedge at all: the works of obligatory
reading for the 2024 competition *will be*. Will be, for this exam, this year.

## What the window holds

Fifty-seven rows. Thirty-seven are Brazilian; twenty come from outside Brazil. All twenty
sit under a Portuguese-language heading — seventeen under Portuguese literature, three under
African literatures in Portuguese.

No other heading exists in this window: no French shelf, no English, no Greek, no Russian.
The guide names no translator, no edition and no language for any single work; the two lists
give a title and an author and stop. The heading overhead is the whole customs declaration.

The window opens onto the language rather than the world, and the programme summary says so
in four headings: Portuguese Language; Brazilian Literature; Portuguese Literature; African
Literatures in the Portuguese Language. That fourth shelf is defined by what it is written
in, not by where it is from.

The sentence that spells out the literary expectation names only two of the four. What is
expected, where literary texts are concerned, is knowledge of the representative works of
the different *periods* of Brazilian and Portuguese literature. The African heading is not
in that sentence, and the shape of the lists underneath repeats the omission. Brazil gets a
lettered run of periods, Baroque through contemporary. Portugal gets a longer one, from the
medieval troubadours to Modernism. Africa gets no periods at all — two lines, Pepetela's
*Mayombe* and José Luandino Vieira's *Luuanda*, two Angolan names and their titles with no
century around them. Two of these shelves are given a history. The third is given authors.

## Two lines of the nine

The obligatory list is where an African title becomes compulsory, and it is worth reading
lines five and six in the order the guide prints them: Fernando Pessoa's *Mensagem*, and
then Luís Bernardo Honwana's *Nós matamos o cão tinhoso!*

*Mensagem* — "Message" — is a short cycle in which the sea, the kings and the waiting for a
lost king become a national myth in miniature: empire told as destiny, which is my reading
of the poems and not the guide's. *Nós matamos o cão tinhoso!* — "We killed the mangy dog!"
— is a book of stories out of Mozambique, about colonialism and racial oppression, by an
author born in 1942 in Lourenço Marques, in a Mozambique that was then Portuguese.

One line apart, to my eye: the empire's poem about its own greatness, and a book of stories
from underneath it. A former colony's entrance exam requires both, in the same language, in
the same year, from the same candidate. The order is the guide's; the resonance is mine.

Why they sit one line apart is not something this door will say. The guide is not silent
about reading: it wants a candidate able to read, understand and critically interpret texts
of every kind, literary and non-literary, and it lists the operations expected — analysis,
interpretation, genre, composition, and the capacity to relate a text to the whole work it
sits in, to other texts, and to its historical and cultural context. What it never does is
come down to the level of a title. No line under any of the nine says why that book and not
another: no gloss, no aim, nothing like the paragraph of purpose a ministry program prints
above every unit. An arrangement is an editorial act, and this one comes with no reason
attached.

Placement does other quiet work here. Padre Antônio Vieira preached most of his life in
Brazil and died in Salvador; the guide files him under Portuguese Literature, Baroque, and
so his row is foreign. Tomás Antônio Gonzaga was born in Porto; the guide files him under
Brazilian Literature, Arcadismo, and so *Marília de Dirceu* is domestic. Two men crossed the
same ocean in the same direction and the document sorts them opposite ways. A row says
foreign because the guide printed it under a foreign heading — never because anyone
adjudicated a life.

The world beyond Portuguese enters on the domestic shelf, as subject matter. Milton Hatoum's
*Dois irmãos*, one of the nine, is a book of mid-twentieth-century Manaus and its Lebanese
immigration; Alcântara Machado's *Brás, Bexiga e Barra Funda*, on the syllabus, is a book of
Italian São Paulo. Lebanese Manaus, Italian São Paulo: arrived, and then Brazilian.

The nine also do something the syllabus does not: three of their authors — Cecília Meireles,
Milton Hatoum, Luís Bernardo Honwana — appear nowhere in its period lists. The repertoire is
lettered by movement; the obligatory list is dated, in its own sentence, to one exam.

## Reading to get in

Not one of the fifty-seven rows asks for memory: nowhere is a candidate told to hold a named
text by heart. That fits the temper of what the guide says it tests, which is knowledge not
reducible to the memorization of facts, dates and formulas, or to the automated use of
techniques. That statement is about knowledge in general, not about poems; the silence about
reciting is the document's own. The books are to be read, and then perhaps argued about in
longhand, on a paper of discursive questions — and marked blind.

If you ever read a book because it stood between you and a place you wanted, you know this
shelf better than any curriculum can teach it. Not the assigned book — the *other* kind. The
one you bought yourself, second-hand, because a list said it would be on the paper; the one
you read at the wrong speed, in the wrong months, with a pen, hunting for what they would
ask. That book is never quite yours and never quite theirs.

Nine books in São Paulo that year; two of them sat one line apart.

What this chapter can't know: this is one entrance exam for one university in one state in
one year, not Brazil's curriculum, and not even the whole of USP, which can also be entered
by two other routes. The two lists are not commensurable — the forty-eight are exemplars
inside a repertoire the guide leaves open, the nine are closed and stamped to a single exam,
and fifty-seven counts printed rows rather than a reading load. And every errand read above
comes from placement, adjacency and what the works are about, not from any purpose the guide
states, because the guide states none.

# Australia: The Word Between Two Books

On a beach outside Algiers, a Frenchman shoots a man the novel never names. The book
calls him the Arab. He has a sister, a knife, and no line of dialogue; the rest of the
novel belongs to the shooter, tried less for the killing than for failing to weep at his
mother's funeral.

Year 12 students in New South Wales meet that beach — but only the ones whose class
picked the third pair on a state list, where two titles are stacked one above the other
with a single word printed between them: Camus's *The Stranger*, **AND** Kamel Daoud's
*The Meursault Investigation*. The second book gives
the dead man a name — Musa — and a brother who will not stop talking about him. Camus
died in 1960; the man who answered him was born ten years later. In a table of 140
prescribed texts, that is the one pairing I read as a book and its rebuttal both holding
a row — and a class that picks it studies the two together, in one module, because a
state exam authority set the word AND between them.

## The door

The document is the HSC English prescriptions for 2019 to 2026, issued by the NSW
Education Standards Authority and updated in May 2024. It is a state document, and that
is the first thing to say plainly: in Australia the enforceable named-text list belongs
to the state, not the federation. The HSC is a New South Wales credential; Victoria's
authority publishes its own annual text list for its own exam. What follows is New South
Wales standing in for a federation — the same finding-shape as Germany's Länder, where
the absence of one national list is not a gap in the evidence but the evidence.

Inside the state, the choosing is pushed down again. The foreword asks that teachers
selecting specific texts consider the ethos of the school and its local community. A row
in this table is not something a pupil will meet; it is something a pupil's teacher may
offer.

And there is an age line above this door worth drawing before anything is counted. At
fifteen — the age this book measures every other country at — Australia names no book.
The national curriculum for those years asks for categories: wide-ranging Australian and
world authors, First Nations texts, different cultures and times. The state syllabus adds
quantities — so many novels, so many films — and exactly one proper noun: at least one
Shakespeare play, title unnamed. The entire named canon of an Australian fifteen-year-old
is one surname. The 140-title menu opens two years later, in elective senior courses, for
the minority who have chosen literature as a path. What follows is not a generation's
canon but the specialists' shelf — and it happens to be the widest window in this book,
which is its own finding: the country that names nothing for everyone offers nearly
everywhere to those who ask.

The mechanics matter, because everything else depends on them. An
Advanced student closely studies four prescribed texts, one drawn from each of four
categories — Shakespearean drama, prose fiction, poetry or drama, and a fourth that may
be nonfiction, film or media. At least two more short texts come in for the writing
module, and those do not contribute to the required pattern. So the floor of the year is
four plus two: at least six rows out of 140. A Standard floor is five. The 140 is the
length of the menu, never of anyone's reading year, and a menu count and a mandate count
are not on the same axis.

The menu-door works like this. Every Standard, Advanced and English Studies pupil does
one common module, whose list holds fourteen titles, of which they study one: Orwell's
*Nineteen Eighty-Four*, Miller's *The Crucible*, Doerr's *All the Light We Cannot See*,
Shakespeare's *The Merchant of Venice*, Slessor's poems, Winton's essays, a
reality-television series about asylum seekers. Fourteen doors into one room; each pupil
walks through one; the other thirteen are somebody else's year. The Advanced comparison
module offers seven pairs and takes one. Extension 1 spreads thirty rows across five
electives with titles like "Literary homelands" and "Worlds of upheaval."

## What is on the shelf, and in what language

The subject is called English, and that name does most of the border work before any
title is chosen. Of the 140 rows, 55 are Australian; the other 85 arrive from elsewhere,
and 77 of those 85 were written in English to begin with. Adiga, Ishiguro, Lahiri, Levy,
Tóibín, Mistry, Zadie Smith, Colum McCann, Langston Hughes: not one needed a translator
to reach a Sydney desk.

Eight rows in the whole table come from another language. Kafka and Fritz Lang from
German, Camus and Daoud from French, Guevara and Guillermo del Toro from Spanish,
Calvino from Italian, Ibsen from Norwegian. Two of the eight are films. Six are print,
and two of those six are the Algiers pair — joined by that AND, in a module where
students choose a pair, so that within this menu a class selecting either French novel
studies both. The second, on my reading, is a prosecution of the first.

Against that, the door carries two written selection requirements, identical across the
Standard, Advanced and EAL/D lists. Across the senior years, the
selection of texts must give students experience of texts widely regarded as quality
literature, including a range of literary texts written about intercultural experiences
and the peoples and cultures of Asia; and of a range of Australian texts, including texts
by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander authors and texts giving insight into diverse
Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander experience. Neither clause carries a number.

Both leave marks on the table, and the marks are different shapes. Thirteen rows are
coded to a subject naming an Asian country, city or people: Adiga's Bangalore, Forster's
Raj, Ishiguro's postwar Japan, Mistry's Bombay, Thien's Cultural Revolution, Baillie's
Tiananmen, Gavron's British-Bangladeshi London, Lahiri's Indian-American crossing,
Yousafzai's Pakistan, Noonuccal's China poems, and the arrivals written by Nam Le, Alice
Pung and Lily Chan — five of the thirteen Australian books about Asian pasts and
Asian-Australian arrivals. Twelve
rows are coded to Indigenous Australia, and every one is Australian: Noonuccal, Ali Cobby
Eckermann, Tara June Winch, Melissa Lucashenko,
Rachel Perkins, Jane Harrison, Linda Burney, Noel Pearson among them. Asia sits on this
shelf as subject matter, written from both sides of the border; Indigenous Australia sits
on it as home. Nothing explains why the shapes differ; the table records placement, not
reasons.

One row bends the pattern hard enough to be worth stopping on. Oodgeroo Noonuccal appears
twice — the only author with two rows in that Indigenous count — her second entry a set of
seven poems: "The Past," then "China…Woman," "Reed Flute Cave," "Entombed Warriors,"
"Visit to Sun Yat-Sen Memorial Hall," "Sunrise on Huampu River," "A Lake Within a Lake."
Six of the seven come out of her 1984 visit to China. What those titles afford a pupil is
an Aboriginal poet set for study as a traveller — looking outward from the country, not
being looked at. The window opens the other way.

## The errand: what the pair affords

The comparison module has its own teaching language, and it is unusually candid about
what the comparing is meant to produce. Students explore how comparing texts can reveal
resonances and dissonances between them and within them, and consider the ways a
reimagining of one text might "mirror, align or collide" with the details of another.

*Mirror, align or collide.* Six of the seven pairs I read as chiefly mirroring: Al Pacino
documenting a staging of *Richard III*, Jane Campion filming Keats's last love, Stephen
Daldry filming Woolf writing *Mrs Dalloway*, Margaret Atwood rewriting *The Tempest*
inside a prison theatre — the canon in conversation with its own admirers. That grouping
is my reading; the document sorts no pair into a bucket.

The Algiers pair I read as a collision. Daoud's narrator is Musa's brother, older now,
drinking in Oran, addressing a stranger about a famous book in which his brother is a
corpse without a name. The novel he is angry at is the other novel on the same page of
the same list, in the same module, examined together.

What the pairing affords is not French existentialism, which Camus alone would supply. It
is the experience of reading a masterpiece and then reading the person it stepped over —
a canon being audited by the place it was set in. And the pupil who does that sits in a
state whose own prescribed shelf includes *The Secret River*, a stage adaptation about
dispossession on the Hawkesbury. Both books can go on one desk. That is what the list
allows; it is not what the list is *for*. The list proves the pairing and the module's
language proves the framing; no statement of intention for either appears anywhere in the
document.

## The book nobody prescribed

There is a second English course in this state, for students learning English as an
additional language or dialect, with its own shelf of 22 rows. Its module on language,
identity and culture centres explicit English study on the Australian vernacular, its
idioms and its colloquialisms. On that same page sit two rows worth reading side by side:
an anthology called *Contemporary
Asian Australian Poets*, and Langston Hughes — seven poems, including "Theme for English
B," in which a young Black student, the only one of his kind in the class, is told to go
home and write a page that will be true. A module built on the Australian vernacular
offers a pupil a poem about being asked, in an English class, to produce something honest
on demand.

Which is close to what the state asks of all of them. Every Standard, Advanced, English
Studies and EAL/D pupil must also study one related text of their own — in the common
module for the first three, in the first module for the last. The document names no list
for it, no category, no title.

So the true end of this 140-row table is not a row. In a document that specifies
publishers, editions and ISBNs, and that pauses to record how many pages one Wordsworth
edition runs to, the one textual slot required in each of these four courses is the one
the document declines to name.

You had a last year of school once, with a list someone had chosen before you walked in.
If your classroom ever let you bring a text of your own in beside the prescribed one —
what did you bring, and did anyone ask you why? A man on a beach outside Algiers got a
name because a novelist in Oran wrote it down; a class in Sydney may read both books at
one desk, because a state authority printed the word AND between them.

What this chapter can't know: this is one state's list for one year of schooling — New
South Wales, not Australia, with Victoria and Queensland setting their own texts
elsewhere — so read every sentence above about Australia as the New South Wales door.
Nothing on this shelf is a book a pupil must read; what is mandatory is a pattern of
categories filled from menus, at least six rows of the 140 for an Advanced candidate, and
every sentence here about a class reading the Algiers pair describes something a class
*may* do. The groupings — Asia, Indigenous Australia, mirroring against collision — are
readings offered for inspection rather than categories the authority assigns.

# The United States: The Book You Bring Yourself

Picture a sitting of the kind the College Board's own sample materials describe — a
sample, not a room anyone observed. Three essays, in a fixed order: a poem, a passage of
prose, and then a third question that stops naming books.

The first two hand the student their texts. The third hands over a concept — a character
given a gift that is also a burden — and then does what no ministry in this book does: it
tells the student to choose a work of fiction, either from their own reading or from the
list printed below. That list is a column of titles offered the way a waiter offers bread;
the student may take one or ignore them all. Then, with a recommended forty minutes to
spend on it, they write in the title of the book they intend to be examined on.

That blank is the American window, and it is cut one reader at a time.

Nobody fills it in from above — but not because nobody chooses. There is no federal
ministry with power to name a book, no national exam codifier, no enforceable list of the
kind that anchors every other chapter here; what stands in their place is selection pushed
down and dispersed. The exam question comes from a course and exam description published by
the College Board, a private organization, which tells schools that the works themselves are
selected locally. This chapter's table comes from somewhere else entirely: an appendix to
academic standards that states adopted one at a time, whose own work group made the final
choice of what it printed. Between them the two documents hold the two things a codifier
holds — force, and titles — and neither holds both. The exam has force over a score and
names no required book. The appendix names sixty-three titles for the ninth- and
tenth-grade band and denies, in its own front matter, being a list at all.

## The door that says it is not a door

The appendix is called *Text Exemplars and Sample Performance Tasks*, and its opening page
explains what an exemplar is for: the samples are there mainly to show the level of
complexity and quality that the standards require students in a grade band to engage with.
Then the writers close the door they appeared to be opening. "They expressly do not
represent a partial or complete reading list."

Take that at face value, because the essay depends on it. Every row in this table — all
sixty-three, the whole ninth-and-tenth-grade band as printed, stories through technical
subjects — is a choice-list row, and none carries a memorization requirement. Not one of
these titles is a book an American fifteen-year-old must read. They are specimens of a
difficulty level, chosen to show teachers what that level feels like in the hand. The
criterion of admission the document states is complexity, quality and range. The criterion
it does not state is nationality.

The other document draws its one boundary the same way. By the end of the course, the AP
description says, students should have studied a variety of texts by diverse authors across
a range of periods, from the English Renaissance to the present. A real boundary — and a
boundary of period. Both American doors specify a shape and leave the passport unchecked.

So the interesting question is not what this country admits. It is what the shelf looks
like when the choosing is dispersed to a work group, districts, schools and teachers.

## What walks in

Forty-one of the sixty-three rows are domestic. Twenty-two are not, and the first thing to
say about those twenty-two is that eleven of them never met a translator. Shakespeare twice,
Donne, Shelley, Housman; Achebe's *Things Fall Apart*, Fugard's *"Master Harold"…and the
boys*, Zusak's *The Book Thief*; three works of exposition, on art, music and mathematics.
They cross a border that is not a language border. Seven of the eleven are British, and an
eighth, Keith Devlin, British-American — an inheritance shelf more than an import shelf, the
family books that came over with the language.

Eleven rows arrive in translation, and they are old, European and few: three from Greek
(Homer, Sophocles, Euclid), three from French (Voltaire, Ionesco, Wiesel), two from Russian
(Gogol's "The Nose," 1836; Turgenev's *Fathers and Sons*, 1862), one each from Latin, German
and Norwegian. The appendix names the glass as well as the view — Fagles for Homer, Ronald
Wilks for Gogol, Constance Garnett for Turgenev, Derek Prouse for Ionesco — which is more
than a citation habit, since the complexity these rows exemplify is the complexity of an
English page a named translator made.

What the translated eleven do not include is worth stating carefully, and only against this
table: no row from Asia, from Latin America, from the Arabic-speaking world; Africa arrives
twice, in English. The Russian rows are the tsarist nineteenth century — a barber finding a
nose in his breakfast roll, a young nihilist arguing with his father — and the Soviet century
is not on this shelf. The shape of a 2010 work group's result can be reported without a
reason being assigned to it.

Group the twenty-two by the era each work is set in and the pattern is real but not clean.
Five foreign rows carry a state consuming people, none of it American: Nazi Germany and the
Holocaust (Zusak; Wiesel's Nobel lecture), apartheid South Africa (Fugard), the neighbours
turning into rhinoceroses under 1930s fascism (Ionesco), the district commissioner arriving
at the end of Achebe's late-nineteenth-century Igbo village. Other foreign rows carry other
injuries — Voltaire's Lisbon earthquake, Ibsen's marriage, Turgenev's generational quarrel in
reform-era Russia, *Macbeth*'s regicide — so the grouping supports a lean, not a law. The
domestic rows hold American injuries, and a great many of them: Jim Crow, the Depression,
Gettysburg, Wounded Knee, the Red Scare. That placement is what the table shows; the work
group recorded no reasons.

## What a foreign book is for here

Every other document in this book eventually says something to a teacher about a foreign
work: study this alongside that, note the ties to our own culture, learn these lines by
heart. The teaching language this appendix attaches to a named work is its sample
performance tasks — illustrations, its front matter says, of how the standards apply to
exemplars already chosen — and each one closes with a standards code in square brackets.

Odysseus, the man of twists and turns, appears in a task that asks students to analyze how
he reflects conflicting motivations through his dealings with other characters, and how
those conflicting loyalties develop the poem's themes. Then the code: RL.9–10.3. What the
task asks of the *Odyssey* is that RL.9–10.3 be practised on it. Two tasks later *Macbeth*
appears as raw material — students analyze how the Japanese filmmaker Akira Kurosawa, in
*Throne of Blood*, draws on and transforms the play to develop a similar plot set in feudal
Japan. In these tasks, and they are a use of the books rather than the reason the books were
chosen, an admitted foreigner's errand is to be an instance of an operation. Not a feeling a
country cannot source domestically; a skill it can be shown performing.

And yet the appendix does something no standard requires. It prints excerpts, and somebody
chose them; the front matter concedes the awkwardness, granting that where excerpts appear
they serve only as stand-ins for the full text.

The stand-in chosen for Fugard is not the scene the play is famous for. *"Master
Harold"…and the boys* ends with a white schoolboy spitting in the face of the Black man who
raised him. What the appendix prints instead is Sam, earlier, trying to explain ballroom
dancing to the boy: none of us knows the steps, there is no music playing, and it does not
stop with us. "America has bumped into Russia, England is bumping into India, rich man
bumps into poor man." Those are big collisions, he says. They make for a lot of bruises.

A South African play about a South African injury, excerpted at the exact half-page where it
turns and names the reader's own country first in a list of collisions. The choice is an
editorial act and a visible one. Its reason is not in the document, and I will not supply
one.

There is one more constraint on this shelf, and it is not a state's. The same front matter
explains that where the compilers could not rely on fair use and could not secure
permission, they listed the title without an excerpt. That establishes exactly this much:
copyright and permission limited which excerpts could be reproduced, so some titles stand on
the page without their text. Which titles, and why each permission failed, the document does
not say.

## The list nobody collects

If your classroom's reading list arrived as a photocopy — stapled, signed by a teacher who
could have chosen otherwise — you already know the argument over it happened somewhere above
you and out of sight: a board meeting, a department vote, a publisher's catalogue. That is
the American arrangement: selection everywhere, and no single national address for it.

Which leaves the exam room. Every other window in this book can be photographed: a codifier
you can print, a program with page numbers, a spine a pupil carries in a bag. The American
window has to be reconstructed one answer at a time — a title chosen by a student with a
recommended forty minutes to spend on it, written into one essay, graded — and nothing here
ever collects those titles into a list.

What this chapter cannot know: the sixty-three titles above come from a single 2010 appendix
that says outright it is not a reading list, so nothing here describes a syllabus an American
pupil actually sat through — what gets read is set by districts, schools and teachers, and
none of that is in view. The split between domestic and foreign is this book's coding rather
than the document's, since the appendix tags no author with a nationality, and eleven of the
twenty-two foreign rows were written in English and never translated at all. And the
appendix states its criteria while explaining no individual choice — which work was proposed,
which excerpt cut, whose permission refused — so placement is visible here and motive is not.

# England: The Door Opens Inward

There is a pot in Manchester. It was made in Nigeria, a museum in that city holds it, and a poet
from that city wrote a whole poem talking to it — not about it, to it. The poem was commissioned
for the museum and written as a conversation with a Nigerian pot in the collection.

Since 2023 that poem has been available to English classrooms as a set text, and in summer 2025
the first cohort sat an exam that could ask them about it — closed book, nothing on the desk but
what the paper supplies. The pot is the only thing in this story that crossed a border.

All seventy-three rows in this table are domestic. Not one arrived in translation. Measured
through this document, the window England opens on world literature is shut — and yet the room is
full of elsewhere.

## The door

The document is the AQA GCSE English Literature specification, and AQA is an awarding body, not a
ministry. The assessment objectives are set by Ofqual and are identical across every English
Literature specification and every exam board; what each board writes for itself is the menu of
books. So nothing below is England's shelf. It is one shelf in England, in one year. England is
my word, not the file's — the corpus is coded for the United Kingdom, and nothing in it covers
Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland.

And it is a menu, not a mandate. A student takes one play from a list of six, one nineteenth-
century novel from a list of seven, one modern text from a choice of twelve, and one poetry
cluster, whose fifteen poems must all be studied because the exam may ask about any of them. Six,
seven, twelve, forty-five: the 2025 menu runs to seventy items. The table carries seventy-three,
because it also keeps the three modern texts whose last exam was 2024 — a list caught mid-change.
Seventy rows are the space of the possible; the set-text list a real teenager carries is eighteen
of them, fifteen of those poems. Eighteen rows, not eighteen works: one of them, *Telling Tales*,
is an anthology of seven stories.

Two more sentences shape the door. All assessments are closed book, with any stimulus material
supplied by the paper itself. And the aim the specification writes for itself is that students
should come to appreciate the depth and power of the English literary heritage. Hold that aim
beside a table with no translated row and the shelf's Englishness looks less like an omission
than like the subject's own definition — my framing, not a reason the board gives. What the
document proves is the measurement.

## What comes through it anyway

Sixteen of the seventy-three rows name a specific place outside Britain and Ireland. More rows
carry foreign material; those sixteen are where the table itself says so. They arrive in three
unlike ways.

**Elsewhere as stage.** Of the six Shakespeare plays a school can set, not one is set in England.
The settings are Verona, Venice, Messina, Rome, eleventh-century Scotland — and, for *The
Tempest*, a colonial Renaissance with a question mark and no country at all. Add Browning's
Ferrara, and a pattern appears: England's most domestic authors do their thinking abroad. The
passport is the author's, never the setting's.

**Wars with a foreign address.** Tennyson takes the Crimea in 1854; Owen the Western Front;
Orwell the Russian revolution; Armitage the 2003 invasion of Iraq; Duffy a photographer's
cold-war conflict zones; Rumens an exile from a homeland the table leaves unspecified. Britain
was a belligerent in the Crimean War, on the Western Front and in Iraq; the allegory, the
photographs and the exile sit elsewhere, or nowhere the table will name. The sharpest is Beatrice
Garland's *Kamikaze*, the only Pacific-war row here and the interior life of a Japanese pilot's
family. Imtiaz Dharker's *A Century Later* does the same office for the 2012 Taliban attack on a
schoolgirl. Both are coded domestic, because both are English poems.

**The world as citizens.** The 2023–2025 exchange of texts grew this pile. The Worlds and Lives
cluster, first taught in 2023 and first examined in 2025, puts fifteen poems in front of a class,
and several of the new ones reach migration, heritage or elsewhere through people living in
Britain and their families. A conversation on an afternoon train from Purley to Victoria in 1955.
Leeds in 1869, a great-great-grandfather with eyes dried out by twelve hours of combing flax,
imagined by a descendant born and raised in the same city. Guyana revisited and found full of car
tyres, plastic bottles and styrofoam cups. Trinidad carried in a pocket.

The modern-text list moved the same way and left a datable seam: three rows whose last exam was
2024 — Bennett, Stephens, Ishiguro — and three first taught in 2023 and first examined in 2025:
Odimba's *Princess & The Hustler*, on the 1963 Bristol bus boycott; de Waal's *My Name is Leon*;
Pinnock's *Leave Taking*. The board's taster booklet for the Pinnock prints the scene it wants
taught, a daughter turning on her mother: "You're too busy bowing and scraping to your beloved
England. And where's it got you?"

England's classroom did not import that line. It grew it.

## The errand: one pot, one collection

The teacher guide published alongside the cluster walks through the pot poem, and says plainly
what it is for: the pot is used to explore cultural repatriation, colonialism and migration. The
speaker knows only half the story of how it reached England. The guide works through the
euphemisms a museum label might use and is blunt about where they land — the white boat sailing
to England, it says, reminds us of the truth of cultural theft. Then the poem turns and speaks to
the pot about itself, tells it that it is not really one of us, and asks it, twice, to answer.
Growl if you hear me. Pot? Pot?

So here is what this window imports. Not the pot: the guide places it in the museum's collection,
and what reaches a classroom is a poem about it — a poem that same guide reads as staged at the
display glass. Not a foreign literature either: a set of questions about a foreign object and
whether it should be here, asked in English by a poet the guide prints in lower case, shamshad
khan, described there as a second-generation British Asian from Manchester, writing for a museum
in her own city.

That is the mechanism in miniature. The world is admitted constantly, and admitted as content; it
clears customs in the hands of a writer who already belongs. And printed at the start of every
AQA mark scheme is a statement of what the subject is for: high-quality English literature
displays recognisable literary qualities and, though shaped by particular contexts, transcends
them and speaks to the universality of the human condition. AQA nowhere offers that as its reason
for setting no translated text. But hold the sentence against the shelf — my reading, not the
board's argument — and the two fit. If literature transcends its context, one board's menu can
stay entirely English-language without its compilers conceding a gap. You can reach Guyana, the
Pacific and a Nigerian pot without setting a translated page.

## Where the door does open

One part of this course has no set texts at all. Unseen poetry is a whole section of the second
paper and cannot appear in a table row, because nothing is prescribed: two poems, unseen, sat
cold. The specification guarantees the two poems and says nothing about where they come from. The
board's teaching guide for that section offers a starter collection — one it calls
non-prescriptive, telling teachers they may substitute poems of their own — and alongside Blake,
Hardy, Rossetti and Owen it prints Langston Hughes's 'Mother to Son' and Kyle Dargan's 'Poem
Resisting Arrest'. The cluster guide does the same at the foot of every entry, under other poems
for comparison: Czeslaw Milosz beside Raman Mundair, Rabindranath Tagore beside Liz Berry, Maya
Angelou beside Caleb Femi.

None of those names is a row in this table, and none can be: a suggestion in a guide is not a set
text. So the finding is narrower than an admission. AQA's guides suggest foreign poets without
prescribing them, and the unseen section — assessed, and heavily — leaves the identity of the
examined poems open until the paper is opened. What matters here is prescription, not assessment.

## Fifteen, sixteen, and no book on the desk

You were fifteen or sixteen once, in a room with a list that looked like the only list there was.
It never is. Somebody chose a board, somebody chose one play from six, and the set texts you
would be tested on narrowed to eighteen rows — one play, one novel, one modern text, fifteen
poems — before you walked in. Two more poems, named to nobody, waited on the paper.

If your own classroom's list ran on a subject named after your country, the test is not how many
foreigners were let in but how they travelled: as scenery, as wars placed abroad, and — in the
rows first examined in 2025 — as family history carried by people living in Britain. Not one came
carrying a translator's name.

And in summer 2025 a teenager could be asked about a pot in a Manchester collection that a poet
had spent a whole poem begging to answer: *growl if you hear me.* No anthology and no book was
allowed on the desk; the named poem was printed on the paper, and the poem you set beside it, you
brought in your head. An exam is not a bad place to be asked that question: it is a place where
you have to answer, on behalf of something brought here and never asked.

What this chapter cannot know: this is one awarding body's menu in one year, not England — other
boards set different books under the same Ofqual conditions, and every row here is a choice, so
the piles above describe what a school may set and never what a classroom read. No 2025 question
paper is in view, which makes the opening and closing scenes what the documents allow rather than
a report of anything a pupil was actually asked. And the grouping of sixteen foreign-placed rows
rests on interpretive codes and on a criterion of mine, while the documents state the subject's
aims without ever explaining why one book was set and another was not.
