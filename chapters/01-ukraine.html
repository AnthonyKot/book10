The Post-Soviet Quartet · Chapter 01

Ukraine: The Door Is a Subject

A ninth-grader stands up to say a poem by heart. The poem is «Буковині» — "To Bukovina" — and it was written in Romanian, by Mihai Eminescu. Bukovina is a historical region now divided between Ukraine and Romania; the pupil is saying the poem in Ukrainian translation. And the lesson is not Ukrainian Literature. On the timetable this hour reads Foreign Literature.

A foreign book can enter a classroom by several routes: a unit inside the national literature course, a line in an exam specification. Ukraine built a door. Foreign Literature sits beside Ukrainian Language and Ukrainian Literature with its own hours, its own programs, its own list of poems to be learned by heart. The program says so in its own voice: the subject is studied separately, as an independent and self-sufficient one, with a uniquely unbroken teaching tradition since 1991, under conditions of Independence.

The window is not a pane in a wall. It is a room.

The door, and its hinges

Who decides what stands in the room? Not the top of the stack, as far as the saved documents show: both foreign-literature programs open by naming the instrument they were built under — the State Standard for basic secondary education, a Cabinet of Ministers resolution — and neither reproduces its contents.

Books are named a level down. Each of these documents is a model program, carrying on its title page a line recommending it, from the Ministry of Education and Science of Ukraine, above an order number. Recommended, in its own word — and there is more than one for the same subject and the same grades.

Inside a program, the shelf then splits. The Nikolenko program divides its own contents in two: a mandatory component of works set for compulsory study, and a variable component from which the teacher may pick one or several. And it grants a further step outward — teachers may use this model program as the basis for programs of their own.

So the state mandates that world literature happen and hands part of the which down the stack: to a program, and inside the program, to a teacher. Only part. The obligatory works stay put; unit headings that read one work at the teacher's choice mark where the handing-down happens, and they do not stand over everything.

The thirty-nine translated rows here are a hybrid: the Nikolenko program's Grade 9 shelf entire, joined by the Voloshchuk program's five-poem memorization list — taken in because the first sets no memorization at all. Beside them, eighteen domestic rows from the Ukrainian Literature programs next door. A selection, not an inventory: no pupil meets all thirty-nine, and no program contains this particular thirty-nine.

What comes through

Everything is read in Ukrainian, and the program makes the translation part of the lesson: the works are to come in the best Ukrainian translations, taking account of the achievements of the Ukrainian school of translation. A foreign book enters this room already holding a Ukrainian hand — and ten source languages come through the door behind it: English supplies nineteen of the thirty-nine rows, French six, German four; the rest arrive in ones and twos — Italian, Polish, Russian, Yiddish, Romanian, Norwegian, Japanese.

English holds four of the five latest-born authors — Foer, Zusak, Whitehead, Rowling — and most of those rows sit in an additional-reading appendix rather than a taught unit.

The shelf sorts into three uneven piles.

Europe as a destination. The Grade 9 Enlightenment unit — reason, nature, education — carries Swift's first voyage, Goethe's lyrics, and Schiller's ode to joy. The teaching line under Schiller does not stop at the Enlightenment. It names the Enlightenment idea of the unification of humanity, and then the pathos of a work that became the anthem of the European Union. The program's sentence gives the ode two lives in one breath: an idea out of its own century, and an anthem.

Other people's wounds. Line up the named injuries on each side and they do not mirror. On the translated rows: Jim Crow in Lee, American slavery in Whitehead, the Congo Free State in Conrad, the First World War in Morpurgo, wartime Germany in Zusak, 9/11 in Foer. On the domestic rows next door: totalitarianism in Bahrianyi and Stus, the 1944 deportation of the Crimean Tatars in Umerov, and a unit of poetry from the war that began in 2014. Among those named categories, the current war appears only on the domestic side.

Subjects do cross the seam. The translated shelf also carries totalitarianism, in Bradbury; the late Russian Empire appears on both sides; and Byron's «Мазепа» puts Cossack-era Ukraine on a translated row. Rows record admission, never intention.

The Ukrainian pages. Every unit in the Nikolenko program carries a standing rubric, Ukraine and the World; the Voloshchuk program runs the equivalent as the Ukrainian motif, covering the ties of foreign writers to Ukraine, the mastery of Ukrainian translators, and authors biographically connected with Ukrainian territories. Its reason, in the program's own words: such authors "join Ukraine to the world," carrying their conceptions of it into other cultures.

Read the shelf through that rubric and a second shelf appears inside the first. Byron arrives with «Мазепа», tagged beside the battle of Poltava and the figure of Mazepa in Ukrainian culture. Conrad's row notes that he was born in Berdychiv. Sholem Aleichem's Tevye the Dairyman, the corpus's single Yiddish row, sits under a Ukraine-and-the-World line that opens on Sholem Aleichem and Ukraine.

Two rows are Russian-language. Gogol's offers The Government Inspector or The Overcoat, and the program frames the author by the Petersburg stage of his life and work, with the rubric over the unit reading Gogol and Ukrainian culture. Bulgakov's row is Heart of a Dog, and his entry opens on the Ukrainian pages of his life and work. The corpus notes mark both as retained — Ukrainian origin in Gogol's case, Kyivan birth in Bulgakov's. What the documents themselves do is placement: two Russian-language authors taught inside a subject about the world, under a rubric about Ukraine.

One thing sits outside this table. There is no row for it, but both Ukrainian-literature model programs set Gogol's Sorochyntsi Fair in Grade 9 — so one author walks through both doors of the same school, a national writer down one corridor, a foreign one down the other.

The poem in the body

Back to the ninth-grader on their feet.

Five rows carry a memorization mandate, and every one of them is translated: Heine, Dante, Petrarch, Shakespeare, Eminescu. The domestic rows carry none — the defining Ukrainian-literature program makes memorization recommendatory and lets the learner choose the poem. Whatever a Ukrainian teenager is required to hold in their body, in this corpus, came from somewhere else.

Four of the five are lyric, and four of the five are about a person: a Dante sonnet to Beatrice, a Petrarch sonnet (61 or 132, learner's choice), a Shakespeare sonnet (116 or 130), one Heine lyric of three. The fifth is «Буковині», and it is the only one whose subject is a place.

The program does not print the poem and walk away. Under the title it sets brief information about Eminescu and Bukovina, and then what the hour is for: the image of Bukovina and its nature, the romantic moods and motifs, the inner world of the lyric speaker. Beside it the Ukrainian-motif rubric sends the class out of the poem and into the geography — Eminescu in Chernivtsi, his travels through Ukraine, the monument to him there.

So the text a ninth-grader must hold by heart, in Ukrainian translation, inside a subject about the world, is a Romanian poet's address to a region that lies partly inside their own country's border — and what the teaching line asks them to find in it is an image of Bukovina and its nature, while the rubric beside it asks them to find a statue in Chernivtsi.

What this placement imports is a Ukrainian place, described in a language that is not Ukrainian. The program itself puts it more narrowly: such authors join Ukraine to the world.

The other program names something adjacent. Over the Grade 9 history-and-modernity unit, where the teacher picks two works from five — Sholem Aleichem, Bulgakov, Bradbury, Zusak, Foer — stands a culture rubric: culture as spiritual resistance to violence. That heading belongs to those five books, not to the whole shelf.

Fifteen

You were fifteen in some room with a list — a photocopy with a column of due dates, or the back page of a textbook you never finished. Someone chose it, and the choosing was invisible to you. If your classroom made you learn a poem by heart, run the Ukrainian test on it: was the poem yours?

In this table the five texts a ninth-grader must hold by memory are an Italian's sonnet to Beatrice, another Italian's sonnet to Laura, an Englishman's sonnet, a German's lyric — and a Romanian's poem to Bukovina, learned in Ukrainian, inside a subject the Nikolenko program describes as having a uniquely unbroken teaching tradition since 1991. The clock in the Voloshchuk header is an hour and a half a week, recommended. Small, weekly, and a room of its own.
